Autoencoders and representation learning

Two widgets for today's derivation and its limit. The first trains a linear autoencoder with a one-number code on a cloud of points and watches it find the principal axis, with the floor from the SVD drawn in. The second puts a tiny non-linear autoencoder on points that lie along a curve, which no line can follow, and shows the error going below that floor.

All data on this page is synthetic, generated in your browser from a fixed seed, so Reset always gives back the same picture.

1A linear autoencoder finds the principal axis

Sixty centred points in the plane, and a linear autoencoder with a one-number code: $z = \mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{a}$, then $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = z\,\mathbf{b}$. Every reconstruction lies on the line through the origin in the direction of $\mathbf{b}$ (blue). Each step is one gradient step on the mean squared error over all sixty points, for the four numbers in $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ together. The dashed purple line is the first principal axis from the SVD, and the dashed level in the cost plot is the floor $\sigma_2^2/m$ that the derivation says no linear autoencoder can go below.

The mathematics

With $\mathbf{r}_i = \mathbf{x}_i - (\mathbf{x}_i\cdot\mathbf{a})\,\mathbf{b}$ the residual of point $i$ and $m = 60$,

$$E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lVert\mathbf{r}_i\rVert^2, \qquad \frac{\partial E}{\partial\mathbf{b}} = -\frac{2}{m}\sum_i (\mathbf{x}_i\cdot\mathbf{a})\,\mathbf{r}_i, \qquad \frac{\partial E}{\partial\mathbf{a}} = -\frac{2}{m}\sum_i (\mathbf{r}_i\cdot\mathbf{b})\,\mathbf{x}_i,$$

and one step is $\mathbf{a} \leftarrow \mathbf{a} - \eta\,\partial E/\partial\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b} \leftarrow \mathbf{b} - \eta\,\partial E/\partial\mathbf{b}$. The derivation of the lecture, with $d = 1$: every $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i$ lies on the line spanned by $\mathbf{b}$; the closest point of that line is the orthogonal projection, so $E \ge$ the projection error onto the line; and over all lines through the origin the projection error is smallest on the first principal axis $\mathbf{v}_1$, where it equals $\sigma_2^2/m$. The optimum is reached only when $\mathbf{a}$ makes $z\,\mathbf{b}$ the orthogonal projection, that is $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{b}/\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert^2$; the scale of $\mathbf{b}$ itself is free, which is the one-dimensional case of the change of basis $\mathbf{T}$.

Watch for
  • The blue line swings onto the purple one within the first few dozen steps, and the cost falls onto the dashed floor and stays there. The readout's angle between $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{v}_1$ goes to zero; the cost never goes below the floor.
  • The grey segments are the residuals $\mathbf{r}_i$. At the optimum they are all perpendicular to the blue line: the reconstruction is the orthogonal projection, which is what step 3 of the derivation requires.
  • The angle is under a degree within the first twenty steps, while the cost is still about a percent above the floor. The direction is found first; the last part of the descent is $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ adjusting their scales against each other so that $z\,\mathbf{b}$ lands exactly on the projection.
Try this
  1. Switch the cloud to nearly round, where the two singular values are close. The line still settles, but far more slowly, and a new start lands it at a visibly different angle for a while: when $\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$ are nearly equal the loss barely prefers one direction, which is what happened to the last two principal angles in the lecture's measurement.
  2. Raise $\eta$ to 0.2. Within a few steps the cost blows up and the readout gives up on the angle: the step along the steep direction of the loss overshoots and each step is larger than the last, the stability limit of Lecture 9, here on the curvature of $E$ in $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$. At 0.12 it still converges, slowly.
  3. Press New start several times. Every run ends on the same purple line with the same cost, and with a different length of $\mathbf{b}$: the subspace is unique, the code's scale is not.

2A non-linear autoencoder follows the curve

Eighty centred points along a parabola, with a little noise. The best line through them (purple, dashed) is the first principal axis, and its error is the floor of widget 1: no linear autoencoder, however trained, gets below it. The green curve is what a tiny non-linear autoencoder decodes from a sweep of its one-number code, $2 \to 8 \to 1 \to 8 \to 2$ with tanh units, trained by gradient descent with momentum on all eighty points. Each step is one gradient step on the whole set.

The mathematics

Encoder $z = \mathbf{w}_2^{\intercal}\tanh(\mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1) + b_2$, decoder $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{W}_4\tanh(\mathbf{w}_3 z + \mathbf{b}_3) + \mathbf{b}_4$, loss $E = \frac{1}{m}\sum_i\lVert\mathbf{x}_i - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_i\rVert^2$, gradients by reverse mode through the four layers (Lecture 8), the gradient clipped to norm at most 1, and the update with momentum $\beta = 0.9$: $\mathbf{v} \leftarrow \beta\mathbf{v} - \eta\,\nabla E$, $\boldsymbol\theta \leftarrow \boldsymbol\theta + \mathbf{v}$ (both from Lecture 9; without the clipping, one large early step can saturate a tanh unit, and a saturated unit has no gradient left to learn with). The derivation of the lecture bounds every linear autoencoder by the projection onto the principal axis; it says nothing about this model, whose reconstructions lie on a curve rather than a line. What the non-linear model gains over the floor is exactly the part of the error that no line can remove: the curvature of the data.

Watch for
  • The green curve starts as a short, nearly straight stroke near the origin and bends to follow the parabola. The cost crosses the dashed floor within the first hundred steps and keeps falling well below it.
  • The code is one number, exactly as in widget 1. The gain comes from what the decoder does with it: a line through the origin in widget 1, a curve here.
  • Away from the data, the green curve goes wherever the decoder happens to extrapolate. A code between two seen codes decodes to a point near the curve; a code beyond the seen range decodes to nothing the data vouches for — the motivation, in one picture, for the variational autoencoder's constraint on the code.
Try this
  1. Set the curvature to none: the points lie on a line. Now the floor is almost all noise, and the non-linear model cannot go far below it, because there is no curvature left to remove. The two models tie, which is the derivation's bound seen as a limit on the gain rather than on the linear model.
  2. Lower $\eta$ to 0.01. The curve takes longer to bend; the floor is crossed later and the cost at step 300 is higher. Capacity that the optimiser has not reached yet is not accuracy, which is how the exact projection beat the network at $d = 128$ in the lecture.
  3. Press New start. The final curve is nearly the same; the final code values along it are not. The representation is the curve; the coordinates on it are whatever the start produced.
