The format, with one change: the deep dive

  • A real interview has two halves. First the candidate sketches the whole system, a sentence per step
  • Then the interviewer picks one box and digs: “tell me more about that”, “what if …”, “why not the other way?” — until the candidate reaches the edge of what they know
  • The deep dive is where senior and junior candidates separate. Anyone can name a technique; few can say when it breaks
  • Today the box is the representation: who decides what the model looks at — you, or the network?
How to survive a deep dive Answer the question asked, then say what it costs. When you do not know, say how you would find out: which experiment, on which data.

The interviewer

“We are a farmers’ cooperative. A farmer photographs a sick leaf with a phone. Design the system that tells them what disease it is.”

What do you say first?

Step 1 · Your turn

Before you design anything: what do you ask?

Write down the questions that would change the design.

Which plants, which diseases, which phones, where the photo is taken, what happens after the answer, and what you can learn from.

Step 1 — The questions, and why each one matters

QuestionWhy it changes the design
Which crops, which diseases?the classes, and whether two of them look alike
Where is the photo taken, and by whom?a lab table or a field: the hardest fact for any representation

Step 1 — What the interviewer answers

  • Crops: tomato, potato and grapevine; about twenty diseases between them, plus “healthy”. Some look alike: early and late blight, downy and powdery mildew
  • Photos: taken in the field, on whatever phone the farmer owns, in sun or shade, often with soil, hands and other leaves in the frame
  • Connection: often none in the field. The diagnosis must run on the phone; photos upload later
  • Next: the app shows the likely diseases and the cooperative’s treatment advice; an agronomist answers the cases the app sends on
  • Labels: a few thousand field photos from past consultations, labelled by the agronomist who answered, mostly of the common diseases; and a large public set of single leaves shot on a plain table in a lab
Write it on the board Two facts will decide the deep dive: field photos, and few field labels for the rare diseases.

Step 2 · Your turn

State the machine learning task

What goes in, what comes out, and what may the app answer when it cannot tell?

Step 2 — Input, output, decision

  • Input: the photo, and the crop the farmer selected — region and date as context
  • Output: a probability for each disease of that crop and for “healthy”: one softmax per crop. The agronomists label the main disease, so one answer per leaf is a fair assumption — say it aloud
  • Decision: show the top three with their treatments; if the top probability is low, or the photo is not a usable leaf, abstain and send it to an agronomist
Red flag An app that always answers. A confident wrong diagnosis sends a farmer to spray the wrong product; “I am not sure, an agronomist will look” is part of the output, not a failure of it.

Steps 3 and 4 — Metrics and labels, at interview pace

Metrics

  • Recall per disease, the dangerous ones read first; top-three accuracy, because the app shows three
  • Mistakes weighted by what they cost: two diseases with the same treatment are a cheap confusion; a fungus called a virus is not
  • Accuracy against coverage: how right the app is on the cases it answers, and how many it sends on
  • Online: the agronomists’ verdicts on a sample of answered cases

Labels

  • Consultation photos: farmers ask when worried, so severe cases are over-represented — selection bias
  • Agronomists disagree on look-alikes: label a slice twice and measure it; that is the ceiling
  • The lab set is plentiful, and taken in conditions the app will never see
  • Split by farm and by season, never by photo: one farm sends many photos of the same field

The interviewer nods and stops you here: “Fine. Now tell me what your model looks at.”

The deep dive · Steps 5 and 6

“What does your model look at — and who decided that?”

Two solutions, then a choice

Steps 5–6 · Your turn

Two ways to turn a photo into a diagnosis

Sketch a system in which you decide what the model measures, and one in which the network decides. Do not pick one yet.

The two solutions, side by side

The pipelineWho decides what is measured
A · Features by handphoto → find the leaf → measure colour, spots, texture, shape → a small classifier on a few dozen numbersthe agronomist and the engineer, in advance
B · End to endphoto → a convolutional network → one probability per diseasegradient descent, from the labelled photos
  • A is how plant pathology was automated before convolutional networks; B is what most such apps ship today
  • Neither is obsolete. Each wins under conditions you can state — that is what the interviewer wants to hear by the end

Solution A · Your turn

You are the agronomist. What would you measure?

An agronomist tells blight from mildew by looking. Turn what they look at into numbers a classifier can use.

And before measuring anything on the leaf: how do you know which pixels are leaf?

Solution A — First, find the leaf

  • Segment by colour: convert to a colour space where “green” is one channel’s range (hue, or the a* axis of Lab), threshold it, keep the largest connected region
  • Separate the lesions inside the leaf the same way: brown, yellow, black and white pixels that are not healthy green
  • Normalise: correct the white balance, so the same yellow under sun and under shade gives the same numbers
Note what just happened Every feature on the next slide is measured on this mask. If the mask is wrong, every feature is wrong, and the classifier is never told.

Solution A — The features, and the symptom each one reads

GroupMeasured asThe symptom it reads
Colourhistograms of the lesion pixels’ colour; the share of the leaf that is yellowchlorosis; the brown of blight against the white of mildew
Spotsnumber of lesions, their sizes, how round they are, concentric ringsthe target-shaped spots of early blight
Texturestatistics of local intensity patterns inside the lesionsthe powdery film of powdery mildew; a mosaic pattern of a virus
Shape and positionleaf outline and curl; lesions on the margin or along the veinsleaf curl; spots bounded by the veins
Severitylesion area over leaf areahow far it has gone — useful advice in itself

Then a classifier that suits a few dozen numbers and a few labels: logistic regression, or gradient-boosted trees for the interactions between colour and spot shape.

The interviewer pushes

“Here is a real photo: three leaves, soil, a thumb, the sun behind them. Walk me through your pipeline.”

  • The colour threshold keeps the green of the other leaves and drops the brown dead tissue — which is the disease. The largest region is the wrong leaf
  • Backlight turns healthy green to yellow: the “chlorosis” feature fires on a healthy plant
  • Soil is brown and spotted: the spot counter counts it
A strong answer Say that the first step fails, and fix the capture before the model: the app asks for one leaf, held against the sky or a sheet of paper, and a quality check refuses dark, blurred or cluttered photos. The user interface is part of the feature engineering.

Solution A — What it buys, and what it costs

Solution B · Your turn

Let the network decide. What exactly do you train?

On which photos, starting from which weights, at which resolution — and how does it fit on a phone?

Solution B — The model

  • Start pretrained. A few thousand field photos cannot train a network from scratch; a backbone pretrained on a large image collection already knows edges, textures and blobs. Probe first: a linear head on frozen features, the number to beat; then fine-tune the last blocks
  • Small enough for the phone: a backbone designed for mobile devices, not the largest one; measure its accuracy against its size
  • Resolution: an early lesion is a few pixels once the photo is shrunk to the network’s input. Find the leaf first with a small detector, crop it, then classify the crop
  • Output: one head per crop, or the crop as an input; a softmax, and abstention below a threshold

Solution B keeps A’s first step, done by a learned detector instead of a colour threshold.

The interviewer pushes

“You have few field photos. Which augmentations would you use — and which would you refuse?”

AugmentationUse it?Why
flips, rotationsyesa leaf has no “up”
random crops and scaleyesfarmers hold the phone at every distance
brightness, contrast, bluryes, mildlysun and shade; phones that do not focus
hue shifts, strong colour jitternoyellow against green is the symptom: shifting it relabels the photo

An augmentation is a claim that the label does not change. Make it only where an agronomist would agree. And on the training loader only: an augmented validation set no longer measures the model.

The interviewer pushes

“You trained on the large lab set and got a near-perfect test score. Ship it?”

  • No. The test photos came from the same lab: one leaf, the same table, the same light, often each disease photographed in its own session
  • A network takes whatever separates the classes most cheaply. If the background or the light differs between diseases, it learns those — a shortcut. The test set shares the shortcut, so it cannot see it
  • This has happened in published work: near-perfect on lab photos, and far lower on photos from the field
How to find out Test on field photos, split by farm and by season. Paste the leaves onto other backgrounds and see if the answer changes. Look at what the network attends to, with the explanation tools of the auditing lecture: the lesion, or the table?

Solution B — What it buys, and what it costs

It buysIt costs

robustness to clutter and light — if, and only if, it was trained on photos that have themshortcuts, learned silently, invisible to a test set that shares them

There is no free winner. The interviewer is waiting for the conditions, not for a name.

A strong answer: B, built on A’s parts

  • Ship B: a mobile backbone, pretrained, fine-tuned on the field photos; the lab set only as extra training data, never as the test
  • Keep A’s pieces where they win: the capture guide and the quality check in front; the leaf found before it is classified; lesion area as the severity shown to the farmer
  • A is the baseline B must beat, on field photos split by farm, disease by disease. A rare disease where B does not beat A keeps A’s answer, or goes to an agronomist
I would switch to A if …I would stay with B because …
the photos were taken at a fixed station — same light, plain background — and labels were scarcethe photos are taken in the field, and each season brings more labelled ones

What the interviewer writes down in the deep dive

  • “Hand-made features are obsolete” — or “networks are a black box, so hand features are safer”: either one, with no conditions
  • “The network learns its own features, so the data does not matter”: what it learns is decided by the photos
  • A test score on lab photos reported as the field accuracy
  • A hue or colour augmentation on a task where colour is the label
  • Training from scratch on a few thousand photos
  • No baseline: B with nothing to beat
  • Cannot say what would change the choice

The whole system, on one slide

Photo, with the crop selected → capture guide and quality check (dark, blurred, no leaf: ask again) → a small detector finds the leaf and crops it → a fine-tuned mobile network: one probability per disease of that crop → above the threshold, show the top three with treatments, and the lesion area as severity; below it, send to an agronomist → the agronomists’ answers, and a random sample they check, become labels → retrain each season, test on the newest season by farm, disease by disease, against the hand-feature baseline.

The red flags an interviewer writes down

  • Designs before asking where the photos are taken
  • An app that always answers
  • Accuracy on a random split of photos: the same farm on both sides
  • Lab photos as the test of a field system
  • One solution named, no baseline, no condition under which the other would win
  • Augmentations that change the label
  • A model too big for the phone it must run on, offline
  • Stops at training: no capture guide, no abstention, no feedback loop

What we did

  1. Ran a second mock interview, with a deep dive: the whole design sketched quickly, then one box — the representation — pushed on until it broke
  2. Framed the task as one softmax per crop with abstention, measured by recall per disease, top three and accuracy against coverage, on field photos split by farm and season
  3. Solution A, features by hand: find the leaf, measure colour, spots, texture and shape, a small classifier. Few labels and an explanation; brittle capture and a ceiling
  4. Solution B, end to end: a pretrained mobile network fine-tuned on field photos, the leaf cropped first, augmentations that keep the label. A higher ceiling; more labels, and shortcuts that a lab test cannot see
  5. Chose B, kept A’s capture step, severity and baseline, and said under which conditions A would win

Solutions — Lecture 14

1. Two boxes are disjoint and moved further apart. State what IoU and its derivative do, and why no learning…

Both are identically zero; a constant has no descent direction.

  • Past contact the intersection is exactly zero for every separation
  • The gradient is absent rather than small, so no optimiser can act on it

2. A detector counts objects with MAE 3.00 at the conventional score cut-off of 0.5. A sweep over the same 128…

The cut-off was chosen on the same images the error is reported on, so 1.92 is optimistic by construction; quote it as a diagnostic, beside the 3.00 at the threshold fixed before looking.

  • Picking 0.75 after seeing the error curve is fitting a hyperparameter on the test data
  • With no held-out images, only a threshold fixed in advance gives an honest figure

Solutions — Lecture 14 (2)

3. Average precision is defined with a maximum inside it. State what that maximum repairs, and which earlier…

It replaces the non-monotone precision by its running maximum — the non-monotonicity proved in Lecture 3.

  • Precision falls at every false positive and can rise again
  • Integrating the raw curve would reward the sawtooth rather than the ranking

4. mAP is a mean over classes of a mean over IoU thresholds. Give one thing each averaging hides.

The class mean hides that a one-instance class weighs as much as a 350-instance one; the threshold mean hides the spread between loose and tight matching.

  • An unweighted mean ignores how many instances each class has
  • A single number in the middle stands for two very different regimes

Solutions — Lecture 14 (3)

5. Your mAP over IoU 0.50 to 0.95 on 128 COCO images is 43.9; torchvision reports 37.0 for the same weights on…

Run a reference evaluator on the same predictions and annotations (ours agreed to 0.0014); the gap is then the sample — 128 images, many easy, and classes with a single instance.

  • An implementation you wrote yourself is checked against one you did not before its number is quoted
  • A small evaluation set moves the score, here in the flattering direction, with the detector unchanged
