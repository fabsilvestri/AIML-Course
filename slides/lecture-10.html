Mathematical thread 5

SVD, PCA, and
Johnson–Lindenstrauss

20 minutes

The question

400 points in $\mathbb{R}^{4,096}$.

How many of those 4,096 numbers are doing work — and what exactly do you lose by throwing the rest away?

Why you need the answer Every cost model on the previous deck was linear in the number of features, and every distance was computed over all of them.

Centre first, and say why

Stack the photographs as the rows of $\mathbf{X}$ and subtract the column means:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{X} - \mathbf{1}\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{\intercal}$$

Without centring, the first component points at the mean face and describes nothing but “this is a photograph of a face”. Scikit-Learn's PCA centres for you; if you write the SVD by hand, it does not.

This is the commonest silent error in a hand-rolled PCA, and it produces a plausible-looking first component.

The singular value decomposition

$$\tilde{\mathbf{X}} \;=\; \mathbf{U}\,\boldsymbol\Sigma\,\mathbf{V}^{\intercal}$$
  • $\mathbf{U}$ — $m \times m$, orthogonal
  • $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ — diagonal, entries $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0$, the singular values
  • $\mathbf{V}$ — $n \times n$, orthogonal; its columns are the principal components

Every real matrix has one. That is the whole reason this works on any dataset rather than on well-behaved ones.

You have met $\mathbf{V}$ before

$$\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\intercal}\tilde{\mathbf{X}} \;=\; \mathbf{V}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{\intercal}\mathbf{U}^{\intercal}\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^{\intercal} \;=\; \mathbf{V}\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{2}\,\mathbf{V}^{\intercal}$$

So the columns of $\mathbf{V}$ are the eigenvectors of $\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\intercal}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}$, with eigenvalues $\sigma_j^{2}$.

Thread 1, returning That is the matrix the normal equation needed to invert. It is singular exactly when some $\sigma_j = 0$ — and the same decomposition that diagnosed the failure there is the method here.

Keeping $d$ components

Write $\mathbf{V}_d$ for the first $d$ columns of $\mathbf{V}$. Projecting and coming back:

$$\mathbf{Z} = \tilde{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{V}_d \qquad\text{and}\qquad \tilde{\mathbf{X}}_d = \mathbf{Z}\mathbf{V}_d^{\intercal}$$

$\mathbf{Z}$ is $m \times d$ — the compressed data. $\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_d$ is $m \times n$ again, but it has rank $d$: it lives in a $d$-dimensional subspace of the original space.

In scikit-learn these two are transform and inverse_transform. The second is not an inverse; it is the best re-embedding of a projection.

In what sense is that the best $d$ dimensions?

Among all rank-$d$ matrices, $\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_d$ minimises the Frobenius distance to $\tilde{\mathbf{X}}$ — and the error it leaves is exactly the tail of the spectrum:

$$\bigl\lVert \tilde{\mathbf{X}} - \tilde{\mathbf{X}}_d \bigr\rVert_F^{2} \;=\; \sum_{j > d} \sigma_j^{2}$$

Eckart–Young–Mirsky

So “PCA minimises reconstruction error” is a theorem, not a slogan — and the quantity it minimises is the sum of squared distances from each point to the subspace.

The same statement, read twice

Minimise error

Choose the subspace closest to the points, in squared distance.

Maximise variance

Choose the subspace in which the projected points are most spread out.

These are the same problem, by Pythagoras: for a centred point, $\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2 = \lVert\text{projection}\rVert^2 + \lVert\text{residual}\rVert^2$, and the left-hand side does not depend on the subspace.

The book states the second form. The first is the one that explains why the reconstructed faces look like faces.

Verify the theorem, on our own data


U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X - X.mean(axis=0), full_matrices=False)

d   = 100
Xd  = U[:, :d] * S[:d] @ Vt[:d]
lhs = ((X_centred - Xd) ** 2).sum()      # ‖X − X_d‖²_F
rhs = (S[d:] ** 2).sum()                 # Σ_{j>d} σ_j²

assert abs(lhs - rhs) / rhs < 1e-5

A cheap, decisive check that the library did what the algebra says. Note again that “small” is meaningless until you divide by something.

The share of total variance carried by component $j$ — pca.explained_variance_ratio_, cumulated to choose $d$.

How many components you actually need

123 components hold 95% of the variance of 4,096 pixels — a factor of 33 fewer numbers per photograph.

The components live in the same space as the data

A principal component is a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{4,096}$. Reshape it to 64 × 64 and it is an image.

Why they have a name The principal components of a face dataset are called eigenfaces, because they are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix and they look like faces. Every photograph in the corpus is the mean face plus a weighted sum of them.

This is not a property of faces. It is a property of PCA: the components are directions in the input space, so whatever an input looks like, a component looks like too.

The first fifteen eigenfaces

Look at the first few. They are lighting — which is precisely what the clusters grouped by.

The same face, with components taken away

Recognisable well before 123 components. The identity survives compression that the pixel values do not.

The other way to reduce a dimension

PCA looks at the data and spends an SVD finding the best subspace. The alternative is to not look at the data at all: draw a random matrix $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ with independent Gaussian entries and set

$$\mathbf{Z} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{R}$$

This sounds absurd. It is not, and the reason is a theorem.

The Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma

For any $0 < \varepsilon < 1$ and any set of $m$ points in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$, there is a linear map into $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ with

$$d \;\ge\; \frac{4\log m}{\varepsilon^{2}/2 \;-\; \varepsilon^{3}/3}$$

such that for every pair of points $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}$,

$$(1-\varepsilon)\lVert \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\rVert^{2} \;\le\; \lVert f(\mathbf{u})-f(\mathbf{v})\rVert^{2} \;\le\; (1+\varepsilon)\lVert \mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\rVert^{2}$$

And a random Gaussian matrix is such a map with high probability, which is why the construction is a one-liner.

Read the formula again. What is missing?

$$d \;\ge\; \frac{4\log m}{\varepsilon^{2}/2 - \varepsilon^{3}/3}$$

In the formula

  • $m$, the number of points — logarithmically
  • $\varepsilon$, the tolerance

Not in the formula

$n$

The dimension you are starting from.

400 points need the same target dimension whether they live in 4,096 dimensions or in four million.

Stop here. Ask the room to say what is missing before advancing. Most will look for $n$ and not find it, which is the moment.

What actually happens to our distances

At $d = 200$ the worst-distorted pair of the 79,800 is off by 0.53 — a tolerance the bound would only guarantee at 1,382 dimensions.

What to take from thread 5

  1. PCA is the SVD of the centred data, and the components are the eigenvectors of $\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\intercal}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}$
  2. It minimises squared reconstruction error, exactly, and the error it leaves is the tail of the spectrum
  3. The explained variance ratio is how you choose $d$ without guessing
  4. Distances can be preserved by a map whose target dimension depends on the number of points and the tolerance — and not on the dimension you start in

Point 4 returns in the last lecture of the course, where the same concentration argument explains why unrelated embeddings on a high-dimensional sphere are nearly orthogonal.

Defect 1 — the clock

Both are linear in $n$. We were carrying 4,096 features and, by the scree plot, about 123 of them were load-bearing.

Roughly 33 times more arithmetic than the problem required — not because anything was written badly, but because nobody chose the representation. It was the size of the image.

Defect 2 — in high dimensions, everything is far away

Take two points uniformly in the unit hypercube. The expected distance between them grows with the dimension: about 0.52 in two dimensions, about 26 in a million.

The consequence for the silhouette $s_i = (b-a)/\max(a,b)$. As $n$ grows, $a$ and $b$ both grow and their ratio approaches one, so $s_i$ approaches zero for every point. A flat, low silhouette curve is what dimensional concentration looks like.

Our best silhouette was 0.1707. That is well above the random anchor and still very close to zero, and now you know one reason why.

Defect 3 — there was no elbow to find

Not a property of the data. A property of inertia: it is non-increasing in $k$ and zero at $k = m$. Nothing in it knows how many people are in the archive.

  • The kneedle rule picked $k = 19
  • At $k = 40$, 46% of the inertia at $k=2$ was still there

This one is not repaired by reducing the dimension. It is repaired by not using inertia to choose $k$, which we already did.

Defect 4 — the number we report is measured on forty photographs

  • 780 pairs, of which only 22 are the same person
  • Every one of those pairs moves the ARI by a visible amount
  • So a difference of a few hundredths between two clusterings is not evidence of anything
The honest way to use it Treat the ARI on the forty as a sanity check with a wide interval, not as a ranking. Use the silhouette, held out, to choose between models; use the forty to confirm the winner is not nonsense.

The repair, in three lines

from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

Z = PCA(n_components=0.95, random_state=42).fit_transform(X)

for k in range(2, 61):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit(Z)

n_components=0.95 — a float between 0 and 1 is read as a variance target rather than a component count, and scikit-learn chooses $d$ for you. Here it chooses 123.

The clustering code is unchanged. Only what it is looking at changed.

Careful: the silhouette went up

This is not evidence that the clustering improved The silhouette is computed in whatever space you hand it. Compressing the data changes the distances, so the criterion is not comparable across the rows of that table.

Which is exactly why the ARI column is there. It is measured against the identities and does not depend on the representation, so it is the only column in which the rows may be compared.

Generalise the rule: never compare two scores measured on different evaluation sets — and a different representation is a different evaluation set.

The default svd_solver="auto" chooses between the first two by a size rule. That is a default you did not ask for; print pca.svd_solver_ if the timing surprises you.

Cost and quality of the four

Random projection is essentially free and pays for it in reconstruction error. The three PCA variants land on the same error, at different prices.

What the random subspace costs

PCA minimises exactly this quantity, so it wins everywhere. The question is whether you need the reconstruction or only the distances.

Choosing between them, as a rule

Use PCA when you will reconstruct, visualise, or interpret the components; when the data fits; when $d$ should be chosen by a variance target.
Use random projection when the ambient dimension is so large that the SVD itself is the bottleneck, and all you need is that distances survive.

Here, PCA. 400 × 4,096 is a small matrix and the components are interpretable images.

k-means still assumes the wrong shape

Compression fixed the clock. It did not fix the geometry: every k-means cluster is still a convex Voronoi cell, every point still belongs to exactly one, and every group is still assumed to be the same size.

Two methods from Chapter 8 that drop those assumptions.

DBSCAN, in three definitions

  • A point is a core point if at least min_samples points lie within eps of it
  • Core points within eps of each other are in the same cluster; so are the non-core points in their neighbourhoods
  • Everything else is noise, labelled $-1$

db = DBSCAN(eps=6.0, min_samples=3).fit(Z)
n_clusters = len(set(db.labels_) - {-1})
n_noise    = (db.labels_ == -1).sum()

DBSCAN has no predict. It is transductive: to label a new point you fit again, or you train a classifier on its output.

DBSCAN on the compressed faces

At small eps everything is noise; at large eps everything is one cluster. The most it ever finds is 50, and never 40.

Reporting a method that did not work

DBSCAN, best over the whole grid ARI 0.133 at eps = 6.00, with 41 clusters and 97 faces called noise.

DBSCAN needs a single density scale that separates clusters from the gaps between them. Forty groups of ten faces, all lit by the same rig, do not have one: the gap between two people is about the same as the gap between two photographs of one person.

This is a real result and it belongs on the slide. A method that fails for a stateable reason is more informative than a method that was never tried.

Gaussian mixtures

Model the data as coming from $k$ Gaussians with unknown means, covariances and weights, and fit by expectation–maximisation:

$$p(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi_j\,\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{x}\,;\,\boldsymbol\mu_j,\,\boldsymbol\Sigma_j\right)$$
  • Clusters are ellipsoids, so they may be elongated and of different sizes
  • Assignment is soft: predict_proba returns a distribution
  • It is a density model, so it also scores how likely a new point is — which is anomaly detection

Why this could not have been done last lecture

A full covariance in $n$ dimensions has $n(n+1)/2$ free parameters.

SettingParameters
One full covariance in 4,096 dimensions8,390,656
Forty of them335,626,240
Estimated from400 photographs
Forty diagonal covariances in 123 dimensions9,880

The first is not slow. It is undefined: every covariance estimate is singular.

covariance_type is "full", "tied", "diag" or "spherical". We use "diag", and that is a decision, not a default.

What BIC chooses, and what recovers the people

BIC picks 5 components; the ARI peaks at 55. The criterion you can compute and the answer you want are not the same function.

BIC and AIC, and what they are for

$$\mathrm{BIC} = p\log m - 2\log \hat{L}, \qquad \mathrm{AIC} = 2p - 2\log \hat{L}$$

$p$ is the number of parameters, $m$ the number of instances, $\hat{L}$ the maximised likelihood. Both penalise complexity; BIC penalises it harder once $\log m > 2$, so it tends to choose fewer components.

They answer a different question BIC asks which model best explains this data as a density. We are asking which grouping matches the people. Those coincide only when the people really are Gaussian blobs.

Anomaly detection, two ways

By density

A Gaussian mixture is a density. Score every image and call the lowest few anomalous.


d = gmm.score_samples(Z)
bad = d < np.percentile(d, 3)

By reconstruction error

An image the eigenfaces cannot rebuild is unlike the images that built them.


R = pca.inverse_transform(pca.transform(Xa))
err = ((R - Xa) ** 2).mean(axis=1)

Test both by planting twelve corrupted images — rotated, dimmed, double-exposed — and counting how many each finds.

What each detector found

Reconstruction error caught 12 of the 12 planted images; the mixture density caught 6.

Why one of them won

  • A rotated or double-exposed face is outside the subspace the eigenfaces span, so its reconstruction error is large — median 0.0017 against 0.0002 for a genuine face
  • The mixture density is estimated inside that subspace, so a corruption that projects onto an ordinary-looking point is invisible to it
The decision rule Use the reconstruction error when the anomaly is a different kind of object. Use the density when it is an ordinary object in an unusual place.

Finally: the forty labels

Split the corpus 280 / 120, stratified by identity. We may label forty of the 280 training photographs. Where we spend them turns out to matter more than what we do with them.

  1. Forty at random
  2. Forty chosen as the photograph nearest each of forty cluster centroids
  3. The representative's label propagated to its whole cluster — 280 labels from 40
  4. Propagated only to the 75% of each cluster closest to the centroid
  5. All 280 true labels — the ceiling, which the project cannot buy

Propagation, in six lines

km   = KMeans(n_clusters=40, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit(Z_train)
dist = km.transform(Z_train)                 # (280, 40)
rep  = np.argmin(dist, axis=0)               # nearest photo to each centroid
y_rep = y_train[rep]                         # the 40 labels we pay for

propagated = y_rep[km.labels_]               # 280 labels from 40

own = dist[np.arange(len(Z_train)), km.labels_]
keep = own <= np.percentile(own[km.labels_ == c], 75)   # per cluster

Line 3 is the whole idea: rather than labelling forty photographs at random, label the forty that are most representative. Line 9 drops the instances furthest from a centroid, which are the ones most likely to have been propagated the wrong label.

What forty labels buy

Same budget, same classifier, same features. The difference is entirely which forty photographs were labelled.

Beat 1 — the prompt

What a reasonable person types “Reduce the faces to 95% of the variance with PCA and train a classifier; report the held-out accuracy and the reconstruction error.”

Every clause is a legitimate request. The order in which they get executed is not stated, and that is the whole defect.

Beat 2 — the code it returns

It runs. It reports two numbers, both believable. One of them is fiction.

Beat 3 — the question that catches it

Reviewer question 2: what was fitted, and on what?

Line 1, before the split on line 4 PCA(...).fit(X) — all 400 photographs, including the 120 we are about to call held out. The subspace those faces are projected onto was chosen partly by those faces.

You met the same shape in the first application, where it was worth about a dollar. Here it is not, and the reason is arithmetic: the 280 training faces span at most a 280-dimensional subspace of 4,096, so adding 120 more points genuinely changes which directions survive.

Beat 3b — measure it, do not assert it


for seed in range(20):
    X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(
        X, y, test_size=0.3, stratify=y, random_state=seed)

    honest = PCA(n_components=123, random_state=42).fit(X_tr)
    leaky  = PCA(n_components=123, random_state=42).fit(X)      # all 400

    rows.append((err(honest), err(leaky), acc(honest), acc(leaky)))

Twenty splits. Two consequences, measured separately, because they do not behave the same way — and reporting only the one that moves would be the dishonesty this course exists to remove.

One number moves a lot; the other barely moves

The leak makes the reconstruction error look 61% better and moves the accuracy by -0.0%.

Why the two behave differently

Fitted on trainFitted on everythingDifference
Reconstruction error, held out0.002430.00096−61%
Accuracy, held out97.4%97.4%-0.0%
  • Reconstruction error is the objective PCA optimises. Letting the held-out faces help choose the subspace optimises it on them directly: lower in 20 of 20 splits
  • Accuracy is a different objective, several steps downstream, and the classifier is refitted honestly either way — so the effect is small and inside the noise

The decision rule, which is why we measured

When does fitting an unsupervised step on everything cost you? Exactly when that step's own objective is the number you report. Reconstruction error is that number. Downstream accuracy usually is not.

Compare with the first application, where scaling before the split cost about a dollar: centring and scaling is an invertible affine map, a least-squares fit is equivariant under one, and there were 20,640 rows. None of those three protections applies to a rank-123 projection of 400 points.

And the procedural rule does not change either way, because you cannot tell which case you are in without doing the split.

Beat 4 — the corrected specification

What to ask for instead “Split first, stratified by identity, fixed seed. Fit PCA on the training faces only and transform the held-out ones. Put both steps in a Pipeline so cross-validation refits the PCA per fold. Report the held-out reconstruction error and the accuracy, each with its spread over seeds.” 

Pipeline([("pca", PCA(n_components=0.95, random_state=42)),
          ("clf", LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000))]).fit(X_train, y_train)

Structurally impossible rather than merely avoided. Same sentence as Application 1, applied to a different estimator.

This is the honest shape of a first project: a correct diagnosis, a plausible fix, and a measurement that refuses it. The remedy that does work is a face-specific representation, which is Lecture 23.

The version of that table that said the opposite

Measured first by sweeping $k$ and keeping whichever gave the best ARI, the same repair appeared to help, by about +0.015. That table would have gone on the slide.

Why it was wrong ARI is computed from the labels — the labels this whole application is pretending not to have. Choosing $k$ by ARI is selecting a hyperparameter on the answer key, and it flatters whichever variant happens to have a lucky $k$ somewhere in the sweep.

Silhouette uses only the geometry, so it is a choice you could actually make without labels — which is why $k$ is chosen by silhouette and ARI is only ever reported.

Same data, same code, two protocols, opposite conclusions. The protocol was the finding.

What we did not do, and why

  • Removed the lighting direction. We did spend the minutes on this one — see the next slide. Both one-line repairs made the clustering worse

  • A face-specific representation. A pretrained embedding would beat all of this and belongs in the computer-vision part of the course, not here

What we learned

  1. PCA is the SVD of the centred data; the components are eigenvectors of $\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\intercal}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}$ and, for images, are themselves images
  2. It minimises squared reconstruction error exactly, leaving the tail of the spectrum — a theorem you can check to machine precision
  3. The explained variance ratio chooses $d$: 123 components hold 95% of 4,096 pixels
  4. Johnson–Lindenstrauss bounds the target dimension by the number of points and the tolerance, never by the dimension you started in — and it is a loose worst case, so measure the distortion you actually get
  5. Compressing first made one sweep 21 times faster without costing agreement with the identities
  6. k-means still assumes convex equal-sized clusters; DBSCAN and mixtures drop that, and here DBSCAN fails for a stateable reason
  7. Reconstruction error finds anomalies of a different kind; density finds ordinary objects in unusual places
  8. Forty labels spent on cluster representatives, then propagated, are worth several times forty labels spent at random
  9. Fitting an unsupervised step before the split costs you exactly when that step's objective is the number you report

Specimen exam question — 3

A team has 5,000 documents represented as 50,000-dimensional vectors. They want to preserve pairwise distances to within 20% while reducing the dimension.
  1. Using the Johnson–Lindenstrauss bound, state the target dimension — and say which of the two given numbers you did not need
  2. They now acquire ten times as many documents. By roughly what factor does the required dimension grow?
  3. Give one reason to prefer PCA here anyway, and one reason not to

Part 1 is two marks: one for the number, one for saying that 50,000 does not enter it.
