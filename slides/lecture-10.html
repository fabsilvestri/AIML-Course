Where we are

Lecture 9 took a twenty-layer network that sat at chance and repaired it:

  • the gradient shrank by a constant factor per layer — a straight line on a log axis — because one layer multiplies the variance by $n\cdot\operatorname{Var}(w)$
  • He initialisation and ReLU set that factor to one; batch normalisation, clipping and an optimiser were measured on top
  • the ablation ladder: one change, ReLU with He, did more than the rest together, and the last rung — dropout — made things worse
Failure condition — a network that learns the training set by heart Train the repaired network for longer and it keeps improving — on the images it trains on. On the images it does not, it stops at forty percent and stays there. Today: why, what the cures are, which of them pay, and what that last rung was.

The error on new images that comes from fitting the particular sample — which pixels this frog has, rather than what a frog looks like. The arithmetic of why the network can:

Dropout: the mechanism

At every training step, each hidden unit is set to zero with probability $p$, independently. At test time nothing is dropped.

layers += [nn.Linear(prev, 100), nn.BatchNorm1d(100), nn.ReLU()]
if dropout and i >= DEPTH - top:        # the top hidden layers only
    layers.append(nn.Dropout(dropout))  # p: the DROP rate, not the keep rate

net.train()   # masks sampled, kept units scaled by 1/(1 - p)
net.eval()    # no masks, no scaling: the full network

Two things to settle before it can be used: what the network without masks computes, and why the kept units are rescaled. Both are the derivation.

A layer of $n$ units is $2^n$ thinned networks

A mask $\mathbf m \in \{0,1\}^n$ chooses which units survive. Each choice is a different, smaller network — a thinned network — and all of them share the same weights. One training step samples one mask, runs that thinned network forward and backward, and updates the shared weights.

$$2^{n} \text{ thinned networks;}\qquad n = 100:\ 2^{100} \approx 10^{30.1}$$ more networks than training steps, by twenty-seven orders of magnitude

So almost every thinned network is trained zero times. What makes this work is that they share weights — a weight that helps one thinned network helps most of them — and the question becomes what the full network, run without masks, has to do with their average.

The expected pre-activation under the mask

One unit of the next layer sees $z = \sum_j w_j a_j$ from activations $a_j$. Under a mask $m_j \sim \text{Bernoulli}(1-p)$, independent across $j$,

$$\tilde z = \sum_j m_j\, w_j\, a_j, \qquad \mathbb E[\tilde z] = \sum_j \mathbb E[m_j]\, w_j a_j = (1-p)\, z$$

by linearity of expectation, with the $a_j$ and $w_j$ held fixed. Train with masks and run without them, and every pre-activation is $1/(1-p)$ times larger than anything the weights were trained on.

$$\tilde z = \frac{1}{1-p}\sum_j m_j\, w_j\, a_j \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbb E[\tilde z] = z$$ inverted dropout: divide the kept activations by $1-p$ in training, and the full network at test time computes the expectation
Animation

The expectation is preserved. The variance is not.

For one term, $m_j^2 = m_j$ because $m_j \in \{0, 1\}$, so

$$\mathbb E\!\left[\Big(\frac{m_j a_j}{1-p}\Big)^{2}\right] = \frac{\mathbb E[m_j]\, a_j^2}{(1-p)^2} = \frac{a_j^2}{1-p}$$

Every dropout layer multiplies the second moment of the signal passing through it by $1/(1-p)$. The expectation is right; the spread around it is not, and on a deep stack it compounds:

Dropout on$p$Factor on the second moment, $(1-p)^{-L}$
one layer0.52
all twenty layers0.18.23
all twenty layers0.51,048,576

Lecture 9’s argument about $\rho$, again: a factor applied twenty times is a different thing from a factor applied once. Dropout goes on the top layer or two.

The derivation, measured on the trained network

On the last hidden layer, 20,000 masks at $p = 0.5$: the average is within 0.0433 of $z$ on pre-activations of mean size 1.999, and the second moment is 2.000 times the unmasked one.

What the result buys you

  • An ensemble for free. The network run without masks computes the expectation over $2^{n}$ thinned networks — an average of models that share weights, Lecture 5’s cure for variance without the forest’s bill
  • No unit can rely on a particular neighbour, because the neighbour is absent half the time; the features it learns are individually useful rather than co-adapted
  • Keep the masks on at test time and you have the ensemble’s spread as well as its mean: Monte Carlo dropout, a measure of how sure the network is, for the price of $T$ forward passes
  • Where to put it: the top layer or two, at $p \in [0.2, 0.5]$, because each dropout layer multiplies the second moment by $1/(1-p)$ and the factor compounds with depth

Weight decay: the step, written out

Add $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\theta\rVert^2$ to the loss. Its gradient is $\lambda\theta$, so one gradient step is

$$\theta \;\leftarrow\; \theta - \eta\,(\nabla L + \lambda\theta) \;=\; (1 - \eta\lambda)\,\theta \;-\; \eta\nabla L$$
$$\theta \leftarrow (1 - \eta\lambda)\,\theta - \eta\nabla L$$ first shrink every weight by the factor $1-\eta\lambda$, then take the usual step

The $\ell_2$ penalty and the per-step shrinkage are the same thing — for gradient descent. With $\eta = 0.001$ and $\lambda = 0.2$ the factor is 0.9998 per step; over the 2,370 steps of a thirty-epoch run a weight that receives no gradient shrinks to 0.62 of itself. A weight that earns its size keeps it; one that does not, decays.

Where AdamW puts it

Adam divides each coordinate’s step by a running estimate of its gradient’s size. Put $\lambda\theta$ into the gradient and it is divided too:

Adam(weight_decay=λ) — coupled$\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\dfrac{\widehat m(\nabla L + \lambda\theta)}{\sqrt{\widehat v} + \epsilon}$normalised away: every weight moves by about $\eta$, whatever its size
AdamW(weight_decay=λ) — decoupled$\theta \leftarrow (1-\eta\lambda)\,\theta - \eta\,\dfrac{\widehat m(\nabla L)}{\sqrt{\widehat v} + \epsilon}$the shrinkage, as derived

Failure condition — the penalty inside Adam

Adam(weight_decay=0.2) on this network Test accuracy 10.00% — chance — and $\lVert\theta\rVert = 0.2$ after thirty epochs, against 83.9 without decay. The normalised penalty moves every weight by about $\eta$ towards zero at every step whatever its size, and five hundred thousand weights of size 0.1 reach zero in a hundred steps.

Lecture 9’s last rung, explained

That ladder stopped at epoch 20 and read dropout 0.1 on every layer as a loss of seven points. Now the whole curve is in view:

  • at epoch 20 it stood at 33.90% on validation against 40.26% without dropout — the row Lecture 9 printed
  • its training accuracy at that epoch was 39.09%: it was not overfitting, it was barely fitting
  • the second moment of the signal was multiplied by 8.23 on the way up twenty layers of masks, and the network spent its epochs learning through that noise

Dropout on every layer of a deep, narrow stack is not a regulariser. It is a slower learner with a narrower gap.

Lecture 9 read that rung as a regulariser added to a network that was not overfitting. The training column, which that deck did not log, says it was overfitting already; the rung failed for the reason on the previous slide.

Monte Carlo dropout: keep the masks on

net.eval()                              # BatchNorm: running statistics, frozen
for m in net:
    if isinstance(m, nn.Dropout):
        m.train()                       # Dropout: masks on
probs = sum(F.softmax(net(X), 1) for _ in range(T)) / T   # T = 20 passes
pred, conf = probs.argmax(1), probs.max(1).values
  • $T$ thinned networks, averaged: an ensemble whose members were trained together
  • The largest averaged probability is the network’s confidence, and it can be acted on: answer when confident, refer the rest
  • Only the dropout modules go back to training mode. Switch the whole network and batch normalisation starts using batch statistics, and the spread measures the batches, not the masks

Confidence that means something

Masks off: 41.81%. Twenty passes averaged: 41.69% — no better. But the top decile by confidence is right 79.6% of the time and the bottom decile 20.2%; answer the 80% most confident and the accuracy on what is answered is 46.81%.

Cure 4 — data augmentation

A photograph flipped left-to-right, or shifted by two pixels, is a photograph of the same thing. Train on random copies and the network is told so: the function it fits has to give the same answer on an image and its copies, which rules out the part of the hypothesis space that was memorising pixel positions.

def augment(xb, g):                      # on standardised rows, as images
    img = xb.view(n, 32, 32, 3)
    flip = torch.rand(n, generator=g) < 0.5
    img = torch.where(flip[:, None, None, None], img.flip(2), img)
    padded = F.pad(img.permute(0, 3, 1, 2), (2, 2, 2, 2))    # 36 x 36
    ...                                  # a random 32 x 32 window of it
xb = augment(Xf[idx], g)                 # a FRESH copy of every batch, every step

Which transforms is a statement about the data: a flipped digit is a different digit, a flipped frog is a frog.

One training image, seven random copies

The network never sees the same batch twice. It cannot memorise what it never sees twice.

Failure condition — the evaluator’s transform

The same transform on the test loader Score the augmented network on a random copy of each test image instead of the image: 43.64% instead of 45.15%. The network did not change; the task did. A shifted test image with a two-pixel border is not a photograph anyone will hand the system.
  • Augmentation lives in the training path, and only there. Validation and test images are never transformed
  • The usual way to get this wrong is a single transform object shared by both loaders. The usual symptom is nothing: a plausible number, a little low
  • The network trained without augmentation, on the same copies: 37.48%. Both numbers are honest answers to a question nobody asked

Design exercise · in the manner of an ML system-design interview

The task: the lesion check inside a dermatology app

The brief A consumer health company sells a skin-care app. Its new feature lets a user photograph a mole or lesion with their phone and get one of two answers: looks fine, check again in a few months, or see a dermatologist — here is how to book one. The model behind it is a classifier trained on photographs labelled by dermatologists. The business measures referrals that turn out to be warranted, users retained, and the cost of each dermatologist visit it triggers.
  • Given: a few thousand labelled photographs from partner clinics, far fewer than the network has parameters; a stream of unlabelled user photographs once the feature ships
  • Actors: the product and data teams, the users, the dermatologists who label and who receive the referrals, and the medical-device regulator, because software that tells a person to see or not see a doctor is a medical device
  • Consumers of the output: the user, the booking flow, and the clinical audit the regulator will ask for

Nine steps follow, each with the pitfall that catches a first attempt and what to do about it. Today’s method is at the centre: a network with far more parameters than images, and the cures that keep it honest.

Step 1 — Frame it: what is predicted, what it costs to be wrong

  • One row per photograph; the label is the dermatologist’s verdict, refer or do not refer; the output is a probability of refer, and a threshold on it is the product decision
  • The two errors are not symmetric: a missed melanoma is a harm the company answers for in court; an unneeded visit is a cost and an anxious user. The metric is sensitivity at a fixed, high value, with the specificity it buys, read on a reliability diagram
  • The baseline: the rule dermatologists already teach — asymmetry, border, colour, diameter — scored on the same photographs
Pitfall Accuracy. Most lesions are benign, so a model that refers nobody is accurate and lethal, and a model that refers everybody is safe and useless. Fix the sensitivity first, as a clinical requirement agreed with the dermatologists and written into the regulatory file, and optimise everything else under it.

Step 2 — The label is a clinician’s opinion, and sometimes a biopsy

PitfallWhat happensWhat to do
Two kinds of labelsome photographs carry a biopsy result, most carry one dermatologist’s visual verdict; the second is noisier and the two are mixedkeep the label source as a column; evaluate on biopsy-confirmed rows; train on both, smoothing the visual ones
Disagreementtwo dermatologists disagree on a fraction of borderline lesions; one opinion per image hides thatdouble-label a slice, measure the agreement, and treat it as the ceiling any model can reach
The clinic’s cameraclinic photographs are taken with a dermatoscope under ring light; the user’s are a phone in a bathroomcollect a labelled set of phone photographs before shipping, or the training set is of a different task

Write down what a refer label means and who gave it. The regulator will ask; so will the lawyer.

Step 3 — Split before anything is fitted, and split by patient

  • Every photograph of one patient goes to the same side of the split: training, validation or test. Validation chooses every knob; the test side is read once, for the regulatory file
  • Stratify by the label and by the clinic, so that no clinic’s camera lives only on one side
Pitfall A random split by photograph puts the same lesion, photographed twice at the same visit, on both sides. The network memorises the patient’s skin and the clinic’s lighting — today’s lecture, exactly — and the validation score is a memory test it passes. The number in the regulatory submission is wrong, and it is discovered in the field, on users.

Step 4 — Augmentation that respects the lesion

  • A few thousand images and a network with millions of weights: the ratio on slide 7, worse. Augmentation is the first cure, and the largest
  • Flips, rotations by any angle, small crops and small changes of brightness: a lesion has no orientation and a phone has no fixed exposure
  • In the training path only. The validation and test photographs are scored as they are
Pitfall Colour and scale are the signal. A hue shift that would be harmless on a frog turns a benign brown lesion into a suspicious black one; an aggressive zoom changes the diameter that the clinical rule is built on. The transforms must be chosen with a dermatologist in the room, and each one tested on the validation side for whether it helps or hurts.

Step 5 — The model, and the cures on it

  • A pretrained convolutional backbone, fine-tuned — the subject of the next three lectures — with a dropout layer before the head
  • AdamW with a small decay; early stopping on the validation sensitivity at the agreed specificity, the best weights kept
  • Label smoothing on the visually-labelled rows, where the label is an opinion
Pitfall A training curve that reaches perfect accuracy on the clinic photographs. With a few thousand images the network will memorise them within a few epochs, and a team that watches only the training loss ships the last epoch. Log the validation curve beside it from the first run, stop on it, and keep the ablation table — which cure bought what — because the regulator’s reviewer will ask why each component is there.

Step 6 — When the model is not sure, it says so

  • Monte Carlo dropout at inference: twenty passes, the mean as the probability, the spread as the confidence
  • Three outputs, not two: looks fine, see a dermatologist, and this photograph cannot be assessed — retake it, or see a dermatologist, for the least confident fraction
  • A separate, cheap check that the photograph is of skin at all, in focus, and lit; most low-confidence cases are bad photographs
Pitfall A two-answer product forces a confident verdict on a photograph the model has never seen the like of — a skin tone absent from the clinic data, a tattoo, a bandage. The least confident decile is where the accuracy is lowest, as it was in the lecture, and it is also where the users who most need a doctor are. Abstain and refer; the cost of a visit is bounded, the cost of a confident wrong looks fine is not.

Step 7 — The threshold, the calibration, and who signs it

  • The sensitivity target fixes the threshold on the validation side; the test side, read once, goes into the submission with its confidence interval
  • Calibrate the probability on a held-out slice so that “eight in ten” means eight in ten, and check it per skin tone, per clinic and per phone model — a model calibrated on average and wrong for one group is a model that is wrong
  • The threshold is a clinical decision, signed by the medical director, not a hyperparameter
Pitfall — tuning on the test set The team tries a dozen backbones and thresholds and reports the best test sensitivity. That number is the largest of a dozen draws, and the regulator’s own evaluation on fresh patients comes in lower. The submission is rejected, or worse, accepted. One test set, read once, by someone who did not choose the model.

Step 8 — Release, monitor, retrain

  • Release behind a clinical safety case: a limited cohort first, every referral followed up, every looks fine sampled and reviewed by a dermatologist
  • Monitor the input distribution — phone models, skin tones, lighting, the abstention rate — and the referral rate against the rate the business planned for
  • Retrain on the new, phone-taken, dermatologist-reviewed photographs, through the same split-by-patient pipeline, and re-evaluate on the untouched test set before every release; a changed model is a changed device
Pitfall The feature changes who photographs what. Worried users send more pictures of ordinary moles; reassured users stop sending the ones that changed. The labels that come back are from the referrals the model chose, so retraining on them teaches the model its own habits. Keep the sampled review of the looks fine answers — that is the only unbiased signal the product produces.

What we did

  1. Trained Lecture 9’s repaired network for thirty epochs and watched it memorise: 78.05% on its training images, 38.90% on validation, a gap of thirty-nine points — variance, with 504,210 parameters for 10,000 images
  2. Derived dropout as an average over $2^n$ thinned networks: $\mathbb E[\tilde z] = (1-p)\,z$, so divide by $1-p$ in training and the full network computes the expectation; the second moment grows by $1/(1-p)$ per dropout layer, which is why one layer works and twenty do not
  3. Derived weight decay as the shrinkage $(1-\eta\lambda)\,\theta$ before every step, and saw Adam normalise the coupled penalty into a network at chance; AdamW applies the shrinkage outside the step
  4. Measured five cures alone and on a three-seed ladder: every one narrowed the gap, and augmentation alone raised the test accuracy beyond the seed spread, +3.12 points, because it constrains the function rather than the weights
  5. Kept the masks on at test time — Monte Carlo dropout — and turned confidence into a decision: answer the surest 80%, refer the rest
  6. Named the failure nobody can see from the number: a knob chosen on the test set; the validation set chooses, the test set is read once
  7. Designed the lesion check inside a dermatology app in nine steps — sensitivity fixed as a clinical requirement, labels with their source, a split by patient, augmentation agreed with a dermatologist, early stopping and dropout on a pretrained backbone, a third answer for the unsure cases, a threshold someone signs, a release that is audited — each step named for the pitfall it avoids

Solutions — Lecture 9

1. One layer multiplies the standard deviation of the backward signal by…

They multiply it by $\rho^{L-1}$ — $L-1$ steps between the first gradient and the last — so anything but 1 vanishes or explodes geometrically.

  • A constant applied $L$ times is exponential in $L$
  • There is no regime where a repeated constant other than 1 is safe

2. Preserving the forward signal asks for $\operatorname{Var}(w) = 1/n_{\text{in}}$ and preserving the gradient…

They agree only when $n_{\text{in}} = n_{\text{out}}$; Glorot takes the harmonic mean, $2/(n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}})$.

  • Any layer that changes width cannot satisfy both
  • The compromise accepts a small error in each direction rather than a large one in either

Solutions — Lecture 9 (2)

3. He initialisation doubles the weight variance for ReLU. Derive the factor of two in one line.

ReLU zeroes half of a symmetric zero-mean input, so $\mathbb{E}[\text{ReLU}(z)^2] = \tfrac12\mathbb{E}[z^2]$.

  • Exactly half the variance is discarded at each layer
  • Doubling $\operatorname{Var}(w)$ puts it back

4. A gradient profile that is a straight line on a log axis tells you something a curved one does not. State…

The attenuation is the same factor at every layer, so it is the initialisation rather than one bad layer.

  • A constant ratio per layer is a straight line in log space
  • A single broken layer would show a step, not a slope

Solutions — Lecture 9 (3)

5. Gradient clipping is applied to a network whose gradients are fifteen orders of magnitude too small. Predict…

No effect — clipping bounds gradients that are too large.

  • The threshold is never reached, so nothing is rescaled
  • Reaching for it means the failure mode was never measured
