Part one

The brief

15 minutes · the problem, the data, the stakeholder

You have been hired again

A national postal operator sorts handwritten postcodes automatically. A camera photographs each digit; a classifier reads it; the envelope goes down a chute.

The task Their audit team has found that one glyph is misread far more often than the others. They want every scanned digit that is a 5 pulled off the line and sent to a human verification desk.

Not “read the digit”. Detect one digit. Yes or no, one class against the other nine.

The first question is never technical

What does the organisation do with the output?

  • A flagged digit costs a person thirty seconds at a desk
  • A missed 5 costs a misdirected envelope, found weeks later, if at all
  • The two costs are not equal, and they are not paid by the same department

Hold on to that asymmetry. It is the whole of the next lecture, and we are about to choose a metric that cannot see it.

Say this line and move on quickly. It is a flag planted for the next lecture, not a hint — nobody can act on it yet.

The constraint the desk imposes

The verification desk is staffed for one shift at a time and can re-check about 1,000 items per shift.

A stakeholder figure, not a measurement We were told this. We did not measure it. Every number we derive from it inherits that status, and it goes on the slide labelled as an assumption — exactly as the experts’ 30% did in Application 1.

One shift is 10,000 scanned digits. Keep both numbers; they come back at the end of the next lecture as literal counts.

The fourth is the one that will age. Handwriting drifts, cameras are replaced, and a model trained on one decade’s post is not automatically valid on the next.

Classification, not regression. So RMSE is gone, and we need a new metric. That is the whole reason this application exists.

What makes this a rare-event problem

The ten digits appear in roughly equal numbers. The task does not.

$$\text{one class against nine} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{about one positive in ten}$$

Every binary detector carved out of a multiclass problem inherits this. Detect one product fault out of forty; one disease out of a hundred; one fraudulent transaction in ten thousand. The arithmetic is the same and only the ratio changes.

Ask for other examples from the room. You want them to own the shape of the problem, not the digits.

The data: MNIST

  • 70,000 small images of handwritten digits, written by US high-school students and Census Bureau employees
  • Each image is 28 × 28 pixels, flattened to 784 features
  • Each feature is one pixel’s intensity, an integer from 0 (white) to 255 (black)
  • Every image is labelled with the digit it shows
The textbook calls MNIST “the hello world of machine learning”. It is studied so often that it is effectively the field’s control experiment: if a method cannot do something here, the something is probably not the method’s fault.

Fetching it


from sklearn.datasets import fetch_openml

mnist = fetch_openml("mnist_784", as_frame=False)
X, y = mnist.data, mnist.target

print(X.shape, X.dtype)      # (70000, 784) float64
print(y.shape, y.dtype)      # (70000,)     <U1  — strings!

as_frame=False asks for NumPy arrays rather than a DataFrame. For 784 homogeneous numeric columns a DataFrame buys nothing and costs memory.

About 30 seconds the first time; scikit-learn caches the download afterwards. (Timing not examinable.)

The first default nobody asked for

y arrives as an array of one-character strings: '5', not 5.


y = y.astype(np.uint8)              # do this before anything else

assert y.dtype == np.uint8
assert set(np.unique(y)) == set(range(10))
What it costs if you forget y == 5 is then False for every single image, with no error and no warning — a string is never equal to an integer. You would build a detector for a class that has no members and score 100% accuracy on it.
This is reviewer question 5 — the default I did not ask for — arriving in the first three minutes of code. Name it as such.

Forty-eight of the seventy thousand

Drawn at random, and exactly 2 of the forty-eight are 5s. That ratio is the whole problem.

What one instance actually is


some_digit = X[0]
some_digit_image = some_digit.reshape(28, 28)

plt.imshow(some_digit_image, cmap="binary")

print(y[0])                 # 5

A 784-vector and a 28 × 28 image are the same object in two shapes. The model never sees the square; it sees 784 numbers in a fixed order, and it has no idea that pixel 0 and pixel 28 are vertical neighbours.

That last sentence is why convolutional networks exist. Chapter 12, and a long way off.

The split has already been made for us


X_train, X_test = X[:60000], X[60000:]
y_train, y_test = y[:60000], y[60000:]

assert len(X_train) == 60000 and len(X_test) == 10000
assert X_train.shape[1] == X_test.shape[1] == 784
  • The first 60,000 rows are the standard training set; the last 10,000 are the standard test set
  • The corpus is already shuffled, so the first 60,000 are not sorted by digit or by writer
  • Using the standard split is what makes any published number on MNIST comparable to ours

“Why not train_test_split?”

You could. You would then be reporting a number that nobody else has measured, on a benchmark whose entire value is that everybody measures the same thing.

But the shuffle still matters, one level down Some algorithms are sensitive to the order of the training instances, and cross-validation folds cut the training set into contiguous blocks unless you say otherwise. That is why every splitter in this course carries shuffle=True, random_state=42 — the same rule as in the previous lecture, for the same reason.

Shuffling is not always right. It is wrong for time series, where a random split puts a point’s own future in the training set. Lecture 20.

Two lines, and the imbalance is created. Nothing about MNIST is imbalanced; our question is.

Balanced data, imbalanced question

Every bar is roughly the same height. The moment we ask “is it a 5?”, one bar becomes the positive class and the other nine become the negative class.

Reviewer question 3, on image data

“What is the shape here?” bites harder on images than on tables, because a wrong shape still runs.

Part two

The metric

15 minutes · and a number in writing

Metric before model

Rule 1 of this course, and we are about to apply it for the second time.

  • Choose what you are optimising before you have anything to optimise
  • Otherwise the metric gets chosen after the result, which is how you end up reporting whichever number happens to look best

And name who chose it. If the metric arrived by default — from a library, from a habit, from an assistant — then nobody chose it.

The obvious metric for a classifier

Accuracy: the fraction of instances the classifier gets right.

$$\text{accuracy} \;=\; \frac{\#\{\,i \,:\, \hat y^{(i)} = y^{(i)}\,\}}{m}$$

The proportion of correct predictions

  • It is between 0 and 1, and bigger is better
  • It needs no threshold, no scaling and no interpretation
  • Every stakeholder in the world understands it without being taught

What it looks like in code


from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score

cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=3, shuffle=True, random_state=42)

scores = cross_val_score(clf, X_train, y_train_5,
                         cv=cv, scoring="accuracy")
print(scores)                    # one number per fold

"accuracy" is also what cross_val_score uses if you say nothing at all for a classifier. We are naming it anyway, because a metric you did not name is a metric you did not choose.

Why StratifiedKFold and not KFold

With 9% positives and three folds, a plain shuffle can hand one fold noticeably fewer 5s than another, and the fold scores then differ for a reason that has nothing to do with the model.

Stratified folds preserve the class proportion in every fold. This is the same idea as stratifying the split on income in Application 1 — and here it is not optional judgement, it is the class we are trying to detect.

For a classifier, cross_val_score(cv=3) already stratifies. We write it out because the default is invisible, and an invisible default is one you cannot check.

What number counts as success?

  • The line reads 10,000 digits per shift
  • The desk can re-check about 1,000 of them
  • The audit wants the 5s found
None of those three is an accuracy. Converting them into one requires an assumption we have not stated — so any target we set today is an order of magnitude, not a specification.

It is also not a baseline. A baseline is something you can run. Next slide.

Do not resolve the tension. They should feel that the translation is awkward without being told why yet.

Before you commit: what does nothing score?

The cheapest possible detector. It looks at nothing and answers “not a 5” every time.


from sklearn.base import BaseEstimator

class NeverFires(BaseEstimator):
    def fit(self, X, y=None):
        return self
    def predict(self, X):
        return np.zeros(len(X), dtype=bool)

cross_val_score(NeverFires(), X_train, y_train_5, cv=cv,
                scoring="accuracy")     # array([0.90965, 0.90965, 0.90965])

It is right 54,579 times out of 60,000 — 90.96% accuracy, with no model, no fit and no features.

The same anchor exists for all ten digits

Whichever digit the audit had picked, doing nothing scores between 88.76% and 90.96%. That is the number every result today has to be measured against.

How to read that

  • An accuracy of 90.96% is available for free, so 90.96% is zero in the units that matter
  • The interesting quantity is the distance above it, not the number itself
  • The anchor is different for every task — 88.76% for digit 1, because there are more 1s in the corpus than any other digit
Rule 2, applied to ourselves A metric with nothing to compare it to is decoration. You measured a constant predictor before committing an RMSE; measure a constant classifier before committing an accuracy.

The plan

  1. Pick an estimator that can handle 60,000 × 784
  2. Put it in a pipeline with the preprocessing
  3. Fit it, and predict one instance to see that it works
  4. Measure it with three-fold cross-validation
  5. Compare against the anchor, and record the result

Five steps and about forty minutes. The other twenty are spent on one prompt that goes wrong.

Stochastic gradient descent

SGDClassifier fits a linear model by taking one training instance at a time and nudging the parameters against the gradient of the loss for that instance.

  • It never needs the whole training set in memory at once — it is capable of online learning
  • It handles very large training sets efficiently, which is exactly our situation
  • It is a linear model, so it is fast, and it will not be the best thing available
The name describes the training procedure, not the model. The same class fits several different linear models depending on its loss argument; the default is a linear support vector machine.

Why start with the worst reasonable model

  • It runs in seconds, so the loop — specify, read, test, verify — can go round several times in one lecture
  • Every later model is measured against it, so it is the second anchor
  • If a linear model on raw pixels already answers the brief, the project is finished and nobody had to buy a GPU

Starting with the most powerful model available is how you end up unable to say which part of your system is doing the work.

Build it


from sklearn.linear_model import SGDClassifier
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

clf = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(),
                    SGDClassifier(random_state=42))

clf.fit(X_train, y_train_5)

Two components, one object. The standing constraint is satisfied by construction: when cross-validation holds out a fold, the scaler is refitted on the other folds only.

random_state=42 because SGD shuffles the training instances. Without it, two runs of the same code give different models — and you will spend an afternoon on the difference.

Scaling pixels — is it even necessary?

Every feature is already on the same scale, 0 to 255. So the usual argument for standardising does not obviously apply. We measure instead of arguing:

EstimatorFold 1Fold 2Fold 3Mean
Pipeline with StandardScaler96.83%96.84%97.04%96.90%
The same classifier, raw pixels96.69%91.63%96.79%95.03%

The means differ by less than two points. The spreads do not compare at all — one fold of the unscaled version lands five points below its own siblings.

This is why you report every fold

Report only the mean, and the unscaled row looks like a 95% classifier. Its worst fold is 91.63% — barely above doing nothing.

Ask what the second row's bad fold means. Gradient descent on unstandardised features takes a badly conditioned path; the answer depends on where it happened to start.

Predict one instance, before measuring anything


some_digit = X_train[0]              # the image we plotted earlier
print(y_train[0])                    # 5

clf.predict([some_digit])            # array([ True])

A test with a known answer, run before any metric exists. Step 4 of the working method, and the cheapest twenty seconds in the lecture.

Note the brackets predict expects a collection of instances, so a single 784-vector has to be wrapped. Passing some_digit bare raises, which is the good case — scikit-learn checks this one for you.

Now measure it, honestly


from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, cross_val_score

cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=3, shuffle=True, random_state=42)

scores = cross_val_score(clf, X_train, y_train_5,
                         cv=cv, scoring="accuracy")
print(scores)          # [0.9683  0.9684  0.97035]
print(scores.mean())   # 0.96902

About 30 seconds: three complete refits of scaler and classifier on 40,000 rows each. (Timing not examinable.)

The headline number

96.90%

Three-fold cross-validated accuracy, on data the model never saw during its own fitting.

Folds: 96.83%, 96.84%, 97.04%. A spread of about two tenths of a point — the three folds agree, which is more than could be said for anything in the previous application.

Against the anchor

DetectorCross-validated accuracy

Our detector is 5.94 percentage points above doing nothing, and 3.10 points below perfect. Of the mistakes the anchor makes, we have removed 65.7%.

What cross_val_score is doing


from sklearn.base import clone

for train_idx, test_idx in cv.split(X_train, y_train_5):
    fold_clf = clone(clf)                          # an unfitted copy
    fold_clf.fit(X_train[train_idx], y_train_5[train_idx])

    pred = fold_clf.predict(X_train[test_idx])
    n_correct = (pred == y_train_5[test_idx]).sum()
    print(n_correct / len(test_idx))

clone copies the hyperparameters and discards anything learned. Reusing clf here would train the same object three times in succession, and each fold would start from the previous fold’s parameters.

Write this loop once in your life. After that, use the library — but you will know what it did.

Beat 3, continued · measure the damage

Do not assert that it is wrong. Measure how wrong, and pair the measurements — the same fitted model scored twice, so the fold-to-fold variation cancels.


from sklearn.model_selection import cross_validate

r = cross_validate(clf, X_train, y_train_5, cv=cv,
                   scoring="accuracy", return_train_score=True)

print(r["train_score"] - r["test_score"])

Repeated over five seeds, so that fifteen paired measurements stand behind the number rather than one. About four minutes. (Timing not examinable.)

The same fitted model, scored twice

Fifteen out of fifteen pairs point the same way. The gap is 0.52 percentage points, with a standard deviation of 0.14.

Half a point. So why is it a bug?

  1. You did not know it was half a point until you measured. A tree in the previous lecture had a gap of 68,574 — and nothing in either piece of code said which case you were in
  2. It is this small for a specific reason: a linear model on 60,000 instances and 784 features has far too few parameters to memorise the training set. Give the same code an unconstrained tree and the gap is the whole number
  3. The sign is guaranteed and the size is not. Fifteen out of fifteen, because a model always fits the data it was fitted to at least as well as data it has not seen

The rule is procedural, not empirical: never score on the rows you fitted — not because the damage is always large, but because you cannot tell whether it is.

Beat 4 · the corrected specification

What to ask instead “Evaluate this classifier with stratified 3-fold cross-validation on the training set, seed 42. Report accuracy per fold and the mean. In the same table, report the same metric for a baseline that always predicts the negative class. Do not touch X_test.”

Four sentences. They name the estimator of generalisation error, the folds, the seed, the granularity of the report, the comparison, and the thing that must not be touched.

Notice what is not in it: the metric is still accuracy. We named it, so it is now ours — and being ours is what makes it something we can be held to.

Specimen exam question — Part A

A colleague reports that their detector for a rare defect achieves 99.2% accuracy on a held-out set, using correct cross-validation with no leakage.

1. State one further quantity you would need before you could say whether that is a good result, and why.
2. Their defect occurs in 0.9% of items. What accuracy does a detector that never fires achieve? Show the arithmetic.

Two marks each. Answer them before the next slide; the worked answers are at the end of the next lecture.

Specimen answer — 2

If 0.9% of items carry the defect, then 99.1% do not, and a detector that answers “no defect” to everything is right on all of them.

$$\text{accuracy}_{\text{never fires}} \;=\; 1 - 0.009 \;=\; 0.991$$

99.1% for a model that does not exist. The colleague’s 99.2% is one tenth of a point above it.

Part 1 is the harder half and we return to it. Any quantity that lets you locate 99.2% relative to something earns the marks; naming the base rate is the cleanest answer.

Do not develop this further today. It is deliberately sitting there, unanswered, for the next ninety minutes to pick up.

  1. A detection brief is a binary classification problem carved out of a multiclass one, and the carving is what creates the imbalance
  2. MNIST arrives with its split already made; the labels arrive as strings, and y == 5 is silently False until you fix that
  3. Accuracy is the obvious metric, and it is the one you get by default if you name nothing
  4. The trivial baseline — never fire — scores 90.96%, so the only meaningful part of any result is the distance above it
  5. An SGD classifier in a pipeline reaches 96.90%, cross-validated, with folds that agree to two tenths of a point
  6. Report every fold: the same classifier without scaling has a mean of 95.03% and a worst fold of 91.63%
  7. Scoring on the rows you fitted flatters the number by 0.52 points here, and by everything on a model that can memorise
