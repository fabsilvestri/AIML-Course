Time series

Two ideas from today, on daily series built to look like ridership: a level that drifts and a strong seven-day cycle. First, the baselines a model has to beat. Then, why a random split flatters a forecaster, and what a rolling origin does instead. Press Step to advance one unit and read what happened.

Every series on this page is synthetic, generated in your browser from a fixed seed, so Reset always gives back the same picture. None of it is the Chicago data.

1The baselines to beat

Fifty-two weeks of a daily series. The first 40 weeks train a linear model on the last $L$ days; the last 12 are forecast one day ahead, one day per Step. Three forecasts are made each day: copy yesterday, copy the same day last week, and the linear model. The middle plot keeps the running mean absolute error of each. On the right are the model's weights, one per lag.

The mathematics

Forecasting day $t+1$ from days up to $t$:

$$\hat y^{\text{naive}}_{t+1} = y_t,\qquad \hat y^{\text{seasonal}}_{t+1} = y_{t-6},$$ $$\hat y^{\text{linear}}_{t+1} = b + \sum_{k=0}^{L-1} w_k\, y_{t-k},$$

which is the deck's model with $L$ lags; $w_6 = 1$ and every other weight 0 is the seasonal naive forecast. The weights minimise the squared error over the training days. They solve the normal equations $\mathbf X^{\intercal}\mathbf X\,\boldsymbol\theta = \mathbf X^{\intercal}\mathbf y$, with one row per training day and the same 252 training days for every $L$. The score is $\mathrm{MAE} = \tfrac1m\sum_i|\hat y_i - y_i|$ over the test days.

The series is $y_t = \ell_t + c_t + \varepsilon_t$, with $\ell_t$ a slow random walk ($\ell_t = \ell_{t-1} + \mathcal N(0, 0.25^2)$), a fixed weekly pattern $c_t$, and noise $\mathcal N(0, 0.6^2)$.

Watch for
  • Copying yesterday is nearly twice as bad as copying last week. It misses badly every Saturday and every Monday, where the weekly cycle jumps.
  • The weights at lags 7, 14, 21 and 28 are all positive, and the lag-1 weight is the largest of the rest. The model has found the week and the recent level, and nobody told it either.
  • The weights sum to a little under 1, so the forecast is pulled slightly towards the training mean.
Try this
  1. Set $L = 1$. The model can only rescale yesterday, and it does no better than copying it, far behind copying last week.
  2. Set $L = 7$. Lag 7 is now available: the model gets about 8% ahead of copying last week. At $L = 28$ it is about 15% ahead.

2Rolling origin against a random split

The same model scored two ways, on 68 weeks of a daily series. In each of eleven folds, a model is fitted on 140 rows and scored on 28. Rolling origin trains on the 140 days just before the origin and forecasts the 28 after it. A random split draws both sets at random from the whole record. Each Step is one fold. Every row's error is divided by the error of copying last week on the same rows, which cancels how hard those days happen to be.

The mathematics

A row is a window: features $y_{t-13}, \dots, y_t$ and target $y_{t+1}$. The linear model is the previous widget's with $L = 14$. The nearest-windows model ($k$-NN, $k = 5$) finds the 5 training windows closest to the test window in Euclidean distance, and predicts the mean of their targets. It can only interpolate between rows it has seen.

The skill ratio of a protocol is

$$\frac{\sum_{\text{folds}}\sum_{i\in\text{test}}|\hat y_i - y_i|}{\sum_{\text{folds}}\sum_{i\in\text{test}}|y_{i-7} - y_i|},$$

model error over seasonal-naive error on the identical rows: the deck's control for how hard the scored rows are. Both protocols also use the same training size and the same test size, as the deck's controlled comparison does.

The series is a slow random walk plus a weekly pattern plus noise that is itself autocorrelated: $u_t = 0.5\,u_{t-1} + \mathcal N(0, 0.5^2)$.

Watch for
  • Under the random split, red training rows lie after test rows: in about half of all (training, test) pairs the training row is the later one. Under the rolling origin, none do.
  • With nearest windows, the random split reports the model about 17% better than copying last week; the rolling origin finds it about 4% worse. The random split lets it copy the neighbours of the test day, from both sides.
  • The linear model's two ratios are much closer: a model with 15 parameters cannot memorise neighbours. On this series the random split still flatters it a little. On other seeds of the same generator the gap is small and goes either way.
Try this
  1. Play both protocols to the end with nearest windows, and compare the two dashed lines. Then switch the model to linear.
  2. Press Another series a few times. The nearest-windows model is flattered by the random split on every one; the linear model's gap changes sign. The deck's 9% for a linear model was measured on real data, whose relationships drift over the years.
