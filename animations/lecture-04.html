1Gradient descent on the cost surface

A straight line $\hat y = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x$ fitted by descent. On the left, the data and the current line; on the right, the cost $J(\theta_0, \theta_1)$ as a contour map, with every step the algorithm has taken. The green cross is the normal-equation answer $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$ — where descent is heading.

The mathematics

The cost is the mean squared error, and its gradient is

$$\nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta) = \frac{2}{m}\,\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\bigl(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol\theta - y\bigr).$$

Each step moves against it: $\boldsymbol\theta^{(k+1)} = \boldsymbol\theta^{(k)} - \eta\,\nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta^{(k)})$. It converges exactly when $0 < \eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}$, where $\lambda_{\max}$ is the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian $\tfrac{2}{m}\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X}$ — the side panel computes it.

Watch for
  • Each step crosses the contour lines at right angles: the gradient is perpendicular to the level set it starts on.
  • With raw $x$ the bowl is a long thin valley, and descent zig-zags across it. Standardising $x$ makes it round, and the same $\eta$ walks straight in.
  • Stochastic and mini-batch steps are noisy: each one uses only the highlighted points, so it is a guess at the true gradient.
Try this
  1. Push $\eta$ just past $2/\lambda_{\max}$ and press Play. The cost curve turns upward and the path spirals out.
  2. Set $\eta$ near $1/\lambda_{\max}$: the fastest it gets.
  3. Switch to stochastic with a large $\eta$, then lower $\eta$: the cloud around $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$ shrinks with it.

2Logistic regression

Two classes in the plane, and a model that outputs a probability $\hat p = \sigma(\boldsymbol\theta^{\intercal}\mathbf{x})$. The shading is $\hat p$ itself; the solid line is $\hat p = 0.5$, the dashed ones $\hat p = 0.1$ and $0.9$. On the right, every point sits on the logistic curve at its own score $t = \boldsymbol\theta^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}$. Click the plot to add points.

The mathematics

Each step of descent lowers the log loss:

$$\begin{aligned}J(\boldsymbol\theta) = -\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i}\Big[&y^{(i)}\log\hat p^{(i)} \\ &+ (1-y^{(i)})\log(1-\hat p^{(i)})\Big].\end{aligned}$$
Watch for
  • Points far on the wrong side of the line sit at the flat end of the curve but carry the largest loss — their term $-\log\hat p$ grows without limit.
  • The boundary $\hat p = 0.5$ is always a straight line: it is $\boldsymbol\theta^{\intercal}\mathbf{x} = 0$.
Try this
  1. Add one gold triangle deep inside the blue cloud and watch how far it pulls the boundary — then compare a point just over the line.

3Softmax regression

Three classes. Each class $k$ has its own score $t_k = \boldsymbol\theta_k^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}$, and softmax turns the three scores into three probabilities that sum to one. The colour is the class with the highest probability, and fades where the model is unsure. Click anywhere on the plot to probe it: the bars show the scores and the probabilities at that point.

The mathematics $$\hat p_k = \frac{e^{t_k}}{\sum_{j} e^{t_j}},\qquad J = -\frac{1}{m}\sum_i\sum_k y_k^{(i)}\log\hat p_k^{(i)}$$

with $y^{(i)}$ one-hot: the log of the probability given to the true class, averaged.

Watch for
  • Each boundary between two regions is a straight line, the set where $t_j = t_k$. Three classes give at most three such lines, meeting at a point.
  • Adding the same number to all three scores changes nothing: softmax only sees their differences.
Try this
  1. Probe a point exactly where the three regions meet: three bars of about $1/3$.
  2. Probe a point far out in one region: one probability near 1 even though the scores differ by only a few units — softmax is exponential.
