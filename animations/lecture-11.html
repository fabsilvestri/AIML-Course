Training deep networks

Two widgets for today's thread. The first races five optimisers on the same surface. The second shows why a learning rate has to come down at the end of a noisy run.

All data on this page is synthetic, generated in your browser from a fixed seed, so Reset always gives back the same picture.

  • 1 · Five optimisers on one surface
  • 2 · Learning-rate schedules and the noise floor

1Five optimisers on one surface

Five optimisers start from the same point on the same cost $J(\boldsymbol\theta)$, and each step advances all five by one iteration. The default surface is a long, narrow valley, 75 times more curved across than along. The cross marks the minimum. Below the plot is $J$ against iteration, on a log scale. SGD, momentum and Nesterov share one learning rate. RMSProp and Adam have their own, as in the lecture's comparison.

The mathematics

With $g = \nabla J(\boldsymbol\theta)$, $\beta = 0.9$, and products and square roots taken coordinate by coordinate:

  • SGD: $\boldsymbol\theta \leftarrow \boldsymbol\theta - \eta\, g$
  • Momentum: $\mathbf v \leftarrow \beta\mathbf v - \eta\, g$, then $\boldsymbol\theta \leftarrow \boldsymbol\theta + \mathbf v$
  • Nesterov: the same, with $g$ evaluated at $\boldsymbol\theta + \beta\mathbf v$
  • RMSProp: $\mathbf s \leftarrow \beta\mathbf s + (1-\beta)\, g^2$, then $\boldsymbol\theta \leftarrow \boldsymbol\theta - \eta\, g/(\sqrt{\mathbf s} + \varepsilon)$
  • Adam: $\mathbf m \leftarrow \beta_1\mathbf m + (1-\beta_1) g$ and $\mathbf s \leftarrow \beta_2\mathbf s + (1-\beta_2) g^2$, divided by $1-\beta_1^t$ and $1-\beta_2^t$ to remove the bias of starting at zero, then $\boldsymbol\theta \leftarrow \boldsymbol\theta - \eta\,\hat{\mathbf m}/(\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf s}}+\varepsilon)$, with $\beta_1 = 0.9$ and $\beta_2 = 0.999$.

On the valley $J = \tfrac12(0.02\,\theta_1^2 + 1.5\,\theta_2^2)$ the Hessian's eigenvalues are $\lambda_{\min} = 0.02$ and $\lambda_{\max} = 1.5$. SGD multiplies the error along each eigenvector by $1 - \eta\lambda$, so it is stable only for $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max} = 1.33$. The same analysis of the two-term recursions gives $\eta\lambda_{\max} < 2(1+\beta)$ for momentum and $\eta\lambda_{\max} < 2(1+\beta)/(1+2\beta)$ for Nesterov. AdamW differs from Adam only through weight decay, and this surface has none, so it is not drawn separately.

Watch for
  • SGD crosses the valley quickly and then crawls along its floor. Each step keeps a fraction $1 - 0.02\eta = 0.984$ of the distance left along $\theta_1$, and it needs about 200 iterations to reach $J < 10^{-3}$.
  • Momentum builds up speed along the floor, overshoots the minimum and swings back, and still arrives about five times sooner. Nesterov looks ahead before it steps, so it brakes earlier and overshoots less.
  • The adaptive methods divide each coordinate by its own gradient scale, so their first steps are diagonal: equal moves in $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, whatever the slope. With a constant $\eta$, RMSProp never settles. It ends rattling between $\pm\eta/2$ in each coordinate, about $\pm 0.1$ here. Adam's $\mathbf s$ still remembers the large early gradients, so its late steps shrink and it converges.
Try this
  1. Raise $\eta$ to 1.0. Nesterov diverges, because 1.0 is above its bound of 0.905, while SGD and momentum still converge. Past 1.33 SGD diverges too, slowly at first, because just above the bound $|1-\eta\lambda_{\max}|$ is barely more than 1. Momentum survives until 2.53.
  2. Halve the adaptive rate. RMSProp's rattle halves with it, which is how you can tell the rattle comes from $\eta$ and not from the surface.
  3. Switch to the curved valley. Its floor bends, so the best direction keeps changing. Watch which methods follow the bend and which cut across it.

2Learning-rate schedules and the noise floor

Thirty independent SGD runs on the bowl $J = \tfrac12(0.5\,\theta_1^2 + 2\,\theta_2^2)$. Each run's gradient carries noise, as a mini-batch gradient does. On the left, each run's current $\boldsymbol\theta$: blue for the chosen schedule, gold triangles for a constant rate. Both clouds use the same noise. On the right, the schedule itself. Below, the cost averaged over the thirty runs, with the floor a constant rate cannot go below.

The mathematics

Along a direction with curvature $\lambda$, a noisy step is $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta(\lambda\theta_t + \sigma\xi_t)$ with $\xi_t \sim N(0,1)$ independent of $\theta_t$. Since $\operatorname{Var}(aX + bY) = a^2\operatorname{Var}X + b^2\operatorname{Var}Y$ for independent $X, Y$,

$$\operatorname{Var}\theta_{t+1} = (1-\eta\lambda)^2\operatorname{Var}\theta_t + \eta^2\sigma^2.$$

With $\eta$ held fixed, this settles where both sides are equal, at $\eta\sigma^2/\bigl(\lambda(2-\eta\lambda)\bigr)$. The expected cost then stops falling at $\tfrac12\lambda\operatorname{Var}\theta = \eta\sigma^2/\bigl(2(2-\eta\lambda)\bigr)$ for each direction. For small $\eta$ this floor is nearly proportional to $\eta$, and only a smaller $\eta$ lowers it. Here $\sigma = 1$, and the schedules over a run of $T$ steps are:

  • exponential: $\eta_t = \eta_0\cdot 0.01^{\,t/T}$, a fixed factor per step
  • cosine: $\eta_t = \tfrac12\eta_0\bigl(1 + \cos(\pi t/T)\bigr)$
  • 1-cycle: a half-cosine rise from $\eta_0/25$ to $\eta_0$ over the first 30% of the run, then a half-cosine fall to $\eta_0/(25\cdot 10^4)$. These are PyTorch's OneCycleLR defaults.
Watch for
  • The constant-rate cloud stops shrinking. Its average cost levels off at the dashed floor, about 0.28 for $\eta_0 = 0.4$, and fluctuates around it for the rest of the run.
  • As the schedule's $\eta_t$ falls, the blue cloud contracts and its cost drops well below the floor. By the end of a cosine run it is about ten times lower.
  • 1-cycle starts with a small rate, peaks at $\eta_0$ 30% of the way through, and is then no better than the constant rate: at that point its cloud is as wide as the gold one. Only the fall that follows takes it below the floor.
Try this
  1. Lower $\eta_0$ to 0.2. The floor falls from 0.278 to 0.115, but the constant run takes longer to reach it: a constant rate trades speed against precision. Raise $\eta_0$ to 0.8 and the floor jumps to 1.25, more than four times higher, because the factor $1/(2-\eta\lambda)$ grows quickly as $\eta$ nears the stability limit $2/\lambda_{\max} = 1$.
  2. Change the run length $T$ and look at the right-hand plot. Every schedule is drawn against $t/T$, so a different epoch count gives a different schedule. This is why each one has to be told total_steps.
