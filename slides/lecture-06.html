Mathematical thread 3

The bias–variance
decomposition

20 minutes

The question

Your held-out error is 0.468 and your training error is 0.395.

Where does the difference come from — and how far down could it ever go?

Why you need the answer The two questions have different repairs. Attack the wrong one and you will spend an afternoon making the model worse.

First: what is random here?

Not the passenger. Fix one passenger $x$ and ask about them repeatedly.

  • The training set $D$ is random — you happened to get these 712 rows out of the 891
  • The label $y$ of the passenger is random — two people with identical recorded values did not both survive

Everything in this thread is an expectation over those two sources, and over nothing else. The model is deterministic given $D$.

This slide is where the room splits. Ask explicitly: "when I write E, what am I averaging over?" Someone will say "the test set". That is the answer to a different question.

Three numbers for one passenger

SymbolMeaningObserved?
$\hat{p}_D(x)$what your model predicts, having seen $D$yes
$\bar{p}(x) = \mathbb{E}_D[\hat{p}_D(x)]$the average prediction over all training sets you could have drawnno — estimated
$p^{*}(x)$the true probability that this passenger survivesnever

$y \in \{0, 1\}$ is what actually happened. It is never $p^{*}$, and that gap is the third term we are about to derive.

The quantity to decompose

$$\mathbb{E}_{D,\,y}\!\left[\left(y - \hat{p}_D(x)\right)^{2}\right]$$

Expected squared error at one passenger

Averaged over passengers this is the Brier score you already report. We decompose it at a single $x$ and then average — the decomposition is linear, so nothing is lost.

Step 1 · Add and subtract the average prediction

Condition on $y$ for a moment and average over $D$ only:

$$\left(y - \hat{p}_D\right)^{2} = \left(\underbrace{y - \bar{p}}_{\text{does not depend on } D} + \underbrace{\bar{p} - \hat{p}_D}_{\text{mean } 0}\right)^{2}$$

Expand the square. The cross term is $2\,(y - \bar{p})\,\mathbb{E}_D[\bar{p} - \hat{p}_D] = 0$, because $\bar{p}$ is defined as $\mathbb{E}_D[\hat{p}_D]$.

Step 2 · Two terms, exactly

$$\mathbb{E}_{D}\!\left[\left(y - \hat{p}_D\right)^{2}\right] = \left(y - \bar{p}\right)^{2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{D}\!\left[\left(\hat{p}_D - \bar{p}\right)^{2}\right]}_{\textbf{variance}}$$

The second term is the variance of your predictor. It says how much the answer moves when the 712 rows move — and it does not mention the truth at all.

This step is an identity, not an approximation. We check it numerically later in this lecture, and the residual is zero to machine precision.

Step 3 · Now average over the label as well

Repeat the trick on $(y - \bar{p})^{2}$, this time inserting $p^{*}$:

$$\mathbb{E}_{y}\!\left[\left(y - \bar{p}\right)^{2}\right] = \underbrace{\left(p^{*} - \bar{p}\right)^{2}}_{\textbf{squared bias}} + \underbrace{p^{*}\!\left(1 - p^{*}\right)}_{\textbf{noise}}$$

Same cancellation: $\mathbb{E}_y[y] = p^{*}$, so the cross term vanishes again. And $\operatorname{Var}(y) = p^{*}(1-p^{*})$ because $y$ is Bernoulli.

The decomposition

$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(y - \hat{p}_D\right)^{2}\right] \;=\; \left(p^{*} - \bar{p}\right)^{2} \;+\; \mathbb{E}_{D}\!\left[\left(\hat{p}_D - \bar{p}\right)^{2}\right] \;+\; p^{*}\!\left(1 - p^{*}\right)$$
TermMeansCured by
squared biaswrong on average — the model family cannot reach the truthmore capacity
varianceunstable — a different sample gives a different answerless capacity, or more data
noisethe label is not a function of the recorded columnsnothing

The two repairs point in opposite directions. That is the whole difficulty.

The whole thread, in one picture

Bias is the distance from the centre of the spread to the truth. Variance is the width of the spread. Neither is visible from one training set.

An honest caveat before we measure anything

The identity above is exact for squared error. Your committed metric is log loss, and log loss does not decompose into three additive terms like this.

What we do about it The decomposition is measured on the Brier score — which the previous lecture already reported alongside log loss, and which is the squared error of a probability. The shape of the answer transfers; the numbers are Brier numbers and are labelled as such.

Saying “bias–variance” about a log loss curve is common and slightly wrong. Doing it knowingly, and saying which metric you decomposed, is the difference.

Start with the term you cannot fix

$p^{*}$ is never observed — but where several passengers share exactly the same recorded values, every model of those columns must give them the same number. Whatever their outcomes do inside that cell is a floor.


keys = ["Sex", "Pclass", "AgeBand", "FamBand", "Embarked"]
cells = full.groupby(keys, observed=True)["Survived"].agg(["sum", "count"])

# k(m-k)/(m(m-1)) is the unbiased estimator of p(1-p) from m Bernoulli draws
multi = cells[cells["count"] >= 2]
var   = multi["sum"] * (multi["count"] - multi["sum"]) / (
        multi["count"] * (multi["count"] - 1))
noise = (var * multi["count"]).sum() / multi["count"].sum()

133 distinct cells; 102 of them contain at least two passengers, covering 858 people.

Identical inputs, different outcomes

62 third-class men aged 26 to 40, travelling alone from Southampton, with every recorded value identical. 13 of them survived. No model of these columns can separate them.

The floor

0.121

Brier score. Measured, not assumed.

56 of the 102 shared cells are mixed — 657 passengers sit in a cell where the outcome went both ways. That is 74% of the training set living inside a cell whose answer cannot be certain.

It is an under-estimate of the true noise: two passengers in the same cell may still differ in ways the Titanic’s manifest never recorded. The real floor is at least this high.

Now put it back on the two curves you drew

The training curve

Falls forever. It is measured on the rows that were fitted, so it sees no variance term at all — the model was tuned to those exact rows.

The held-out curve

Turns at degree 2 and climbs. It is the sum of all three terms, and from degree 3 the variance term dominates.

The identification, stated once The vertical gap between your two curves is the variance term. Degree 1: gap 0.073. Degree 5: gap 1.672. The gap grew by a factor of 23 while the model family stayed the same size in every other respect.

Two shapes, two diagnoses

  • Both curves plateau high and together → bias. The model is too rigid; more data changes nothing
  • A gap that is still open at the last point → variance. More data would help, and you may not be able to get any

These are the only two shapes, and telling them apart takes ten seconds once you know what you are looking at.

The two shapes, drawn

The repairs are written on the diagram because they are opposites. Applying the left-hand repair to a right-hand curve is the most common wasted afternoon in applied machine learning.

Why it is called a trade-off

Increasing a model’s complexity typically increases its variance and reduces its bias. Reducing complexity does the reverse.

$$\text{total} \;=\; \underbrace{\text{bias}^{2}}_{\downarrow\ \text{with capacity}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{variance}}_{\uparrow\ \text{with capacity}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{noise}}_{\text{fixed}}$$

You are minimising a sum of two terms that move in opposite directions. The minimum is interior, and on this dataset it sits at degree 1 or 2 — which is exactly where your held-out curve turned.

The word “trade-off” is doing real work. There is no setting that is good at both.

Thread 3 · Summary

  • Expected squared error at a point splits exactly into squared bias, variance, and $p^{*}(1-p^{*})$
  • The expectation is over the training set and the label, not over test points
  • Bias is being wrong on average; variance is being unstable; noise is neither and cannot be reduced
  • The gap between a training curve and a held-out curve is the variance term made visible
  • Curves plateauing high together mean bias; a gap still open at the last point means variance
  • On this dataset the floor is 0.121 Brier, and the degree-1 model is already within 0.01 of it in squared bias

How is that even possible?

Accuracy at degree 5 was 75.4% — poor, but not absurd. Log loss went to 1.957. The two metrics disagree because they measure different things.

$$-\log(0.01) \;\approx\; 4.6 \qquad\text{one confident mistake}$$

A single passenger given probability 0.01 who then survives contributes 4.6 to the average, on its own. Accuracy records that passenger as one error out of 179 and moves on.

Log loss is unbounded above. That is not a defect — it is the metric telling you that confident-and-wrong is a different failure from wrong.

Why the minimiser does not exist

Suppose that in the expanded feature space some vector $\boldsymbol\theta$ separates the two classes perfectly: every survivor has $\boldsymbol\theta^{\intercal}\mathbf{x} > 0$ and every casualty has $\boldsymbol\theta^{\intercal}\mathbf{x} < 0$.

$$\sigma\!\left(c\,\boldsymbol\theta^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}\right) \;\longrightarrow\; \begin{cases}1 & \text{if } \boldsymbol\theta^{\intercal}\mathbf{x} > 0\\ 0 & \text{if } \boldsymbol\theta^{\intercal}\mathbf{x} < 0\end{cases} \qquad \text{as } c \to \infty$$

So the training log loss falls monotonically towards zero as $\lVert\boldsymbol\theta\rVert \to \infty$. The infimum is never attained. There is no minimum to find.

143 columns and 712 rows makes separation easy. lbfgs runs out of iterations because we told it to stop, not because it arrived.

What Thread 1 said would happen

Lecture 2, on the normal equation “If one column is a linear combination of others, the columns are dependent and $\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X}$ is singular.” And: “when creating new combined features, avoid simple weighted sums of existing features.”

A weighted sum with weights $1, 1$ and an intercept is still a weighted sum. The singularity was manufactured by hand, in a feature engineering cell, and it looked like good practice at the time.

Look at the spectrum

Standardise the five numeric columns and form $\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X}$. Its eigenvalues:


>>> np.linalg.eigvalsh(Z.T @ Z)
array([1.81e-12, 4.29e+02, 5.43e+02, 7.90e+02, 1.80e+03])

The smallest eigenvalue is $1.8\times10^{-12}$ — zero, to the precision the arithmetic allows. Four columns of information in five columns of data.

Condition number: about $9\times10^{14}$. A double has about $10^{16}$ of relative precision, so roughly two significant digits survive the solve.

What adding $\alpha\mathbf{I}$ does to that spectrum

If $\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$, then

$$\left(\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X} + \alpha\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{v} \;=\; \left(\lambda + \alpha\right)\mathbf{v}$$
  • Same eigenvectors; every eigenvalue moved up by exactly $\alpha$
  • $\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X}$ is positive semi-definite, so $\lambda \ge 0$, so $\lambda + \alpha \ge \alpha > 0$
  • No eigenvalue is zero ⇒ the matrix is invertible for every $\alpha > 0$
The callback, stated outright Lecture 2 promised that ridge regression makes $\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X}$ invertible for every $\alpha > 0$. That is the promise, and this is its two-line proof.

And it bounds the conditioning

$$\kappa\!\left(\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X} + \alpha\mathbf{I}\right) \;=\; \frac{\lambda_{\max} + \alpha}{\lambda_{\min} + \alpha} \;\le\; \frac{\lambda_{\max} + \alpha}{\alpha}$$

The bound on the right does not mention $\lambda_{\min}$ at all. Whatever the data do — exact collinearity included — the conditioning is controlled by a number you choose.

At $\alpha = 1$ the condition number of our singular matrix falls from $9\times10^{14}$ to 1798.

Condition number against $\alpha$

A straight line of slope $-1$ over twelve decades: in this regime the condition number is simply $\lambda_{\max}/\alpha$. The dependence the data has is not repaired — it is dominated.

The idea, in one sentence

Stop asking for the best fit. Ask for the best fit among small models.

$$J(\boldsymbol\theta) \;=\; \underbrace{L(\boldsymbol\theta)}_{\text{fit the data}} \;+\; \underbrace{\alpha\,\Omega(\boldsymbol\theta)}_{\text{stay small}}$$

$\Omega$ penalises the size of the weights. $\alpha$ says how much you care. Setting $\alpha = 0$ recovers exactly what you did in the previous lecture.

Constraining a model is one of the three cures for variance, and the only one available when the dataset is fixed at 891 rows.

Fix 1 · Ridge — the $\ell_2$ penalty

$$J(\boldsymbol\theta) \;=\; \mathrm{MSE}(\boldsymbol\theta) \;+\; \frac{\alpha}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_j^{2}$$
  • The sum starts at $j = 1$: the bias term $\theta_0$ is not penalised. Shrinking the intercept would shift every prediction towards zero rather than towards the mean
  • Equivalently, $\Omega(\boldsymbol\theta) = \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_2^2$ where $\mathbf{w}$ is $\boldsymbol\theta$ without the bias

Also called Tikhonov regularisation. The book gives both names.

Ridge has a closed form too

Differentiate and set to zero, exactly as in Thread 1:

$$\hat{\boldsymbol\theta} \;=\; \left(\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X} + \alpha\mathbf{A}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\,y$$

$\mathbf{A}$ is the identity with a zero in the top-left cell — the identity, minus the row and column belonging to the bias term.

This is Thread 1’s failure condition, closed The normal equation could fail to be solvable in two ways: $m < n$, or collinear columns. Adding $\alpha\mathbf{A}$ removes both, for every $\alpha > 0$. Ridge is not only a bias–variance device; it is what makes the linear algebra work.

The same term, on our loss

$$J(\boldsymbol\theta) \;=\; \underbrace{-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i} \left[y^{(i)}\log \hat{p}^{(i)} + (1-y^{(i)})\log(1-\hat{p}^{(i)})\right]}_{\text{log loss, from the previous lecture}} \;+\; \frac{\alpha}{m}\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_2^{2}$$

Now the objective is coercive: as $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert \to \infty$ the penalty $\to \infty$ while the log loss is bounded below by zero. A minimiser exists, and strict convexity makes it unique.

Both of Diagnosis 2 and Diagnosis 3 are gone, and we have not looked at the data once.

A trap in the API

Scikit-Learn’s LogisticRegression does not take $\alpha$. It takes C.

$$C \;=\; \frac{1}{\alpha}$$
  • Small C → large $\alpha$ → strong regularisation
  • Large C → weak regularisation; C=1e6 is what we used to switch it off
  • The default is C=1.0, and penalty="l2". Logistic regression in Scikit-Learn is regularised unless you say otherwise

Reviewer question five: what is the default I did not ask for? Here it was regularisation, and the previous lecture turned it off on purpose to have something to repair.

Ridge requires scaling. Not as a nicety

The penalty $\sum \theta_j^2$ treats every weight identically. If Fare is in pounds and SibSp is a count of people, then a “weight of 1” means two unrelated things and the penalty falls unequally on them.

Rescale a column by $c$ and its fitted weight scales by $1/c$ — so its contribution to the penalty scales by $1/c^{2}$. Measuring fares in pence instead of pounds changes which coefficients ridge decides to shrink.

We already have StandardScaler inside the pipeline, fitted per fold. Regularisation is the reason it is not optional.

Fix 2 · Lasso — the $\ell_1$ penalty

$$J(\boldsymbol\theta) \;=\; \mathrm{MSE}(\boldsymbol\theta) \;+\; 2\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|\theta_j\right|$$

Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator. The word selection is the point: lasso drives weights to exactly zero, so it performs feature selection as a side effect of fitting.

Ridge shrinks everything and eliminates nothing.

Why $\ell_1$ produces exact zeros and $\ell_2$ does not

Look at the gradient of the penalty near $\theta_j = 0$.

Ridge

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_j}\,\theta_j^{2} = 2\theta_j \;\to\; 0$$

The push towards zero fades as you approach zero. You get there only in the limit.

Lasso

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_j}\,\left|\theta_j\right| = \pm 1$$

The push has constant size right up to zero. If the data pulls with less than $\alpha$, the weight arrives at zero and stops.

At $\theta_j = 0$ the absolute value is not differentiable; the subgradient is the whole interval $[-1, 1]$, and any data pull inside that interval is held at exactly zero.

Fix 3 · Elastic net — both at once

$$J(\boldsymbol\theta) \;=\; \mathrm{MSE}(\boldsymbol\theta) \;+\; r\left(2\alpha\sum_j\left|\theta_j\right|\right) \;+\; (1-r)\left(\frac{\alpha}{m}\sum_j\theta_j^{2}\right)$$

$r$ is the mix — l1_ratio in Scikit-Learn. $r = 0$ is ridge, $r = 1$ is lasso.

The book’s advice: prefer some regularisation to none, so avoid plain linear regression. Ridge is a good default. If you suspect only a few features matter, prefer lasso or elastic net — and prefer elastic net to lasso when features are strongly correlated or outnumber the instances, because lasso can behave erratically there.

Both conditions hold for us: 143 columns from 712 rows, and $\text{Age}$ and $\text{Age}^2$ are about as correlated as two columns get.

Resist the urge to soften this. The honest result is the interesting one: regularisation is a repair, not an upgrade, and choosing the right capacity in the first place beats penalising the wrong one.

So what is regularisation actually for?

  • It makes the problem well-posed. A unique minimiser exists for every $\alpha > 0$, whatever the columns do
  • It makes capacity a continuous dial. Degree is an integer; $\alpha$ is not, so you can sit between two degrees
  • It is insurance. When you cannot know the right capacity in advance — which is usually — it bounds the damage
  • It selects. Lasso’s 32 columns are a hypothesis about which features matter, generated by the fit

What it is not is a way to make an over-parameterised model beat a well-chosen one on a dataset this small.

Fix 4 · Early stopping

A different way to keep the weights small: fit iteratively, and stop before the optimiser gets where it is going.

  • Gradient descent starts at $\boldsymbol\theta = \mathbf{0}$ — the smallest possible model
  • Each epoch moves the weights outward, fitting more of the training set
  • Held-out error falls, reaches a minimum, and then rises

Géron calls it a “beautiful free lunch”: no penalty term, no extra hyperparameter in the objective — just a stopping rule.

    clf.fit(Z_train, y_a)                 # warm_start: resume, do not restart
    train.append(log_loss(y_a, clf.predict_proba(Z_train)[:, 1], labels=[0, 1]))
    valid.append(log_loss(y_b, clf.predict_proba(Z_valid)[:, 1], labels=[0, 1]))

warm_start=True with max_iter=1 is what makes each call one epoch of the same fit. penalty=None is deliberate: we want early stopping to be the only regularisation in the room.

The transform is fitted on the training part only, outside the loop, and applied to both. Refitting it inside the loop would be the previous application’s leak, 500 times over.

The point where the two curves disagree

Epoch 105 is the best model this run ever holds. Running to epoch 500 costs 1.274 of log loss and never announces it.

Reading it honestly

  • These are bad numbers in absolute terms. Plain SGD at a constant learning rate on 143 correlated columns is a poor optimiser, and we are showing the shape, not a competitive model
  • The training curve is noisy — each epoch shuffles — so “stop at the minimum” needs a patience rule in practice, not a single-epoch comparison

The stopping epoch is chosen by looking at held-out data. It is a hyperparameter like any other, and it must be chosen on validation rows, never on the test set.

Four repairs, one idea

RepairKeeps $\lVert\boldsymbol\theta\rVert$ small byZeros?

Lassopenalising $\sum\left|\theta_j\right|$yes

All four reduce variance by reducing the set of models the fit is allowed to reach. All four increase bias. Which is why all four need $\alpha$ — or an epoch count — chosen by cross-validation.

The assistant, asked badly

“Find the best value of C for my logistic regression and tell me how well it does.”

Every word is reasonable. Nothing in it says on what data the best C is to be found, or on what data “how well it does” is to be measured.

This is the failure this lecture is about: choosing a hyperparameter is choosing a model, and it consumes whatever data it looks at.

What it returns — and it runs


best_C, best_score = None, np.inf

for C in np.logspace(-4, 4, 17):

No exception, no warning, a plausible number, and a variable named best_score that a reader will quote in a report.

The review question that catches it

Reviewer question 1 What touched the test set?

Answer: seventeen models did, and then we reported the score of whichever one it liked best. best_score is a minimum over seventeen noisy estimates, and the minimum of noisy estimates is biased downward even if every estimate is individually unbiased.

The code never writes to the test set. It reads it eighteen times, and reading is enough.

Measure the damage — do not assert it

20 seeds. For each: split, run the loop above, and also run the honest version that picks C by 5-fold cross-validation inside the training part. Score both on the same held-out rows.

Reported numberLog lossSpread over 20 seeds
Chosen on the test set, scored on the test set0.4510.049
Chosen by cross-validation, scored on the test set0.4700.045

Optimism: 0.019 of log loss, about 4.1%. On 13 seeds of 20 the dishonest number is the flattering one, and the two procedures picked the same C on 7 seeds of 20.

Read that result carefully — both halves

It is small.

0.019 against a fold spread of 0.045. On a single split you would never see it. Seventeen candidates is a small search.

It is real, and it is one-sided.

13 seeds of 20 flattered. It never averages away, and it grows with the number of candidates you try.

The decision rule Small search, small bias. Search a thousand configurations — which a randomised search does in an afternoon — and the same procedure will hand you a number that is confidently wrong. The size of the lie scales with how hard you looked.

Reporting “the effect is small here” is the lesson only if you also say why it is small here.

The specification that would have prevented it

Say this instead “Choose C by 10-fold cross-validation on the training set only, using GridSearchCV over a pipeline. Refit the winner on the full training set. Then evaluate once on the test set and report both numbers separately, with the fold spread.” 

gs = GridSearchCV(pipeline(degree=3), {"clf__C": np.logspace(-4, 4, 17)},
                  scoring="neg_log_loss", cv=cv).fit(X_train, y_train)

print(-gs.best_score_)                                    # the honest estimate
print(log_loss(y_test, gs.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]))   # the test set, once

Same seventeen candidates, same runtime, two numbers instead of one. The second is allowed to be worse than the first.

The lesson

A hyperparameter chosen on a set of rows makes that set part of the training procedure.

It is in the silent-failure catalogue, it was diagnosed once already in Lecture 2, and it came back in a lecture whose entire subject is choosing a hyperparameter. That is not a coincidence — it is what makes it worth a catalogue.

The assistant did exactly what was asked. The specification was the defect.

Is that good?

The brief asked for calibrated probabilities and a defensible cut-off, not for a headline. Against that brief:

  • Log loss 0.427 against an anchor of 0.666 — the model has genuinely learned something beyond the base rate
  • Brier 0.134 against a measured floor of about 0.121. There is roughly 0.013 of Brier score left on the table, total, for any model of these columns
  • 179 test passengers, so a difference of a point or two of accuracy is three or four people and is not a difference

That third bullet is the one to put in the report. A test set of 179 cannot support the comparisons people will want to make with it.

Regularisation checklist — keep this

  • Is the data scaled, inside the pipeline, before a penalty is applied?
  • Is the bias term excluded from the penalty?
  • Was $\alpha$ (or C) chosen on validation folds, never on the test set?
  • If the search sat at the edge of the grid, was the grid widened?
  • Is the unregularised model in the comparison table, so the penalty has to earn its place?
  • Was the stopping epoch, if any, chosen on held-out rows?

Six questions. The fifth is the one people skip, and it is the one that would have saved us an hour today.

What we learned

  1. Expected squared error splits exactly into squared bias, variance and irreducible noise, over the training set and the label
  2. The gap between a training curve and a held-out curve is the variance term; curves plateauing together mean bias instead
  3. The noise floor is measurable: 0.121 Brier, from passengers whose recorded values are identical
  4. Degree 5 failed three ways at once — high variance, no minimiser, and no unique minimiser
  5. Adding $\alpha\mathbf{A}$ fixes the last two for every $\alpha > 0$, which is Thread 1’s open question answered
  6. Ridge shrinks, lasso selects, elastic net does both, early stopping does it by not arriving
  7. None of them beat choosing the right degree. Regularisation is a repair, not an upgrade
  8. A hyperparameter chosen on the test set costs 0.019 of log loss here, and the cost grows with the size of the search
