First: what is random here?

Not the passenger. Fix one passenger $x$ and ask about them repeatedly.

  • The training set $D$ is random — you happened to get these 712 rows out of the 891
  • The label $y$ of the passenger is random — two people with identical recorded values did not both survive

Every expectation in this derivation is over those two sources and nothing else. The model is deterministic given $D$.

This slide is where the room splits. Ask explicitly: “when I write E, what am I averaging over?” Someone will say “the test set”. That is the answer to a different question.

The quantity to decompose

$$\mathbb{E}_{D,\,y}\!\left[\left(y - \hat{p}_D(x)\right)^{2}\right]$$

Expected squared error at one passenger

SymbolMeaningObserved?
$\hat{p}_D(x)$what your model predicts, having seen $D$yes
$\bar{p}(x) = \mathbb{E}_D[\hat{p}_D(x)]$the average prediction over all training sets you could have drawnno — estimated
$p^{*}(x)$the true probability that this passenger survivesnever

Averaged over passengers this is the Brier score the previous lecture already reported. We decompose it at a single $x$ and then average — nothing is lost by doing it one passenger at a time.

The decomposition

$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(y - \hat{p}_D\right)^{2}\right] \;=\; \left(p^{*} - \bar{p}\right)^{2} \;+\; \mathbb{E}_{D}\!\left[\left(\hat{p}_D - \bar{p}\right)^{2}\right] \;+\; p^{*}\!\left(1 - p^{*}\right)$$
TermMeansCured by
squared biaswrong on average — the model family cannot reach the truthmore capacity
varianceunstable — a different sample gives a different answerless capacity, or more data
noisethe label is not a function of the recorded columnsnothing

How it arises: inside the square, add and subtract $\bar{p}$ and average over $D$; then add and subtract $p^{*}$ and average over $y$. Both cross terms vanish, because $\bar{p} = \mathbb{E}_D[\hat{p}_D]$ and $p^{*} = \mathbb{E}_y[y]$; the last term is $\operatorname{Var}(y)$ for a Bernoulli label.

The first two repairs point in opposite directions. That is the whole difficulty, and it is why it is called a trade-off.

Animation

The three terms, in one picture

Bias is the distance from the centre of the spread to the truth. Variance is the width of the spread. Neither is visible from one training set.

An honest caveat before we measure anything

The identity is exact for squared error. Our headline metric is log loss, and log loss does not split into three additive terms like this.

What we do about it The decomposition is measured on the Brier score — which the previous lecture already reported alongside log loss, and which is the squared error of a probability. The shape of the answer transfers; the numbers are Brier numbers and are labelled as such.

Saying “bias–variance” about a log loss curve is common and slightly wrong. Doing it knowingly, and saying which metric you decomposed, is the difference.

Start with the term you cannot fix

$p^{*}$ is never observed — but where several passengers share exactly the same recorded values, every model of those columns must give them the same number. Whatever their outcomes do inside that cell is a floor.


keys  = ["Sex", "Pclass", "AgeBand", "FamBand", "Embarked"]
cells = full.groupby(keys, observed=True)["Survived"].agg(["sum", "count"])

# k(m-k)/(m(m-1)) is the unbiased estimator of p(1-p) from m Bernoulli draws
multi = cells[cells["count"] >= 2]
var   = multi["sum"] * (multi["count"] - multi["sum"]) / (
        multi["count"] * (multi["count"] - 1))
noise = (var * multi["count"]).sum() / multi["count"].sum()

133 distinct cells; 102 of them hold at least two passengers, covering 858 people.

The floor

0.121

Brier score. Measured, not assumed.

56 of the 102 shared cells are mixed — 657 passengers sit in a cell where the outcome went both ways. That is 74% of everyone on board, living inside a cell whose answer cannot be certain.

It is an under-estimate of the true noise: two passengers in the same cell may still differ in ways the manifest never recorded. The real floor is at least this high.

Failure condition — a model measured against zero error The irreducible noise here is 0.121 Brier, measured from passengers whose recorded values are identical and whose outcomes differ. A model judged against zero rather than against that floor will be tuned forever toward an error that no model of these columns can reach.

Two shapes, two diagnoses

  • Both curves plateau high and together → bias. The model is too rigid; more data changes nothing
  • A gap that is still open at the last point → variance. More data would help, and you may not be able to get any

These are the only two shapes, and telling them apart takes ten seconds once you know what you are looking at.

Diagnosis 2 · The minimiser does not exist

Suppose that in the expanded feature space some $\boldsymbol\theta$ separates the classes perfectly. Then

$$\sigma\!\left(c\,\boldsymbol\theta^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}\right) \;\longrightarrow\; \begin{cases}1 & \text{if } \boldsymbol\theta^{\intercal}\mathbf{x} > 0\\ 0 & \text{if } \boldsymbol\theta^{\intercal}\mathbf{x} < 0\end{cases} \qquad \text{as } c \to \infty$$

so the training log loss falls monotonically towards zero as $\lVert\boldsymbol\theta\rVert \to \infty$. The infimum is never attained.

Diagnosis 3 · The minimiser is not unique

Recall from the previous lecture that FamilySize = SibSp + Parch + 1 exactly, on every row. Standardise the five numeric columns, form $\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X}$, and look at its spectrum:


>>> np.linalg.eigvalsh(Z.T @ Z)
array([5.59e-13, 4.29e+02, 5.43e+02, 7.90e+02, 1.80e+03])

The smallest eigenvalue is zero to the precision the arithmetic allows. Four columns of information in five columns of data.

Condition number about $2.82\times10^{15}$. A double carries about $10^{16}$ of relative precision, so roughly one significant digit survives the solve.

What adding $\alpha\mathbf{I}$ does to that spectrum

If $\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$, then

$$\left(\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X} + \alpha\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{v} \;=\; \left(\lambda + \alpha\right)\mathbf{v}$$
  • Same eigenvectors; every eigenvalue moved up by exactly $\alpha$
  • $\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X}$ is positive semi-definite, so $\lambda \ge 0$, so $\lambda + \alpha \ge \alpha > 0$
  • No eigenvalue is zero, so the matrix is invertible for every $\alpha > 0$
The normal equation, repaired Lecture 3 derived $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta} = (\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}y$ and showed that it fails when $\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X}$ is singular. Adding $\alpha\mathbf{I}$ makes it exist, for every $\alpha > 0$, whatever the columns do. Those three lines are the proof.

and it bounds the conditioning

$$\kappa\!\left(\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X} + \alpha\mathbf{I}\right) \;=\; \frac{\lambda_{\max} + \alpha}{\lambda_{\min} + \alpha} \;\le\; \frac{\lambda_{\max} + \alpha}{\alpha}$$

The bound on the right does not mention $\lambda_{\min}$ at all. Whatever the data do — exact collinearity included — the conditioning is controlled by a number you choose.

At $\alpha = 1$ the condition number of our singular matrix falls from $2.82\times10^{15}$ to 1798.

And $\kappa$ is what set the step count for gradient descent in the previous lecture. The penalty is not only a statistical device; it is what makes the optimisation tractable.

Condition number against $\alpha$

A straight line of slope $-1$ over twelve decades: in this regime the condition number is simply $\lambda_{\max}/\alpha$. The dependence in the data is not repaired — it is dominated.

The idea, in one sentence

Stop asking for the best fit. Ask for the best fit among small models.

$$J(\boldsymbol\theta) \;=\; \underbrace{L(\boldsymbol\theta)}_{\text{fit the data}} \;+\; \underbrace{\alpha\,\Omega(\boldsymbol\theta)}_{\text{stay small}}$$

$\Omega$ penalises the size of the weights; $\alpha$ says how much you care. Setting $\alpha = 0$ recovers exactly the previous lecture.

Constraining the model is one of the two cures for variance, and the only one available when the dataset is fixed at 891 rows.

Ridge — the $\ell_2$ penalty

$$J(\boldsymbol\theta) \;=\; \mathrm{MSE}(\boldsymbol\theta) \;+\; \frac{\alpha}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta_j^{2}$$
  • The sum starts at $j = 1$: the bias term $\theta_0$ is not penalised. Shrinking the intercept would pull every prediction towards zero rather than towards the mean
  • Equivalently $\Omega(\boldsymbol\theta) = \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_2^2$, where $\mathbf{w}$ is $\boldsymbol\theta$ without the bias

Also called Tikhonov regularisation; the book gives both names.

Lasso — the $\ell_1$ penalty

$$J(\boldsymbol\theta) \;=\; \mathrm{MSE}(\boldsymbol\theta) \;+\; 2\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left|\theta_j\right|$$

Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator. The word selection is the point: lasso drives weights to exactly zero, so it performs feature selection as a side effect of fitting.

Ridge shrinks everything and eliminates nothing.

Why $\ell_1$ produces exact zeros and $\ell_2$ does not

Look at the gradient of the penalty near $\theta_j = 0$.

Ridge

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_j}\,\theta_j^{2} = 2\theta_j \;\to\; 0$$

The push towards zero fades as you approach zero. You arrive only in the limit.

Lasso

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_j}\,\left|\theta_j\right| = \pm 1$$

The push keeps its size right up to zero. If the data pulls with less than $\alpha$, the weight reaches zero and stops.

At $\theta_j = 0$ the absolute value is not differentiable; the subgradient is the whole interval $[-1, 1]$, and any data pull inside that interval is held at exactly zero.

Resist the urge to soften this. Regularisation is a repair, not an upgrade, and choosing the right capacity beats penalising the wrong one.

Early stopping — a penalty by another route

Fit iteratively, and stop before the optimiser gets where it is going.

  • Gradient descent starts at $\boldsymbol\theta = \mathbf{0}$ — the smallest possible model
  • Each epoch moves the weights outward, fitting more of the training set
  • Held-out error falls, reaches a minimum, and then rises

Géron calls it a “beautiful free lunch”: no penalty term and no extra term in the objective — just a stopping rule. The knob is the epoch count, and it is a hyperparameter like any other.

Choosing a hyperparameter is choosing a model


best_C, best_score = None, np.inf

for C in np.logspace(-4, 4, 17):
    m = pipeline(degree=3, C=C).fit(X_train, y_train)
    score = log_loss(y_test, m.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1])
    if score < best_score:
        best_C, best_score = C, score
Failure condition — a hyperparameter chosen on the rows you then report best_score is a minimum over seventeen noisy estimates, and the minimum of noisy estimates is biased downward even when every estimate is individually unbiased. The code never writes to the test set. It reads it seventeen times, and reading is enough.

The procedure that does not have the problem


gs = GridSearchCV(pipeline(degree=3), {"clf__C": np.logspace(-4, 4, 17)},
                  scoring="neg_log_loss", cv=cv).fit(X_train, y_train)

print(-gs.best_score_)                                    # the honest estimate
print(log_loss(y_test, gs.predict_proba(X_test)[:, 1]))   # the test set, once

Same seventeen candidates, same runtime, two numbers instead of one. Report both. The second is allowed to be worse than the first, and if it is much worse that is itself the finding.

The standing rule, extended by eleven words: nothing derived from the test set may appear in the training path — including the choice of any hyperparameter.

Summary

  1. Expected squared error splits exactly into squared bias, variance and irreducible noise — over the training set and the label, not over test points
  2. A gap still open between training and held-out curves means variance; curves plateauing together mean bias
  3. The noise floor is measurable: 0.121 Brier, from passengers whose recorded values are identical
  4. Degree 5 failed three ways at once — high variance, no minimiser, and no unique minimiser
  5. Adding $\alpha\mathbf{I}$ makes $\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X}$ invertible for every $\alpha > 0$ and bounds its condition number
  6. Ridge shrinks, lasso selects, early stopping keeps the weights small by not arriving
  7. None of them beat choosing the right capacity. A penalty is a repair, not an upgrade
  8. A hyperparameter chosen on the test set costs 0.019 of log loss here, and the cost grows with the size of the search

Solutions — Lecture 4

1. Gradient descent on the same data, with the features unscaled, takes many more steps to converge than on…

Unscaled features make the contours elongated, so the gradient points across the valley rather than along it.

  • The Hessian is $X^{\mathsf T}X$, so the curvature along a feature goes as the square of its scale — multiply a column by ten and its curvature rises a hundredfold, which is how one column comes to dominate
  • The learning rate must then suit the steepest direction, which makes it far too small for the shallow one

2. On the Titanic manifest, your logistic regression on one-hot encoded columns produces coefficients that look…

Five come from the encoder, one from you. The repair is both: drop one level per categorical, and delete the engineered column.

  • Each one-hot block sums to 1 in every row, and so does the intercept column — five categoricals, five exact dependencies
  • The sixth is FamilySize = SibSp + Parch + 1, an identity rather than a correlation. Dropping a level alone still leaves the rank one short of the column count, because the dependency you wrote yourself survives it

Solutions — Lecture 4 (2)

3. A model reaches 0.80 accuracy and is badly calibrated. Explain what that means, and name a decision it would…

Its predicted probabilities do not match observed frequencies; any decision using a cost-weighted threshold is then wrong.

  • Accuracy reads only the arg-max, so it is blind to the probability that produced it
  • Choosing a threshold from expected cost requires the probability to mean what it says

4. In a logistic regression with first class as the reference level, the coefficient on Pclass_3 is $-1.169$.…

It multiplies the odds of survival by $e^{-1.169} = 0.31$, relative to first class, holding the other features fixed.

  • The linear part predicts the log-odds, so adding 1 to an indicator adds its coefficient to the log-odds and multiplies the odds by its exponential
  • The probability moves by an amount that depends on where it started: odds of 1 times 0.31 give $p = 0.24$, while $p = 0.9$ becomes 0.74

Solutions — Lecture 4 (3)

5. State the update rule for batch gradient descent, and say what changes in it for stochastic gradient descent…

$\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\nabla J$. Only the set the gradient is computed over changes; the rule does not.

  • SGD estimates the same gradient from one instance rather than all of them
  • The estimate is unbiased — averaged over a uniformly drawn row it is exactly the batch gradient — but noisy, and it costs $O(n)$ per step instead of $O(mn)$
