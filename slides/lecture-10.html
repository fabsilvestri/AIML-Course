Why look inside a model that already scores well

ReasonWhat can go wrong unseen
Debuggingthe score is high for a reason you would reject: an identifier, a column filled in after the outcome, a quirk of how the data were collected — a shortcut that holds in the test set and not in the world
Trustthe people who act on the model — an underwriter, a recruiter — cannot tell when to overrule it
The lawthe EU AI Act classes credit scoring and recruitment as high-risk: data examined for bias, documentation, logging, human oversight, monitoring after release. Under the GDPR a person refused by an automated decision can ask for a human and for meaningful information about the logic
Fairnessan accuracy is an average over everyone, and the people it fails can all be in one group

None of the four is answered by the test score. All of them are answered by measurements on the fitted model.

Which columns does the model rely on? Two ways to ask

Impurity importance — trees only

A tree grows by splits. Each split sends a node’s rows left or right so that each side is purer: closer to all above 50K, or all below. Credit every split’s gain in purity to the column it split on, weighted by the rows it split; add up over all the trees; scale so the columns sum to 1.

It asks: how much did the trees use this column to fit their rows?

Permutation importance — any model

Score the model on a set of rows. Then shuffle one column, so that every person gets someone else’s value, and score it again. The fall in AUC is the column’s importance: large if the predictions depend on it, zero if the model ignores it.

It asks: how much do the predictions depend on this column?

Permutation importance, defined

$$I_j \;=\; \operatorname{AUC}\bigl(f;\, X, y\bigr) \;-\; \frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\operatorname{AUC}\bigl(f;\, X^{(\pi_r,\,j)}, y\bigr)$$
  • $X$, $y$: held-out rows; $X^{(\pi_r, j)}$: column $j$ replaced by a random permutation of itself
  • Shuffling keeps the column’s distribution and destroys its link with the target and with the rest of the row
  • Needs only predictions: it works for any model, the boosted one included, at $R$ prediction passes per column
  • $R$ repeats give a spread; report it beside the mean
Xp = X.copy()
Xp[:, j] = X[rng.permutation(len(X))][:, j]       # one column, or a group
drop = auc(model, X, y) - auc(model, Xp, y)

On which rows? The forest has memorised its own

forest = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, random_state=42).fit(X_fit, y_fit)
impurity = pd.Series(forest.feature_importances_, index=FEATURES)
Training rows · AUC 1.0000 The rows the 200 trees were grown on. The forest gets every one right: it has memorised them. Importance here says what the forest needs to reproduce rows it has memorised.
Held-out rows · AUC 0.9046 Rows the forest never saw. Importance here says what helps it predict people it has not seen — the bank’s question.

Impurity exists only on the training rows: it is a by-product of growing the trees. Permutation runs on either. The boosted model has no feature_importances_, so the comparison runs on a forest.

The column to watch: fnlwgt

What it is The final weight: the Census Bureau’s estimate of how many people in the US population this one respondent stands for.
How it is made From the survey’s sampling design, adjusted so that weighted counts match population totals by state, age, sex, race and Hispanic origin.
Why it should not matter It records how the person was sampled, not their work, education or household. And most people have their own value: 20,077 distinct values on the fit rows.

An importance measure that ranks fnlwgt high is measuring something other than what predicts income.

Why impurity importance misleads: two causes, both measured

CauseMechanismMeasured
Computed on the training rowsa fully grown forest fits its own rows exactly, so every split, even one on noise, reduces impurity thereforest AUC on its training rows 1.0000, on test rows 0.9046
Many distinct valuesa column with a value per row offers a split anywhere; deep in a tree, some split on it always cleans a small nodefnlwgt takes 20,077 distinct values on the fit rows; age takes 73

Panel 2 of the figure separates the two: on the training rows, permutation still credits fnlwgt (0.0313, rank 6), because the trees memorised it there. Only rows the model never saw say it is worth nothing.

Failure condition — importance earned on the training rows Impurity importance measures how much a tree used a column to fit its training rows, not how much the predictions on new rows depend on it. It is biased towards columns with many distinct values, and it exists only for trees.

How does the prediction change with age?

ICE — one applicant’s curve Take one applicant. Keep every answer they gave and change only their age: 20, 22, … 80. Ask the model each time. The predictions, joined, are that applicant’s curve: individual conditional expectation.
Partial dependence — the average curve Draw the ICE curve of every applicant. At each age, average their predictions. One curve: what the model predicts, on average, if everyone had that age.
What each one tells you The partial dependence gives the overall shape of the effect in one line. The ICE curves show whether everyone follows that shape — and sometimes nobody does.
Importance told us how much the model uses a column; it does not tell us how the prediction moves when the column changes. Does the probability go up with age? Steadily? For everyone? The simplest way to find out: take one applicant, keep everything they told us — job, education, marriage — change only their age, and ask the model again. Age 20, ask; 22, ask; all the way to 80. Join the answers: what the model would say about this person at every age. That is an ICE curve, one per person. Do it for everyone and, at each age, average the curves: that single curve is the partial dependence.

The average rises by 0.20. The married rise by 0.40, twice that; the unmarried do not move. Not one of the four rises by 0.20: the average curve describes nobody. The effect of age depends on another answer, marriage — an interaction — and the partial dependence averages two different stories into one that is true of no one. The fix: look at the ICE curves, coloured by what you suspect, here marriage. If asked whether the partial dependence is useless: no, it is a good one-line summary when everyone moves roughly the same way; never show it alone.

ICE and partial dependence, in general

$$\operatorname{ICE}_i(v) = f\bigl(v,\ \mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{-j}\bigr), \qquad \operatorname{PD}(v) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{ICE}_i(v)$$
  • $f$: the model; $j$: the column studied (age); $v$: a value on the grid; $\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{-j}$: every other answer of applicant $i$, kept as given
  • In probabilities, the units of the product rule; on the data, 1,000 test applicants, ages 18 to 80
for k, v in enumerate(grid):
    Xg = X.copy(); Xg[:, j] = v                   # everyone gets age v

pdp = ice.mean(axis=0)

Failure condition — an average over an interaction Where the effect of one column depends on another, the partial dependence averages curves of different shapes, and can even average a rise and a fall into a flat line. Draw the ICE curves; colour them by the suspect.

The mathematics: the Shapley value of one prediction

Features are players; a coalition $S$ is the set whose answers are the applicant’s. The others are filled in from a background sample $B$ of applicants, and the model averaged:

$$v(S) \;=\; \frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in B} f\bigl(\mathbf{x}_S,\ \mathbf{b}_{\bar S}\bigr)$$ $$\phi_i \;=\; \sum_{S \subseteq N\setminus\{i\}} \frac{|S|!\,(M-|S|-1)!}{M!}\,\bigl(v(S\cup\{i\}) - v(S)\bigr)$$
  • $v(S \cup \{i\}) - v(S)$: what revealing answer $i$ adds to a coalition that already holds $S$
  • The weight is the share of the $M!$ orders of revealing the answers in which exactly $S$ comes before $i$: $\phi_i$ is the marginal contribution averaged over every order

Efficiency, and the three other properties

$$\sum_{i=1}^{M}\phi_i \;=\; v(N) - v(\varnothing) \;=\; f(\mathbf{x}) \;-\; \frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in B} f(\mathbf{b})$$
PropertyStatement
Efficiencythey add up to the prediction minus the baseline: in every order the contributions telescope
Symmetryanswers that add the same to every coalition get the same value
Dummyan answer that adds nothing to any coalition gets zero
Additivitythe values for $f + g$ are those for $f$ plus those for $g$

The only attribution with all four. Efficiency is proved in the notes, and examinable.

Computed exactly: every coalition, once

masks = np.array(list(itertools.product([False, True], repeat=M)))  # 2^M
rows  = np.where(masks[:, None, :], x, background[None])            # 2^M x |B| x M
v     = predict(rows.reshape(-1, M)).reshape(len(masks), -1).mean(1)
index, size = masks @ bit, masks.sum(1)                             # S -> row of v
for i in range(M):
    without = ~masks[:, i]                                          # every S without i
    phi[i] = (WEIGHT[size[without]]
              * (v[index[without] + bit[i]] - v[without])).sum()
  • $M = 12$ answers: 4,096 coalitions × 200 background applicants = 819,200 predictions, in one call
  • Checked first on a linear function, where the answer is known: $\phi_i = w_i\,(x_i - \bar b_i)$
  • The cost doubles with every feature. At fifty, exact enumeration is out of reach: sampling estimators, and exact algorithms for trees, are what the shap library implements

Three fairness criteria, each reasonable

$\hat Y$ the decision, $Y$ the truth, $A$ the group, $s$ the model’s probability:

CriterionRequires, for every group $a$In words
Demographic parity$P(\hat Y = 1 \mid A = a)$ equalthe same share pre-approved
Equal opportunity$P(\hat Y = 1 \mid Y = 1, A = a)$ equalthe same TPR: a qualified applicant’s chance does not depend on the group
Calibration within groups$P(Y = 1 \mid s, A = a) = s$a probability means the same in every group; at a threshold, its footprint is equal PPV

PPV, from Lecture 3’s precision: of those pre-approved, the share who earn above 50K.

Each one has been written into a law, a policy or a court judgment somewhere. Each one protects a different person.

One threshold, one model, three verdicts. A pre-approval means the same thing for a man and for a woman; a qualified woman gets one less often.

Why: qualified women score lower

The model sees fewer of the answers that go with high incomes among qualified women, so more of them sit below 0.5. A threshold at 0.3846 would give them the men’s TPR on the validation rows.

Calibrated within both groups

Men: mean probability 0.3019, base rate 0.3038. Women: 0.1165 and 0.1093. Calibration error 0.0146 and 0.0137 — a probability means the same thing in both groups.

When base rates differ, they cannot all hold

For one group with base rate $p$, count its true positives twice — as a share of those above 50K, and of those selected:

$$\text{TPR} \;=\; \frac{\text{selected}\times\text{PPV}}{p}$$
  • Hold any two of the three equal across groups, and the identity fixes the third by the ratio of the base rates
  • Equal PPV and equal TPR force the selection ratio to $p_{\text{women}}/p_{\text{men}}$ = 0.3598. Measured, with PPV equal and TPR not: 0.3061. Parity is out of reach for any rule that keeps the other two
  • Only equal base rates escape, and no model changes a base rate
What the result buys you No model satisfies every criterion. Choosing which to hold is a decision about whom to protect, and it belongs to whoever is accountable for the product.

A threshold per group is a policy decision

  • It uses the attribute at the moment of decision. The model never saw sex; the rule now reads it for every applicant. Anti-discrimination law may forbid exactly that in credit — a question for the bank’s legal and policy owners, not for the data team.
  • Every option has a price, and the table states it. Equal opportunity costs almost no accuracy and lowers the women’s PPV; parity costs a great deal of both.
  • There are remedies upstream. Better answers on the form, the blank occupation fixed, a manual review for everyone in a band below the threshold — none of which needs the attribute at decision time.
The data team’s job Measure every criterion, per group, with counts; show what each remedy costs and buys; write it down. The choice is made by the people accountable for it, and it is recorded with its reasons.

Design exercise · in the manner of an ML system-design interview

The task: audit a credit-limit model before it is filed

The brief A digital bank operating across the European Union sells a credit card inside its app. The feature is the model that decides, at application, whether to offer the card and with what credit limit, and later whether to raise it. The users are applicants and cardholders; the business measures losses from defaults, the share of applicants who take the card, and the revenue a limit earns. Under the EU AI Act a creditworthiness model is a high-risk system: before it ships, the bank must document it, examine its data for bias, and put human oversight and monitoring in place.
  • Given: application answers, the bank’s own account history, credit-bureau data, and repayment outcomes for the cards it issued
  • Consumers of the output: the app, the credit officers who review cases, the letters that tell an applicant why
  • Regulators: the financial supervisor, the data protection authority, and anti-discrimination law

Step 1 — Frame the audit: decisions, harms, groups

  • Name the decisions the model makes — offer or refuse, the limit, each raise — and the harm each error does: a good customer refused or kept at a low limit; a customer given more credit than they can repay
  • Name the groups the audit will read before any number is computed: the protected attributes the law lists, age bands, regions, new customers against existing ones
  • Write the metrics per harm: among customers who would repay, the share offered the card; among those who default, the share given a high limit
Pitfall Auditing the overall score. A model can rank well on everyone and fail one group, and an average reported to the regulator hides exactly that. Decide the groups and the harms first, so that the numbers cannot be chosen to pass.

Step 2 — The attributes the audit needs

  • The model takes no protected attribute as input. The audit needs them anyway, to measure outcomes per group
  • Collect them with a legal basis and the applicant’s knowledge, store them apart from the model’s features, with access limited to the audit team and every read logged
  • Measure the proxies: fit a model that predicts each attribute from the features, and list the features that carry it
Pitfall “We do not collect it, so we cannot discriminate.” Postcode, employer, household answers and spending patterns carry the attribute, the model uses it through them, and without the attribute nobody can show whether it does. Keep it, guarded, for the audit.

Step 3 — Who was never labelled

  • Repayment is observed only for applicants the old policy approved. Everyone it refused has no outcome, and they are not a random sample
  • Keep a small randomised approval slice, within risk limits, so that some outcomes exist where the old policy said no; document the rest as an assumption
  • Audit refusals on the applicants with outcomes, and say plainly which groups the outcomes cover thinly
Pitfall Auditing only the approved. A group the old policy refused often has few labels, so its error rates rest on a handful of people — or on none — and the audit reports the model fair where it simply cannot see.

Step 4 — What the model relies on

  • Permutation importance on held-out applicants, with its spread over repeats, for the technical documentation
  • A column of random numbers added as a control: any feature that scores no better than it is reported as unused
Pitfall Filing the library’s impurity chart. An application identifier, a timestamp or a sampling weight with thousands of distinct values comes first, the supervisor reads it as what drives the credit decision, and the bank has documented a model it does not have.

Step 5 — Shapes, and shortcuts

  • ICE curves for every important feature, coloured by the suspected interaction; the partial dependence only beside them
  • Look for shortcuts: a feature that encodes how the data were collected — the branch, the channel, the device — rather than whether the customer repays
  • Review every important feature with the credit officers: would they defend its effect in front of a customer?
Pitfall One partial dependence curve filed alone. It averages customers whose curves have different shapes, and the shape the bank documents is one the model shows nobody real.

Step 6 — Reasons for one applicant

  • Shapley values for every refusal and every reduced limit, against a stated and versioned background — for instance the customers approved last quarter
  • The few largest negative values become plain-language reasons in the letter, from a reviewed list; the values themselves go to the credit officer who reviews the case
  • The values must add up to the score minus the baseline; that check runs on every letter
Pitfall A reason that is a proxy, or that nobody can act on. “Household status” in a refusal letter tells the applicant the bank weighed something close to a protected attribute, and invites the complaint; reasons are reviewed before they can be sent, and a proxy is a finding for step seven, not a reason.

Step 7 — The audit by group

  • Per group: the offer rate, the true-positive rate among customers who repay, the false-positive rate among those who default, calibration of the default probability, and the limits granted
  • Every rate with its count and an interval; small groups pooled or flagged, never silently dropped
  • All three fairness criteria reported, with the identity that says they cannot all hold when default rates differ
Pitfall Reporting the one criterion that passes. A model calibrated in every group passes predictive parity and fails equal opportunity whenever the base rates differ, and a report that shows only the first has hidden the finding the supervisor asked for.

Step 8 — Remedies, oversight, monitoring

  • Remedies upstream first: better features, a fixed form, a manual review band below the threshold where most wrongful refusals sit
  • Any rule that reads a protected attribute at decision time goes to the legal and policy owners, and their decision is recorded with its reasons
  • Ship with a model card, logs of every decision and its reasons, credit officers able to overrule with the override logged, and slice metrics monitored in production; re-audit on every retrain
Pitfall A per-group threshold chosen by the data team. It may be unlawful differential treatment, it was never approved by anyone accountable, and when the regulator asks who decided to treat applicants differently by sex, the answer is a notebook.

Step 9 — File it: pipeline and pitfalls

Decisions, harms and groups named → protected attributes kept apart for the audit, proxies measured → the unlabelled refused accounted for → held-out permutation importance with a control → ICE and shortcut review → reviewed reasons from Shapley values → every criterion per group, with counts → remedies decided by their owners → model card, logs, oversight, monitoring.

What we did

  1. Asked a good model two questions its test score cannot answer: what does it rely on, and for whom does it fail

  3. Shape: partial dependence averages ICE curves of different shapes, and can describe nobody
  4. Local: the Shapley value, computed exactly over 4,096 coalitions; efficiency makes the values add up to the prediction minus the baseline; a blank on the form was the largest thing held against one applicant

Solutions — Lecture 9

1. A harmful-content model ends in one softmax over five harms. Say why this is the wrong output layer, and what…

Harms co-occur and a softmax forces the probabilities to sum to one, so a post cannot be both violent and hateful. Use one sigmoid output per harm, each trained with its own binary cross-entropy term.

  • The task is multi-label, not multi-class
  • Actions are taken by a separate policy layer with a threshold per harm, so each harm needs a probability of its own

2. Harmful posts are a fraction of a percent of all posts. Explain why accuracy and the area under the ROC curve…

Predicting “not harmful” for everything is almost always right, and the ROC curve looks excellent because negatives dominate the false positive rate. Report the precision–recall curve per harm, read at the precision each action requires, and check calibration.

  • With rare positives a tiny false positive rate is still a flood of wrong flags; precision shows it, the ROC curve hides it
  • Different harms carry different costs, so one number for all of them hides the trade-off the policy team has to make

Solutions — Lecture 9 (2)

3. The training labels are past reviewer decisions on posts the current system flagged. Name the bias, its…

Selection bias: only flagged posts are ever labelled, so the next model never learns what the current one misses. Label a random sample of all traffic every week, use it for evaluation and prevalence, and add it to training.

  • The loop closes on itself: the model chooses its own training data
  • Only a sample drawn independently of the model can measure what the model does not see

4. Adding “number of user reports” sharply raises the offline recall of the model that checks posts…

It is leakage in time: at upload the count is always zero, so the gain cannot appear in production. Compute every feature as of the moment of the decision; use reports only in a second model that re-scores the post as reactions arrive.

  • A feature is valid only if the serving system can produce it at decision time
  • The offline split must reproduce what is known when the decision is made

Solutions — Lecture 9 (3)

5. For each harm the policy layer has three thresholds: remove automatically, send to a human reviewer, reduce…

Automatic removal: the precision the policy team accepts with no human in the loop. Review: reviewer capacity, since the expected daily volume must fit the queue. Reduced distribution: the cost of spreading a harmful post against hiding a good one. Each is a point on that harm’s validation precision–recall curve, signed off as policy.

  • A threshold is a decision about costs, not a hyperparameter tuned for a score
  • The bands only mean something if the probabilities are calibrated
