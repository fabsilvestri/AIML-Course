Part one

The brief

15 minutes · the problem, the data, the stakeholder

The stakeholder

The request “We have a photographic archive. We do not know how many distinct people are in it. Group the photographs by person, so that a human can name a group instead of naming every photograph.”
  • The archive is not annotated, and nobody remembers who is in it
  • A trained annotator costs money and time per photograph
  • We are funded for forty labels. That is it

This is the ordinary situation outside a benchmark: data is cheap, labels are not.

Why this is a different kind of problem

Supervised

  • You are given $(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}, y^{(i)})$
  • The metric compares prediction to truth
  • The baseline is “predict the majority”
  • You know when you are wrong

Unsupervised

  • You are given $\mathbf{x}^{(i)}$ only
  • The metric compares the grouping to itself
  • The baseline is “group at random”
  • You find out you were wrong when someone looks
The consequence for the rest of the hour Every number you compute today is a statement about geometry, not about people. Whether the geometry is the people is a separate question, and it is answered by looking.

Forty people

Same crop, same lighting rig, same background. What varies between rows is the person — and, as much, the lamp.

What “the same person” looks like

Glasses on and off, eyes shut, head turned, lit from either side. All twenty of these must end up in exactly two groups.

Ask which two photographs in the top row look least alike. Then ask whether they would look alike to a Euclidean distance.

A photograph is a point

Flatten the 64 × 64 grid and a photograph is a vector

$$\mathbf{x}^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^{4,096},\qquad x_j \in [0,1]$$

The corpus is 400 points in a space of 4,096 dimensions. Every method today measures the distance between two of them.

More dimensions than points, by a factor of ten. Hold on to that; it is the whole of the next lecture.

Before building: is this possible at all?

k-means groups points that are close in Euclidean distance. So the question that decides the hour is:

Are two photographs of the same person closer than two photographs of different people?


D = pairwise_distances(X)                 # 400 x 400

same = y[iu[0]] == y[iu[1]]               # AUDIT: the hidden labels

79,800 pairs in total; 1,800 of them are the same person.

Distance is not identity

The two distributions overlap over most of their range. Pixel distance is partly identity and partly lighting.

The number that sets the ceiling

6%

of same-person pairs sit further apart than the median different-person pair.

What this predicts No method that groups by Euclidean distance in raw pixels will recover the forty people cleanly. Not because the method is bad — because the representation puts a lamp and a nose on the same axis.

Mean distance: 8.38 within a person, 12.41 between people. The gap is real, and it is small.

k-means, in five lines

  1. Choose $k$ centroids (scikit-learn uses k-means++, which spreads the initial ones out)
  2. Assign every point to its nearest centroid
  3. Move every centroid to the mean of its assigned points
  4. Repeat 2–3 until no assignment changes
  5. Repeat the whole thing n_init times and keep the best
Step 5 is not optional. Lloyd's algorithm converges — to a local minimum that depends entirely on where you started.

What k-means assumes, whether you asked or not

  • Every cluster is convex — the partition is a Voronoi diagram of the centroids
  • Every cluster is roughly spherical and the same size, because the only quantity is distance to a centre
  • Every point belongs to exactly one cluster; there is no “none of these”
  • $k$ is given

Three of those four are false for a photographic archive. The fourth is the one we spend the hour on.

Do not fix the first three today. They are Lecture 10's DBSCAN and Gaussian mixtures, and they only earn their place after the students have felt the constraint.

Two of these can choose a model. One of them can be shown to a stakeholder. They are not the same one.

Inertia

$$J \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl\lVert \mathbf{x}^{(i)} - \boldsymbol\mu_{c(i)}\bigr\rVert_2^{2}$$

The sum of squared distances to the assigned centroid

This is what k-means minimises, and it is what km.inertia_ returns. It is also what km.score() returns, negated, because scikit-learn's convention is that greater is better.

Note what it cannot do: $J$ is non-increasing in $k$ and reaches exactly $0$ at $k = m$. Minimising it chooses one cluster per photograph.

The silhouette coefficient

For a point $i$, let $a$ be its mean distance to the other points of its own cluster, and $b$ its mean distance to the points of the nearest other cluster.

$$s_i \;=\; \frac{b - a}{\max(a, b)} \;\in\; [-1, 1]$$
  • $s_i \to +1$: far inside its own cluster
  • $s_i \approx 0$: on the boundary between two clusters
  • $s_i \to -1$: it has been assigned to the wrong cluster

The silhouette score is the mean of $s_i$ over all points. Unlike inertia it is penalised both for splitting a group and for merging two, so it has an interior maximum.

What the silhouette costs

$a$ and $b$ need the distance from every point to every other point.

$$\underbrace{\binom{m}{2}}_{79,800\ \text{pairs}} \times \underbrace{n}_{4,096\ \text{dimensions}}$$

Quadratic in the number of photographs, linear in the number of pixels. Both factors matter later: the archive is bigger than 400, and the representation is wider than it needs to be.

There is a subsampled variant, silhouette_score(..., sample_size=...). It is not free either: it makes the estimate noisy, and a noisy criterion is exactly what we are about to have trouble with.

The brief says labelling is expensive. That is a claim about a budget, so put a number on it before designing around it.

Labelling a face is not reading a number off a form — it is asking a person whether these two photographs are the same person, and every pair is a separate judgement. That is why 79,800 is the wrong plan and 40 labels is the brief.

Ask what they would do with a budget of 400 labels instead. The honest answer is that they would still cluster first — the labels are better spent checking a grouping than building one.

The forty labels

The annotator is handed forty photographs, drawn at random, and asked which of them show the same person.


rng   = np.random.default_rng(42)

  • 780 pairs among the forty

  • 10% of the corpus is labelled

Forty labels is a small evaluation set. Remember that when the number it produces looks decisive.

Adjusted Rand index

Compare two groupings pair by pair. Count the pairs the two agree about — together in both, or apart in both — over all pairs:

$$\mathrm{RI} \;=\; \frac{a + b}{\binom{m}{2}}$$

$\mathrm{RI}$ is high for free at large $k$ — almost every pair is apart in both. The adjusted index subtracts what chance would give:

$$\mathrm{ARI} \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{RI} - \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{RI}]}{1 - \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{RI}]}$$

Random scores $0$ in expectation at any $k$; perfect scores $1$; negative is worse than chance.

What the silhouette cannot see

The silhouette compares each point’s distance to its own cluster against its distance to the nearest other cluster. That comparison assumes clusters are roughly spherical and roughly equal in spread.

So it can be confidently wrong A long, curved group of points scores badly even when it is exactly the grouping you wanted, and two blobs of very different density score well when merged. The number does not know what shape a face-identity cluster ought to be — only what a sphere looks like.

So when we choose k, the metric will answer confidently — and the confidence is about sphericity, not identity.

In two lectures this becomes actionable: DBSCAN and Gaussian mixtures drop the spherical assumption, and we compare all three on this corpus. Say it rather than putting it on the slide — it is a forward reference they cannot use yet.

Before you commit: what does nothing score?

The cheapest possible method. Put every photograph in a uniformly random group, twenty times, and look at the spread.


for seed in range(20):
    r   = np.random.default_rng(1000 + seed)
    lab = r.integers(0, k, size=len(X))
    sil.append(silhouette_score(X, lab))
    ari.append(adjusted_rand_score(y_audit, lab[audit]))

Twenty seeds, because a single-seed number is not a measurement — and because how far from zero is noise is the thing we actually need to know.

Rule 2, applied to ourselves: a metric with nothing to compare it to is decoration. A silhouette of 0.02 is not a discovery.

Ask for a show of hands on what random assignment scores before showing the table. A few always guess a large negative number.

What would a good system look like?

  • The stakeholder wants to name a group, not a photograph. A group holding two people costs them a mistake in the archive
  • ARI $=1$ means every pair of the forty is grouped exactly as the annotator grouped it
  • ARI $=0$ is the random anchor above
An ARI in the high tenths would let a human confirm a group in one glance each. Somewhere near zero means the human is back to looking at 400 photographs one at a time, which is the situation we were hired to remove.

That is an order of magnitude, not a target. Turning it into a number is your job for the next sixty seconds.

The specification

  • Input — X, shape (400, 4,096), floats in $[0,1]$
  • Output — one integer group per photograph, and the $k$ that produced it
  • Constraint — the full y may not enter any decision; only y[audit]

The constraint is the one people omit, and it is the one that makes today's result honest.

Choosing $k$: the elbow

The book's first suggestion. Plot inertia against $k$ and look for the bend.


inertia = []
for k in range(2, 61):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit(X)
    inertia.append(km.inertia_)

590 runs of Lloyd's algorithm. Start it now; it is still running when we come back to it.

Find the elbow

A smooth convex curve. Ask three people where the elbow is and you get three answers, all defensible.

Why that did not work

Inertia cannot choose $k$, ever $J(k)$ is non-increasing by construction: refining a partition cannot increase the sum of squared distances to the assigned centre. It reaches exactly $0$ at $k = m$. There is no interior minimum to find.
  • The kneedle rule — the point furthest below the chord joining the ends — picks $k = 19
  • The truth, which we are not supposed to know, is $k = 40$
  • At $k = 40$, 46% of the inertia at $k = 2$ is still there

The elbow is not a criterion. It is a picture of a criterion that does not exist.

A criterion with a maximum


from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

sil = []
for k in range(2, 61):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=42).fit(X)
    sil.append(silhouette_score(X, km.labels_))

best_k = 2 + int(np.argmax(sil))

Same loop, one more line, and now the criterion is penalised for splitting a group as well as for merging two.

Inside the cleanest and the worst cluster

The bottom row is not a person. It is a lighting condition — several different people lit and posed the same way.

What it grouped instead of identity

In 4,096 raw pixels, a lamp is bigger than a nose Moving the light source changes hundreds of pixel values by a large amount. Changing the person changes a few dozen by a small one. Euclidean distance adds them all up with equal weight.

  • Worst cluster: 24 photographs of 10 different people, purity 0.21
  • Mean purity across all 40 clusters: 0.74

The worked failure

“Pick the best $k$
and report the score”

Four beats: prompt, code, question, specification

Beat 1 — the prompt

What a reasonable person types “Cluster the faces with k-means, pick the best number of clusters using the silhouette score, and report the score.”

Under-specified in exactly one place. Nothing about a test set — because there is no test set, which is precisely why this one slips through the checklist you already have.

Beat 2 — the code it returns

best_score, best_k, best_model = -2, None, None

for k in [5, 10, 20, 40, 60]:
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=3, random_state=42).fit(X)
    s  = silhouette_score(X, km.labels_)
    if s > best_score:
        best_score, best_k, best_model = s, k, km

print(f"best k = {best_k}, silhouette = {best_score:.4f}")

It runs. It imports nothing exotic. It prints a believable number. The number it prints is optimistic.

Beat 3 — the question that catches it

Which number chose the model, and which number reports it?

Line 5 chose. Line 9 reports. They are the same number Five candidate values of $k$, five noisy estimates of the silhouette, and the code prints the largest of them. The maximum of several noisy estimates is biased upwards, and the bias grows with how many candidates you tried.

You have met this once already: it is why a GridSearchCV may not report its own best_score_ as the model's performance.

Beat 3b — measure it, do not assert it


for seed in range(20):
    a, b = np.split(rng.permutation(len(X)), 2)      # 200 / 200

    chosen, model = -2, None
    for k in [5, 10, 20, 40, 60]:
        km = KMeans(k, n_init=3, random_state=42).fit(X[a])
        s  = silhouette_score(X[a], km.labels_)
        if s > chosen: chosen, model = s, km

    held = silhouette_score(X[b], model.predict(X[b]))

Choose on one half, score on the other. Twenty seeds, because a single split of 400 photographs is not a measurement.

And the archive is not 400 photographs

  • k-means is linear in $m$, so ten times the photographs is roughly ten times the fitting
  • The silhouette is quadratic in $m$, so ten times the photographs is a hundred times the scoring
  • Both are linear in the 4,096 features, and that factor is the one nobody chose — it is just the size of the image
Two of those three factors are properties of the problem. The third is a property of the representation, and a representation is something you are allowed to change.

What we deliberately did not do today

  • Scale the features. Every pixel is already in $[0,1]$ and they are commensurable; standardising each pixel separately would amplify the corners of the image, which are background
  • Reduce the dimension. Next lecture, and it is the lecture
  • Try any clustering method except k-means. Also next lecture, and for a stated reason each time
  • Use the labels. Except as the forty, and as two audits that were announced

Naming what you left out is part of reporting a result. A method you did not try is not a method that failed.

What we learned

  1. Without labels, the metric measures geometry — and whether the geometry is the answer is settled by looking, not by the metric
  2. Inertia cannot choose $k$: it is monotone in $k$ and zero at $k=m$
  3. The silhouette can, because it is penalised for splitting as well as merging — but read it against a random anchor
  4. The silhouette diagram shows the shape the mean hides
  5. k-means assumes convex, equal-sized, spherical clusters and a known $k$. Three of those are false here
  6. In raw pixels, a lighting condition is a larger vector than a face
  7. The score that selected a model is an optimistic estimate of that model's performance — measured here at 50%
  8. One model-selection sweep cost 1 minutes on 6.6 MB of data
