Four lectures on one idea: a weighted sum $\boldsymbol\theta^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}$, fitted by least squares or by gradient descent, read through a sigmoid for a class — and judged on data it never saw.

The setting

An agency that manages a national forest has to publish a map of forest cover type — which species dominates each 30 by 30 metre patch of ground — over an area far too large to survey on foot.

The task From cartographic measurements alone — elevation, slope, aspect, distances to water, road and fire ignition points, hillshade, soil type — predict which of seven tree species covers the patch.

The survey: 581,012 patches, 54 cartographic columns, no missing values. We work on a stratified 60,000-patch subsample, split 48,000 / 12,000 before anything is fitted, and say so.

The map goes into the public record and decides where logging is permitted. A landowner refused a permit is entitled to ask why, so the agency wants each prediction to come with a reason a person can read: conditions on the measured columns, at most eight of them.

Keep that requirement in mind. It is what the model family of the first half of today satisfies, and what the second half gives up.

What a decision tree is

A binary tree. Each internal node asks one question about one feature; each leaf carries a prediction.

  • A question is always of the form $x_k \le t_k$ — one feature, one threshold
  • Prediction: start at the root, answer the question, go left or right, repeat until a leaf
  • The leaf predicts the majority class of the training instances that reached it — the $\arg\max_k p_k$ of that node’s class distribution

No weighted sum anywhere, so no feature scaling: $x_k \le t_k$ is invariant under any increasing transformation of $x_k$.

The path from the root to the leaf is the justification the agency asked for: one condition per level, in the measured columns. That is what makes a tree readable, and it is free.

How CART chooses a split

A node holds some training instances; write $p_k$ for the fraction in class $k$. Its Gini impurity is

$$G(\mathbf{p}) \;=\; 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k^{\,2}$$

the probability that two instances drawn from the node, with replacement, differ in class; a pure node scores 0. A split $x_k \le t_k$ sends $m_{\text{L}}$ instances left and $m_{\text{R}}$ right; CART picks the feature and threshold minimising the size-weighted impurity of the children:

$$J(k, t_k) \;=\; \frac{m_{\text{L}}}{m}\,G_{\text{L}} \;+\; \frac{m_{\text{R}}}{m}\,G_{\text{R}}$$

Greedy, top down, one node at a time, until every leaf is pure or a limit stops it. criterion="entropy", $-\sum_k p_k \log_2 p_k$, ranks splits almost identically; we leave the default.

The measurement this lecture turns on

Measured in the notebook, before anything is averaged Refit the same depth-8 tree, with the same hyperparameters and the same seed, on twenty different 90% subsamples of the same training rows. Test accuracy moves by a standard deviation of 0.25 points — and 9.1% of the individual predictions change. Only 71.4% of test patches get the same answer from all twenty.

A stable metric, an unstable model. This is variance: the sensitivity of the fitted function to the particular training sample it happened to get.

The whole of today follows from one question: what does averaging do to variance?

Why the members are trees

An ensemble aggregates many models, and the book’s promise is that enough sufficiently diverse weak learners make a strong one. “Sufficiently diverse” is about to get a symbol, a formula and a measured value. First, the members.

  • Averaging attacks variance, and trees are the high-variance model we have just measured: 9.1% of predictions move when 10% of the rows do
  • A tree is cheap to fit, so two hundred of them are affordable
  • An unconstrained tree has low bias — and averaging does nothing whatever to bias, so the members had better not have any to spare
That third point is why the ensembles in this lecture use unconstrained members. It looks reckless after an opening spent capping the depth at 8, and by the end of the mathematics it will look obvious.

Credit scoring, fraud flags, churn, click prediction, risk scores in medicine, nearly every tabular competition: the same shape of data, and on it a forest or a gradient-boosted ensemble is the first model a practitioner fits.

Not images, text or sequences: a pixel or a token is not a column with a meaning of its own, which is why Parts II–IV exist.

Start with two, then take $n$

Forget trees for a moment. You have $n$ predictors of the same quantity, each with variance $\sigma^2$, and you average them. With $\operatorname{Var}(f_1) = \operatorname{Var}(f_2) = \sigma^2$ and $\operatorname{Corr}(f_1, f_2) = \rho$:

$$\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\tfrac{f_1 + f_2}{2}\right) = \tfrac{1}{4}\left[\sigma^2 + \sigma^2 + 2\rho\sigma^2\right] = \frac{\sigma^2(1+\rho)}{2}$$

$\rho = 0$ halves the variance; $\rho = 1$ leaves it at $\sigma^2$, and averaging achieves nothing. For $n$ identically distributed predictors with a common pairwise correlation $\rho$, the same expansion — $n$ variances and $n(n-1)$ covariances $\rho\sigma^2$, over $n^2$ — gives

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar{f}) \;=\; \rho\sigma^2 \;+\; \frac{(1-\rho)\,\sigma^2}{n}$$
Animation

The same formula, plotted

Four curves, four floors. The floor is set entirely by $\rho$ — which is why every ensemble variant you are about to meet is an attempt to lower it.

What this says before we build anything

  1. Averaging many models reduces variance, but only the part they do not share
  2. The return on more members collapses quickly. There is little point past the point where $(1-\rho)\sigma^2/n \ll \rho\sigma^2$
  3. Therefore the way to improve an ensemble is not more members. It is less correlated members
  4. And anything that lowers $\rho$ by making individual members worse is a trade, not a free lunch: $\sigma^2$ appears in both terms, so it can rise while $\rho$ falls

Point 4 is the one that gets forgotten, and it is the one we will measure — and be embarrassed by — later today.

Voting — the simplest ensemble

Combine different algorithms fitted to the same data.


from sklearn.ensemble import VotingClassifier

vote = VotingClassifier(
    [("tree",   DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=42)),
     ("logreg", LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)),
     ("knn",    KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5))], voting="soft")
  • voting="hard" — majority of the predicted labels
  • voting="soft" — average of the predicted probabilities; usually better, because a confident member outweighs several hesitant ones

Different algorithms make different kinds of errors, which is the informal way of saying $\rho$ is small. The formula applies unchanged.

Bagging

Bootstrap aggregating. Draw $m$ rows from the training set with replacement, fit a tree, and repeat $n$ times.

  • Each bootstrap sample has the same size as the training set, but about 63% of its distinct rows — the rest are duplicates
  • Aggregate by majority vote for classification, by the mean for regression
  • Each member is individually worse than one tree fitted to the whole set. The ensemble is better — that is the formula, not a paradox

Pasting is the same thing without replacement. It produces less diverse members, so bagging usually wins — but it is a cheap thing to cross-validate rather than assume.

The members are unconstrained trees. We want each one to have low bias, because averaging cannot remove bias — and we are about to average the variance away, so there is no reason to restrict their depth.

A validation set you get for nothing

Each member never sees about 37% of the rows — its out-of-bag instances. Score each member on its own out-of-bag rows and you have a generalisation estimate with no validation split and no extra fit.

Why 37% A given row is missed by one draw with probability $1 - 1/m$, so by all $m$ draws with probability $\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{m}\right)^{m} \to e^{-1} \approx 0.368$ as $m$ grows. Hence about 63% distinct rows in, 37% held out.

Cheap, and it does not replace the test set. It replaces the validation set — and it estimates one member’s procedure, not the ensemble you ship. With bootstrap=False there are no out-of-bag rows and oob_score=True raises. If it comes out much better than the test score, something in your pipeline saw the out-of-bag rows anyway.

Random forests

Bagging, plus one more source of difference: at every node of every tree, only a random subset of the features is considered for the split.


from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier

rnd = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, max_features="sqrt",
                             random_state=42, n_jobs=-1).fit(X_train, y_train)

max_features="sqrt" is the classification default: $\lfloor\sqrt{54}\rfloor = 7$ of the 54 columns, drawn afresh at each node. A tree with 200 nodes therefore draws 200 different subsets.

It runs, it is fast, and the top of the list is entirely sensible: elevation first, then the distances. Nothing invites suspicion.

Boosting — a different idea entirely

Everything so far trains members in parallel and averages them. Boosting trains them in sequence, each one correcting its predecessor.

The members are not identically distributed and are certainly not exchangeable, so today’s formula does not apply to boosting. Bagging reduces variance; boosting reduces bias.

Two consequences: boosting cannot be parallelised across members, and it is much easier to overfit with. Both follow from the sequence.

AdaBoost

  1. Fit a weak learner — typically a tree of depth 1, a decision stump
  2. Increase the weight of the instances it got wrong
  3. Fit the next learner on the reweighted data; repeat
  4. Predict by a weighted vote, each learner weighted by its own accuracy

from sklearn.ensemble import AdaBoostClassifier

ada = AdaBoostClassifier(DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=1),
                         n_estimators=200, learning_rate=0.5,
                         random_state=42)

If it overfits, reduce n_estimators or learning_rate, or regularise the base estimator.

Animation

Gradient boosting

Same sequence, different correction. Instead of reweighting the instances, fit each new learner to the residual errors of the ensemble so far.

  • learning_rate scales each member’s contribution. A low rate needs more members and generalises better — this is shrinkage
  • Too few members underfits; too many overfits. Use early stopping with n_iter_no_change
  • The number of members is therefore not a “more is better” parameter, which is exactly the opposite of bagging
When it bites Adding trees to a bagged ensemble can only help, because $(1-\rho)\sigma^2/n$ falls monotonically. Adding trees to a boosted ensemble eventually hurts. Carrying the first intuition into the second is one of the commonest mistakes with these models.

What practitioners actually reach for

HistGradientBoostingClassifier bins each feature into at most 255 buckets before growing anything, so a split search costs $O(\text{bins})$ rather than $O(m)$.


from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier

hgb = HistGradientBoostingClassifier(max_iter=100, learning_rate=0.05,
                                     early_stopping=False,
                                     random_state=42).fit(X_train, y_train)
  • Dramatically faster on a dataset this size, and it is the same algorithm LightGBM and XGBoost implement
  • Handles missing values and categorical columns natively — no imputer, no encoder, up to max_bins (255) categories per column
  • On tabular data it is the usual first choice, and on most benchmarks it is the strongest single model

On this dataset, at a budget that fits a lecture, it lands several points below the bagged forest. The notebook measures both, and measures what happens when you raise the learning rate. Usual is not always.

A forest in practice: the recipe that usually works


rf  = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, max_features="sqrt",
                             oob_score=True, n_jobs=-1, random_state=42)
hgb = HistGradientBoostingClassifier(learning_rate=0.05, max_iter=500,
                                     early_stopping=True, random_state=42)
  • No scaling; HistGradientBoosting takes missing values and categories as they are
  • A forest: more members never hurt, so set $n$ by the budget and read the out-of-bag score as your validation
  • Boosting: tune learning_rate, let early stopping pick the number of rounds
  • Imbalance: class_weight="balanced", and per-class recall beside the accuracy
  • Then the honest part, unchanged since Lecture 2: cross-validate the choice, read the test set once

Design exercise · in the manner of an ML system-design interview

The task: the probability that a user clicks an Instagram ad

The brief Every time a feed is loaded, an auction chooses which ad fills the slot. For each candidate (user, ad, context) the system must estimate, in a few milliseconds, the probability of a click. The auction ranks candidates by bid × probability, so the number has to be a probability, not a score: a model that ranks well but misprices every bid loses money on every impression.
  • Given: the log of past impressions — who saw which ad, where, when, and whether a click followed — plus what is known about the user, the ad and the moment
  • Scale: billions of impressions a day; clicks are rare, of the order of one impression in a hundred
  • Consumers of the output: the auction, budget pacing, and the advertiser’s reports

Nine steps follow, each with the pitfall that catches a first attempt and what to do about it. The model at the centre is today’s: an ensemble of trees on a table.

Step 1 — Frame it honestly: what is predicted, and how it is scored

  • One row per impression; the label is click within the attribution window; the output is $p(\text{click} \mid \text{user, ad, context})$
  • The metric is a proper scoring rule — log loss — read beside its anchor, the log loss of always predicting the base rate; AUC beside it for the ranking, never instead
  • The baseline before any model: the ad’s own historical click rate, shrunk towards its campaign’s when it has few impressions
Pitfall With one click in a hundred, “no click” is right ninety-nine times out of a hundred, so accuracy says nothing, and a model can have a splendid AUC while every probability it emits is twice too large. Score with log loss and a calibration plot, against the base-rate anchor, or the first number you report will be meaningless.

Step 2 — The label is not as clean as it looks

PitfallWhat happensWhat to do
Delayed feedbacka click can arrive minutes or hours after the impression; a row logged too early is a negative that is not onelabel only impressions older than the attribution window, or model the delay explicitly
Invalid trafficbots and accidental taps are clicks in the log and not in the worldfilter with the platform’s invalid-traffic rules before anything is fitted
Unseen impressionsan ad logged below the fold was never a candidate for a clickkeep viewable impressions only, and say so in the definition

Write the label’s definition down before the first fit. Every later number is a number about that label.

Step 3 — Split before you look, and split by time

  • Train on the days up to a cut, validate on the next day, test on the day after that — the order the system will actually face
  • Choose everything — features, rounds, calibration — on the validation day; read the test day once
Pitfall A random split puts the same user, the same campaign and the same hour on both sides. The model learns a campaign’s clicks of one evening and is scored on the next morning’s impressions of it: the offline score climbs, and the gain vanishes the day the model goes live. Split by time, and keep the gap between training and test at least as long as the attribution window, or the delayed labels leak across it too.

Step 4 — Features: let the trees find the interactions

  • User: coarse demographics, past behaviour as counts and rates — impressions, clicks, time since the last click
  • Ad: campaign, advertiser, creative type, its own click history; context: position in the feed, time of day, device, connection
  • No scaling, no manual crosses: a path through a boosted tree is “this advertiser × this device × this hour”, found rather than written down — today’s argument for the model family
Pitfall — leakage through a feature “The ad’s click rate” computed over the whole log contains the row’s own click and every click after it. Compute every history feature as of the impression’s moment, from the past only, with the same code that will compute it when the ad is served — a feature the serving system cannot produce in time is not a feature.

Step 5 — One click in a hundred: sample, then put the probabilities back

  • Billions of rows, almost all negatives: keep every click and a random fraction of the non-clicks, and training fits in the budget
  • A model fitted on that sample sees too many clicks, so its probabilities are too high by a known amount: shift its log-odds back by the log of the sampling rate, or re-fit the calibration on an unsampled slice
Pitfall class_weight="balanced" fixes the ranking and wrecks the probabilities: it tells the model that clicks are as common as non-clicks, and the auction then overbids on everything. Reweighting is a tool for a classifier that outputs a label; this system outputs a price.

Step 6 — The model: a boosted ensemble of trees, then early stopping

  • Tabular rows, mixed types, interactions nobody can write down, tens of millions of rows even after sampling: the case for an ensemble of trees from an hour ago, exactly
  • Gradient boosting over bagging here: shallow members are cheap to evaluate, and the model is evaluated billions of times a day; a forest of two hundred deep trees is two hundred paths per impression
  • Tune the learning rate on the validation day and let early stopping choose the number of rounds; the ablation table is the record of what each feature family bought
Pitfall More rounds always lower the training loss and, past a point, raise the next day’s: the opposite of bagging, where more members never hurt. Stop on the validation day’s log loss, not on the training curve.

What the large platforms do next — the trees as feature generators for a linear or neural layer — starts from this model and belongs to Part II.

Step 7 — Calibration: the auction needs the number to mean what it says

  • Bucket the validation day by predicted probability and compare each bucket’s prediction with its observed click rate: the reliability diagram, now a business requirement
  • If it bends, fit a calibration layer — isotonic or Platt — on a slice the model never trained on, and check it per segment: new ads, new users, each device
Pitfall — the cold start A new ad has no history, so every history feature is empty and the tree sends it down the branch for “unknown”. Back it off to its campaign’s and advertiser’s rates, and give new ads a small, deliberate share of impressions so that their own rate can be learned.

Step 8 — The model chooses its own training data

  • The log holds only the impressions the previous model chose to show. An ad it never shows gets no clicks, so the next model never learns to show it: the loop closes on itself
  • Keep a small randomised slice of impressions outside the model’s choice, and train and evaluate on it too
  • Retrain daily; monitor the input distributions and the calibration against the base rate, which drifts with seasons, campaigns and the news
Pitfall An offline gain is a hypothesis. A model that lowers the validation day’s log loss changes which ads are shown, which changes the users, the advertisers and the log. The only test that counts is an A/B test on live traffic, read on revenue and on what users do afterwards, with the budgets of both arms held equal.

Step 9 — Ship it: the pipeline, and the pitfalls in one table

Solutions — Lecture 4

1. Gradient descent on the same data, with the features unscaled, takes many more steps to converge than on…

Unscaled features make the contours elongated, so the gradient points across the valley rather than along it.

  • The Hessian is $X^{\mathsf T}X$, so the curvature along a feature goes as the square of its scale — multiply a column by ten and its curvature rises a hundredfold, which is how one column comes to dominate
  • The learning rate must then suit the steepest direction, which makes it far too small for the shallow one

2. On the Titanic manifest, your logistic regression on one-hot encoded columns produces coefficients that look…

Five come from the encoder, one from you. The repair is both: drop one level per categorical, and delete the engineered column.

  • Each one-hot block sums to 1 in every row, and so does the intercept column — five categoricals, five exact dependencies
  • The sixth is FamilySize = SibSp + Parch + 1, an identity rather than a correlation. Dropping a level alone still leaves the rank one short of the column count, because the dependency you wrote yourself survives it

Solutions — Lecture 4 (2)

3. A model reaches 0.80 accuracy and is badly calibrated. Explain what that means, and name a decision it would…

Its predicted probabilities do not match observed frequencies; any decision using a cost-weighted threshold is then wrong.

  • Accuracy reads only the arg-max, so it is blind to the probability that produced it
  • Choosing a threshold from expected cost requires the probability to mean what it says

4. In a logistic regression with first class as the reference level, the coefficient on Pclass_3 is $-1.169$.…

It multiplies the odds of survival by $e^{-1.169} = 0.31$, relative to first class, holding the other features fixed.

  • The linear part predicts the log-odds, so adding 1 to an indicator adds its coefficient to the log-odds and multiplies the odds by its exponential
  • The probability moves by an amount that depends on where it started: odds of 1 times 0.31 give $p = 0.24$, while $p = 0.9$ becomes 0.74

Solutions — Lecture 4 (3)

5. State the update rule for batch gradient descent, and say what changes in it for stochastic gradient descent…

$\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\nabla J$. Only the set the gradient is computed over changes; the rule does not.

  • SGD estimates the same gradient from one instance rather than all of them
  • The estimate is unbiased — averaged over a uniformly drawn row it is exactly the batch gradient — but noisy, and it costs $O(n)$ per step instead of $O(mn)$
