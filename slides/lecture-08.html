Mathematical thread 4

Impurity, and why
averaging reduces variance

20 minutes

Two questions, in order

  1. CART minimises Gini impurity. Scikit-Learn offers entropy instead. Does the choice matter?
  2. If you average $n$ predictors, what happens to the variance — and what happens when the predictors are not independent?
Why you need the second answer today It is the entire justification for random forests, and it tells you in advance which parts of the variance an ensemble can remove and which it cannot.

Gini impurity

$$G \;=\; 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k^{\,2}$$

$p_k$ is the fraction of the node’s training instances in class $k$. There is a probabilistic reading worth having:

Draw two instances from the node independently, with replacement. $\sum_k p_k^2$ is the probability that they are the same class, so $G$ is the probability that they are different. A pure node never disagrees with itself.

Entropy

$$H \;=\; -\sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k \log_2 p_k \qquad (p_k \ne 0)$$

From Shannon’s information theory: the average number of bits needed to transmit the class of an instance drawn from the node. A pure node needs none, because you already know the answer.

Both measures share the two properties CART needs:

  • zero when the node is pure, and only then
  • maximal when all $K$ classes are equally represented

They are not the same function

On a two-class node with $p_1 = p$, the two are

$$G(p) = 2p(1-p) \qquad\qquad H(p) = -p\log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p)$$

$H$ has maximum 1 and $G$ has maximum $1/2$, so compare $H/2$ with $G$. Both are then $0$ at $p \in \{0,1\}$ and $1/2$ at $p = 1/2$. They agree at three points. Between them they do not.

Two measures of the same idea

Same zeros, same maximum, different shoulders. Entropy penalises a nearly-pure node more heavily than Gini does.

Where the two disagree, and by how much

The largest gap between $H/2$ and $G$ is 0.055, at $p \approx 0.10$ and again at $p \approx 0.90$ — the nearly-pure nodes.

  • Entropy is steeper near the ends, so it is more reluctant to accept a node as “almost pure enough”
  • Géron: entropy tends to produce slightly more balanced trees; Gini is marginally faster because there is no logarithm to evaluate
  • “Most of the time it does not make a big difference”

That last sentence is a claim about behaviour. We have a dataset, a model and ninety minutes. Do not accept it — measure it.

“Statistically detectable” and “worth acting on” are different claims and need different evidence. Twenty paired resamples give you both, and one run gives you neither.

Part two — the variance of an average

Forget trees for ten minutes.

You have $n$ predictors of the same quantity. Each has variance $\sigma^2$. You average them.

$$\bar{f} \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n} f_t$$

What is $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{f})$?

Ask for the answer before showing it. Almost everyone says σ²/n, which is the special case they were taught and is wrong here.

The answer everyone gives first

$$\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_t f_t\right) \;=\; \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_t \operatorname{Var}(f_t) \;=\; \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$

That step is only valid when the $f_t$ are uncorrelated.

The variance of a sum is the sum of the variances plus twice the sum of the covariances. Dropping the covariances is an assumption, and it is exactly the assumption that fails for an ensemble of trees grown on the same data.

Start with two

The textbook does this case for two regressors. With $\operatorname{Var}(f_1) = \operatorname{Var}(f_2) = \sigma^2$ and $\operatorname{Corr}(f_1, f_2) = \rho$:

$$\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\tfrac{f_1 + f_2}{2}\right) = \tfrac{1}{4}\left[\sigma^2 + \sigma^2 + 2\rho\sigma^2\right] = \frac{\sigma^2(1+\rho)}{2}$$
  • $\rho = 0$: variance $\sigma^2/2$ — the familiar halving
  • $\rho = 1$: variance $\sigma^2$ — the two predictors are the same predictor, and averaging achieves nothing

Now $n$ of them

Assume the $n$ predictors are identically distributed — each with variance $\sigma^2$ — and have a common pairwise correlation

$$\operatorname{Corr}(f_s, f_t) \;=\; \rho \quad \text{for all } s \ne t$$

Equivalently $\operatorname{Cov}(f_s, f_t) = \rho\sigma^2$, since both have the same variance.

Identically distributed, not independent. That is the whole point: $n$ trees grown on bootstrap samples of one dataset have the same distribution and are certainly not independent.

Expand the variance of the sum

$$\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\sum_{t=1}^{n} f_t\right) \;=\; \sum_{s=1}^{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n} \operatorname{Cov}(f_s, f_t)$$

There are $n^2$ terms in that double sum. Split them by whether $s = t$:

  • $n$ diagonal terms, each $\operatorname{Cov}(f_t, f_t) = \sigma^2$
  • $n(n-1)$ off-diagonal terms, each $\rho\sigma^2$

Collect and divide by $n^2$

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar{f}) \;=\; \frac{1}{n^2}\Big[\,n\sigma^2 \;+\; n(n-1)\rho\sigma^2\,\Big] \;=\; \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \frac{n-1}{n}\rho\sigma^2$$

Write $\dfrac{n-1}{n} = 1 - \dfrac{1}{n}$ and gather the two $1/n$ terms:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar{f}) \;=\; \rho\sigma^2 \;+\; \frac{(1-\rho)\,\sigma^2}{n}$$

The variance of an average of $n$ identically distributed predictors with pairwise correlation $\rho$

Read the two terms

TermDepends on $n$?What it is
$\rho\sigma^2$nothe part every predictor shares
$(1-\rho)\sigma^2/n$yes, like $1/n$the part each predictor has to itself

Averaging destroys the independent component and leaves the correlated one completely untouched.

Note what this does not say. It says nothing about bias. An average of $n$ biased predictors is exactly as biased as one of them.

The whole thread, in one picture

Everything above the dashed line can be averaged away. Nothing below it can.

Three limits worth memorising

CaseVariance of the averageReading
$\rho = 0$$\sigma^2/n$independent: the classical result
$\rho = 1$$\sigma^2$identical predictors: averaging is a no-op
$n \to \infty$$\rho\sigma^2$the floor — and it is reached quickly

Because the second term falls like $1/n$, most of the available reduction happens in the first few predictors. Going from $n = 1$ to $n = 10$ removes 90% of the independent part; going from 100 to 1,000 removes 0.9% more.

The same formula, plotted

Four curves, four floors. The floor is set entirely by $\rho$ — which is why every ensemble variant you are about to meet is an attempt to lower it.

What this says about ensembles, before we build one

  1. Averaging many models reduces variance, but only the part they do not share
  2. The return on more models collapses quickly. There is little point past the point where $(1-\rho)\sigma^2/n \ll \rho\sigma^2$
  3. Therefore, the way to improve an ensemble is not more members. It is less correlated members
  4. And anything that lowers $\rho$ by making individual members worse is a trade, not a free lunch: $\sigma^2$ can rise while $\rho$ falls

Point 4 is the one that gets forgotten, and it is the one we will measure at the end of the lecture.

Thread 4 · Summary

  • Gini and entropy are both zero exactly at purity, maximal at uniformity, and differ by at most 0.055 in the two-class case
  • Measured, they choose the same root split every time and agree on 85.3% of predictions; the accuracy difference is 0.44 points
  • For identically distributed predictors with pairwise correlation $\rho$, $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{f}) = \rho\sigma^2 + (1-\rho)\sigma^2/n$
  • The first term is a floor no amount of averaging reaches below

Examinable: derive the variance formula from the double sum, and state what each term does as $n$ grows. Thread 3’s bias–variance decomposition told you variance was one of three terms; this tells you what you can do about it.

Why those two results are consistent

  • A tree is a hierarchy. The root split is chosen from 48,000 patches and is decided by a wide margin, so it is stable
  • A node eight levels down was chosen from a few hundred patches, where two candidate splits are often separated by a hair
  • Change one row and the loser becomes the winner — and everything below that node is now a different tree
  • Accuracy is an average over 12,000 patches. Averages hide substitutions: a tree that loses a patch here and gains one there scores the same

A stable metric is not evidence of a stable model. It is evidence that you measured the wrong thing.

Name what we are looking at

This is variance In the sense of the bias–variance decomposition: the sensitivity of the fitted model to the particular training sample. Small changes in the data produce large changes in the function.

Trees have it badly, and for a structural reason: the greedy, hierarchical fitting procedure turns an arbitrarily small change in one split into an arbitrarily large change in the subtree below it. Nothing smooths it out.

The previous lecture’s note about axis-aligned splits — rotate the data and the tree changes completely — was the same fact wearing a different hat.

Why this is the regulator’s problem, not just ours

Go back to what we promised: every prediction comes with a human-readable justification.

What we can now say “This parcel was refused because its elevation is 3,763 m and it is not on soil type 26. Had the survey included ninety more patches from an unrelated valley, the justification — and possibly the answer — would have been different.”

A justification that changes when data the parcel has nothing to do with changes is not a justification. It is a coincidence, written in the imperative.

The diagnosis

  1. The committed number, 73.3%, is correct
  2. It is also uninformative: it is stable to within 0.26 points while 9.0% of the underlying predictions are not
  3. The cause is variance, and the mathematical thread tells us exactly what to do about variance: average

And it tells us the cost in advance. Averaging removes $(1-\rho)\sigma^2/n$ and nothing else — so before we start, we should expect the reduction to stop somewhere, and we should expect to be able to predict where.

The idea

Ask a difficult question of a thousand random people and aggregate their answers. The aggregate is often better than an expert’s. The same is true of models, and it has a name: an ensemble.

A group of weak learners can form a strong learner — provided there are enough of them and they are sufficiently diverse.

“Sufficiently diverse” is the informal name of $\rho$. You now have the formula that makes it precise, twenty minutes before you need it.

Where do many different trees come from?

CART is deterministic: same data, same tree. To get twenty different trees you must give them twenty different things to look at.

Source of differenceName
Different training algorithms on the same dataa voting classifier
The same algorithm on different random subsets of rows, drawn with replacementbagging
… drawn without replacementpasting
… plus random subsets of columns at each splitrandom forest
… plus random thresholdsextra-trees

The list is in increasing order of how hard it works to make two members unlike each other. That ordering is the plan for the next twenty minutes.

Bagging

Bootstrap aggregating. Draw $m$ rows from the training set with replacement, fit a tree, and repeat $n$ times.

  • Each bootstrap sample has the same size as the training set, but about 63% of its distinct rows — the rest are duplicates
  • Aggregate by majority vote for classification, by the mean for regression
  • Each tree is individually worse than one fitted to the whole set. The ensemble is better

Pasting is the same thing without replacement. It produces less diverse members, so bagging usually wins — but it is a cheap thing to cross-validate.

Bagging, in code


from sklearn.ensemble import BaggingClassifier

bag = BaggingClassifier(
    DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=42),   # unconstrained members
    n_estimators=200, bootstrap=True, oob_score=True,
    random_state=42, n_jobs=-1)

bag.fit(X_train, y_train)                      # about 40 s
print(f"out-of-bag {bag.oob_score_:.1%}")

The members are unconstrained trees. We want each one to have low bias and we are about to average the variance away, so there is no reason to restrict their depth.

A free validation set

Each member never sees about 37% of the rows — its out-of-bag instances. Evaluate each member on its own out-of-bag rows and you get a generalisation estimate without a validation split and without a single extra fit.

Why 37% A given row is missed by one draw with probability $1 - 1/m$, so by all $m$ draws with probability $(1 - 1/m)^m \to e^{-1} \approx 0.368$ as $m$ grows.

Cheap, and it does not replace the test set. It replaces the validation set.

Random forests

Bagging, plus one more source of difference: at every node of every tree, only a random subset of the features is considered for the split.


from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier

rnd = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, max_features="sqrt",
                             random_state=42, n_jobs=-1)
rnd.fit(X_train, y_train)                      # about 25 s

max_features="sqrt" is the classification default: $\lfloor\sqrt{54}\rfloor = 7$ of the 54 columns, drawn afresh at each node. A tree with 200 nodes therefore draws 200 different subsets.

Extra-trees

One further step. Having chosen the candidate features, do not search for the best threshold — draw one at random for each candidate and keep the best of those.

  • Much faster: the threshold search is the expensive part of training a tree
  • More diverse members, each individually worse
  • Scikit-Learn’s default is bootstrap=False — the random thresholds replace the bootstrap rather than joining it

That default is a decision somebody else made, it is not what most descriptions of extra-trees imply, and we are going to measure what it costs.

What each variant randomises

Rows, columns, thresholds. Three knobs, and all three are turned for the same reason.

The claim this lecture is really making

One formula, three algorithms Bagging randomises the rows. Random forests also randomise the columns. Extra-trees also randomise the thresholds. All three exist to reduce one quantity: $\rho$, the correlation term in $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{f}) = \rho\sigma^2 + (1-\rho)\sigma^2/n$.

That is the payoff of the thread, and it is the thing to remember if you remember one thing from this application.

It is also a claim, and this course does not accept claims. Measure it.

Measuring $\rho$ needs something we do not have

$\rho$ is the correlation between two members over the randomness of the whole procedure — which includes which training set you were handed. Two trees grown on one dataset are correlated through that dataset.

So a single training set cannot show it. Conditional on the data, the members are independent by construction and $\rho$ would measure as zero.

What the experiment needs Several independent training sets. CoverType has 581,012 rows, so we can cut 20 disjoint training sets of 20,000 rows — 400,000 rows in all, no row reused — and still keep 12,000 rows aside as a shared test set.

The design


for k in range(20):                        # 20 disjoint training sets
    idx = pool[k * 20_000:(k + 1) * 20_000]
    model = make_ensemble(kind, n=20, seed=1000 + k).fit(X[idx], y[idx])
    for j, member in enumerate(model.estimators_):
        S[k, j] = (classes[member.predict(X_test)] == y_test)
# S has shape (20 training sets, 20 members, 12,000 test patches)

$S_{k,j,i} = 1$ when member $j$ of ensemble $k$ is right about patch $i$. Two indices of randomness: which training set, and which member given the set. That is exactly the split the formula needs.

Splitting the variance in two

At each test patch, decompose the variance of a single member across all 400 fitted trees:

$$\sigma^2 \;=\; \underbrace{\tau^2}_{\text{between training sets}} \;+\; \underbrace{w^2}_{\text{within a training set}} \qquad\qquad \rho \;=\; \frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2 + w^2}$$
  • $w^2$: how much two members of the same ensemble differ
  • $\tau^2$: how much the ensembles differ from one another — the part every member of a given ensemble inherits from its data
  • $\tau^2$ is the floor $\rho\sigma^2$. That is not a coincidence; it is the same quantity written twice

One trap in that code, worth ten minutes of your life

The honest reading of that table

  • Feature subsampling is doing the work here. It cuts $\rho$ by 40%, from 0.072 to 0.043
  • Random thresholds are a substitute for the bootstrap, not an addition to it. Extra-trees without a bootstrap lands at 0.067, near bagging; with one, at 0.043, exactly where the forest is
  • There is a floor to the floor. Nothing we tried gets below 0.043, because all four ensembles ultimately saw the same 20,000 rows
That last point is the formula’s real message. $\rho$ is not a property of the algorithm. It is the share of the variance that comes from which data you were given, and no amount of algorithmic randomness reaches it.

That is not a contradiction — read the formula again

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar{f}) \;=\; \rho\,\boldsymbol{\sigma^2} \;+\; \frac{(1-\rho)\,\boldsymbol{\sigma^2}}{n}$$
  • The formula is about variance. It says nothing about bias, and accuracy depends on both
  • Every mechanism that lowers $\rho$ does so by handicapping the members. A tree allowed only 7 of 54 columns per node is a worse tree: 73.6% against bagging’s 78.8%
  • $\sigma^2$ appears in both terms. Lowering $\rho$ while raising $\sigma^2$ can leave you worse off

On this dataset, 54 columns of which perhaps a dozen carry most of the signal, throwing away 47 of them at every node costs more than the decorrelation is worth. On a dataset with hundreds of weakly informative columns it is usually the other way round — which is why the default exists.

What the thread actually bought us

  • It predicted the shape of the accuracy curve — steep, then flat, with the knee where $(1-\rho)\sigma^2/n$ falls below $\rho\sigma^2$
  • It predicted the variance of a 20-member average to three decimal places, on four different ensembles
  • It told us in advance that more members would stop helping, and roughly when
  • It did not tell us which ensemble would be most accurate, and it never claimed to

A theory that predicts three things and declines to predict the fourth is not a weak theory. A theory that predicts all four without being asked is usually being misread.

A column of uniform random numbers. It cannot possibly carry information about which trees grow where. Where does it rank among the 55?

Ask for a guess. "Last" is the usual answer, and it is wrong by forty-five places.

Tenth of fifty-five

10 / 55

The random column ranks above 45 of the 54 real measurements, with an impurity importance of 0.0399 — against 0.2277 for elevation.

Put that list in front of a regulator and you have asserted that a column of noise matters more than 45 things the surveyors actually recorded.

Why impurity importance does that

feature_importances_ sums, over every node that split on a feature, the weighted impurity reduction that split achieved. Two biases follow directly:

  • It is measured on the training data. A split that reduces impurity on the rows that chose it will do so whether or not the feature is informative
  • It favours high-cardinality features. A continuous column offers thousands of candidate thresholds; a 0/1 soil-type column offers one. More chances to find a lucky split means more accumulated impurity reduction

Our decoy is continuous and 40 of the 54 real columns are binary. The ranking is not a mystery once you know what is being summed.

The measurement that has a control built in

Shuffle one column of the held-out set, re-score, and see how much accuracy falls. Repeat, and report a spread.


from sklearn.inspection import permutation_importance

r = permutation_importance(rnd, X_te2, y_test, n_repeats=5,
                           random_state=42, n_jobs=-1)   # about 90 s
Not examinable permutation_importance goes beyond Chapter 6. It is here because the alternative is to ship the biased measurement, and because the idea — break the column and see what breaks — is worth ten minutes of anyone’s time.

The corrected specification

What to ask for instead “Report feature importance by permutation_importance on the test set with n_repeats=5, and print the standard deviation beside every mean. The other way to build an ensemble

Instead of many copies of one algorithm, combine different algorithms fitted to the same data.


from sklearn.ensemble import VotingClassifier

vote = VotingClassifier([("tree", DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=42)),
                         ("forest", rnd), ("bag", bag)], voting="soft")
  • voting="hard" — majority of the predicted labels
  • voting="soft" — average of the predicted probabilities; usually better, because a confident member outweighs several hesitant ones

Same principle, same formula. Different algorithms make different kinds of errors, which is another way of saying $\rho$ is small.

Boosting — a different idea entirely

Everything so far trains members in parallel and averages them. Boosting trains them in sequence, each one correcting its predecessor.

The members are not identically distributed and are certainly not exchangeable, so today’s formula does not apply to boosting. Boosting reduces bias, where bagging reduces variance.

The consequence: boosting cannot be parallelised across members, and it is much easier to overfit with.

AdaBoost

  1. Fit a weak learner — typically a tree of depth 1, a decision stump
  2. Increase the weight of the instances it got wrong
  3. Fit the next learner on the reweighted data; repeat
  4. Predict by a weighted vote, each learner weighted by its own accuracy

from sklearn.ensemble import AdaBoostClassifier

ada = AdaBoostClassifier(DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=1),
                         n_estimators=200, learning_rate=0.5,
                         random_state=42)

If it overfits, reduce n_estimators or learning_rate, or regularise the base estimator.

Gradient boosting

Same sequence, different correction. Instead of reweighting the instances, fit each new learner to the residual errors of the ensemble so far.

  • learning_rate scales each member’s contribution. A low rate needs more members and generalises better — this is shrinkage
  • Too few members underfits; too many overfits. Use early stopping with n_iter_no_change
  • HistGradientBoostingClassifier bins the features first and is dramatically faster on a dataset this size; it also handles missing values and categorical columns natively

On tabular data of this shape, gradient boosting is usually the strongest single thing you can reach for. Try it in the notebook and see whether it beats 89.8%.

Stacking

Rather than voting or averaging, train a model to do the aggregation. The base learners' predictions become the input features of a final estimator, the blender.

  • The blender must be trained on predictions the base learners made for instances they did not see — otherwise it is learning from memorised answers
  • StackingClassifier does this with cross-validation internally, which is the only reason it is safe to use

Reviewer question 1, applied to an ensemble: what touched the data the blender learns from? If you build stacking by hand, that question is the whole difficulty.

What we learned

  1. Gini and entropy pick the same root split every time here, agree on 85.3% of predictions, and differ by 0.44 points. Real, and not worth tuning
  2. $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{f}) = \rho\sigma^2 + (1-\rho)\sigma^2/n$. Averaging destroys the independent component and cannot touch the correlated one
  3. The tree’s accuracy was stable to 0.26 points while 9.0% of its predictions were not. A stable metric is not a stable model
  4. Bagging, feature subsampling and random thresholds all attack $\rho$ — measured, from 0.072 down to 0.043 — but they do not stack
  5. Lower $\rho$ did not mean higher accuracy, because every mechanism that lowers it also weakens the members
  6. We bought 15.1 points of accuracy with 1,497,919 leaves, and lost the thing the regulator asked for first
