Part one, continued

The corpus

and exactly how big it is

COCO

  • Common Objects in Context — the standard detection benchmark, and the set these pretrained weights were trained on
  • 80 object categories, drawn by hand, in everyday scenes rather than studio shots
  • Every object carries a box, a category, and a polygon outline
  • The validation split, val2017, is 5,000 images

The textbook names COCO as the dataset the pretrained detection and segmentation models in this chapter are trained on.

Our corpus is 128 images. Not COCO

128

images, out of 5,000 in val2017

  • The 128 numerically lowest image ids — a rule, not a selection
  • About 20 MB of JPEG, plus the official annotation file
  • Full COCO with the training split is roughly 20 GB and is not downloaded anywhere in this course

Every number in these two lectures is measured on 128 images. When we say a score, we say the sample size beside it.

This slide is not administrative. It is the honesty clause, and it is examinable behaviour rather than examinable content: a measurement quoted without its sample size is not a measurement.

Why not all of it

  • Time. A hall of laptops cannot each pull 20 GB inside a lecture
  • Honesty is cheaper than size. A detector scored on 128 images is a perfectly good lesson if the slide says 128
  • It is a dishonest lesson the moment the slide implies 5,000
The rule you are being taught Report the denominator. Always. An estimate with an unstated sample size cannot be checked, cannot be reproduced, and is worth nothing to whoever reads it next — including you, in three months.

The eight annotations we threw away

iscrowd = 1 When a scene contains too many instances to draw separately — a stadium of people, a bowl of apples — the annotator draws one region around all of them and sets a flag. It is not one object, and it is not $n$ objects. It is a refusal to decide.
  • Eight of the 906 annotations in our corpus are crowd regions
  • COCO's own evaluator ignores them, and so do we
  • Dropping them is a choice, it changes the counts, and this slide is where it is recorded

Twelve categories carry most of it

39.0% of every annotated object in this corpus is a person. Remember that when we start averaging over categories.

“Ground truth” is a person with a mouse

  • Someone decided where the object ends and the shadow begins
  • Someone decided that a handbag half behind a leg is one object
  • Someone decided that a row of children is thirteen boxes and then a crowd region
Keep this in your pocket Every disagreement you will measure today is a disagreement between two opinions, one of which happens to be written in a file. That does not make the metric useless — it makes the metric a comparison rather than a verdict.

Part two

Choose a metric,
then commit to a number

15 minutes

What can we actually measure today?

Not “what would be nice”. What can be computed from a predicted box and a true box, this afternoon, by you.

  • Whether the two boxes overlap at all
  • Whether the predicted centre falls inside the true box
  • The distance between the two centres
  • How many objects were predicted against how many are there
Take these from the room rather than reading them out. You will get all four within a minute, usually in this order. Write them on the board; you will cross three of them out in the next four slides.

Take the first one seriously

Proposed metric “A detection is correct when its box overlaps the true box.” Score the system by the fraction of true objects that some predicted box overlaps.

It is computable, it is unambiguous, and it needs no parameters. Before adopting it, do what this course does before adopting anything: find out what the stupidest possible system scores.

The trivial baseline for detection

predict exactly one box per image: $[0,\;0,\;W,\;H]$ the whole image
  • No network. No weights. No data
  • It is the detection analogue of “predict the majority class” — and unlike that one, it is not obviously hopeless

Score it under the proposed metric before you go further.

One box, covering everything

Every true object intersects the whole image, so the overlap metric gives a system that has learned nothing a perfect score.

Do not explain the 0.086 yet. Someone will ask what "overlap over union" means; say "that is the next lecture" and move on. The point here is only that the proposed metric is unusable.

The other two proposals, quickly

Only the last one punishes the baseline, and it does so because it is the only one of the four that notices the box is doing the work of twenty boxes.

So today's metric is counting.

The metric, defined so two people get the same number

$$\text{MAE}_{\text{count}} \;=\; \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} \bigl|\, \hat n_i - n_i \,\bigr|$$ $M = 128$ images; $n_i$ true objects; $\hat n_i$ predicted
  • Mean absolute error, so overcounting and undercounting do not cancel
  • We will also report the signed mean, because the direction of the error is a different fact from its size
  • Crowd regions excluded from $n_i$, as recorded four slides ago

Your anchor, before you write anything down

The second row is a system that never looks at the image. The third is a system that looks at the corpus and not the image. Both are computed from the annotations alone, before any model runs.

A commitment made without these two numbers is a wish.

What you are not committing to

You are committing to a count. You are not committing to anything about the boxes themselves, because there is currently no way to say it.

Hold this thought until minute 80 If the system draws the right number of boxes in the wrong places, this metric says it is perfect.

Step 1 · Specify, before anything is generated

Input128 JPEG files and one COCO annotation file
Outputper image: boxes in corner form, category ids, scores; plus one integer count
Constraintnothing is trained; the pretrained weights are used as they ship
Checkevery predicted box lies inside the image; scores are non-increasing; the count equals the number of boxes kept

The Check row is the one people skip, and it is the one that catches a corner-versus-size mix-up in one line.

Step 2 · Load the corpus, and convert the boxes once

import json, numpy as np

raw = json.loads(ANN_PATH.read_text())
images = sorted(raw["images"], key=lambda i: i["id"])[:128]
ids = {im["id"] for im in images}

gt = {i: {"boxes": [], "labels": []} for i in ids}
n_crowd = 0
for a in raw["annotations"]:
    if a["image_id"] not in ids:
        continue
    if a["iscrowd"]:                      # a refusal to decide
        n_crowd += 1
        continue
    x, y, w, h = a["bbox"]                # COCO gives x, y, w, h
    gt[a["image_id"]]["boxes"].append([x, y, x + w, y + h])
    gt[a["image_id"]]["labels"].append(a["category_id"])

The conversion happens once, at the edge of the program. From line 17 onward every box in memory is corners.

Step 3 · Assert it, do not hope

for iid, g in gt.items():
    b = np.asarray(g["boxes"], dtype=float).reshape(-1, 4)
    assert (b[:, 2] >= b[:, 0]).all(), "x2 < x1 — you read w as x2"
    assert (b[:, 3] >= b[:, 1]).all(), "y2 < y1 — same bug"
    gt[iid]["boxes"] = b

total = sum(len(g["boxes"]) for g in gt.values())
assert len(gt) == 128
assert total == 898, total
print(f"{len(gt)} images, {total} objects, {n_crowd} crowd regions dropped")

Reading w as x2 gives x2 < x1 for every box wider than its own left edge, which is almost all of them. Two lines catch it; nothing else will.

Step 4 · The detector, off the shelf

import torch
from torchvision.models.detection import (
    fasterrcnn_resnet50_fpn, FasterRCNN_ResNet50_FPN_Weights)

weights = FasterRCNN_ResNet50_FPN_Weights.COCO_V1
model = fasterrcnn_resnet50_fpn(weights=weights)
model.eval().to(DEVICE)                   # eval(), every time
preprocess = weights.transforms()

print(len(weights.meta["categories"]), "label slots")
print(weights.meta["_metrics"])

Step 9 · There is already a cut-off, and it is not yours

  • box_score_thresh defaults to 0.05: anything below is never returned at all
  • box_nms_thresh defaults to 0.5: near duplicates are already removed before you see them
  • box_detections_per_img defaults to 100
Reviewer question 5 What is the default I did not ask for? Three of them, and one is a hard cap you will hit: our busiest image returns exactly 100 boxes.

Step 10 · The count is a function of the threshold

Nothing in the model chose 0.5. Move the cut-off and the answer to the stakeholder's question changes by a factor of ten.

Pick one, and write down why

Our operating point 0.5, because it is the conventional reporting threshold and because it was chosen before looking at the error curve.

The minimum of the right-hand panel is at 0.75, with MAE 1.92. Adopting that would be choosing a hyperparameter on the same data you then report — Lecture 2's error, in a new costume. We will quote it as a diagnostic and not as a result.

Someone always asks why we do not just use 0.75. This is the answer, and it is worth two extra minutes: the 128 images are the only data we have, so tuning on them and reporting on them is the same set twice.

Step 11 · Count the objects

THRESH = 0.5

n_pred = np.array([int((preds[im["id"]]["scores"] >= THRESH).sum())

Two numbers, not one. The absolute error says how far off; the signed error says which way.

Beat 4 · The corrected code

def count_objects(pred, thresh):
    """Objects detected at or above `thresh`. Never call it with the
    default; there is no sensible default."""
    keep = pred["scores"] >= thresh
    assert keep.sum() <= len(pred["scores"])
    return int(keep.sum())

n_pred = np.array([count_objects(preds[im["id"]], 0.5) for im in images])
print(f"model    {np.abs(n_pred - n_true).mean():.2f}")
print(f"baseline {np.abs(np.ones(128) - n_true).mean():.2f}")
assert np.abs(n_pred - n_true).mean() < 6.02, "worse than one box per image"

Three faults, and nothing to say about them

  • A box that is the right object, slightly too large — is that a success?
  • Two boxes on one bottle — is that two successes, one success and one failure, or one failure?
  • A box that covers a bottle and its neighbour — which of the two did it detect?

Every one of those questions is the same question: how much of the right thing is inside this box, and how much of this box is the right thing?

What such a number would have to do

  1. Be 1 when the boxes coincide and 0 when they do not touch
  2. Punish a box for being too large, or the whole-image baseline wins again
  3. Punish a box for being too small, or a one-pixel box in the right place wins
  4. Have no units, so that a 40-pixel cup and a 400-pixel sofa are on the same scale

Four requirements. One quantity satisfies all four, and you have already seen its value on this deck once, without a name.

Slide 24, row two: 0.086. If nobody spots it, do not point it out — leave it for the opening of the next lecture.

Specimen exam question — Part A

A detector is evaluated on a corpus of images. Its only reported metric is the mean absolute error in the number of objects detected per image, and it scores 3.00.

1. State one system that would score better than 3.00 without locating anything correctly.
2. State what the metric fails to measure, in one sentence.

Three marks. Worked answer on the next slide.

Specimen answer — Part A

  • 1. Any system that emits the corpus mean number of boxes, in fixed positions, for every image. On our corpus that scores 4.88 — better than a baseline of one box, worse than the detector, and it never looks at the image at all
  • 2. It measures how many objects were found and nothing about where; nine boxes in the wrong places score exactly as well as nine in the right places

The common wrong answer to part 1 is “a perfect detector”. Read the question: it asks for one that locates nothing correctly.

Specimen exam question — Part B

A COCO annotation gives a box as [100, 50, 20, 30]. A torchvision detector predicts [100, 50, 120, 80] for the same object.

1. Are these the same box? Justify in one line.
2. A colleague compares them directly and reports a mismatch. State the single line of code that should have been written, and where.

Two marks.
