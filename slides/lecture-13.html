What a pretrained backbone has actually learned

Not “flowers”. A hierarchy, and only the bottom of it is general:

DepthWhat the filters respond toTransfers?
first blockedges, colour opponency, simple gradientsto essentially anything
middle blockstextures, repeated motifs, part-like shapesto most natural images
last blocksobject-specific configurationsonly to similar objects
the headthe 1,000 ImageNet classesnot at all — it is replaced

Which is the whole recipe: keep the bottom, adapt the top, replace the head. Freezing is not a compromise forced by compute — it is a statement that those layers already encode what you would otherwise be trying to learn from 1,020 images.

The diagnosis

One sentence We asked 1,020 labelled images to pay for 4,807,494 parameters, most of which encode facts about photographs in general rather than facts about flowers.

The repair is therefore not a better architecture, a longer run or a cleverer schedule. It is starting from weights that already work.

Which backbone?

FamilyParamsUse it when

The rule that matters more than the family A bigger backbone is not automatically better on a small corpus. You are fitting a head on frozen features; more features means more parameters in the head and more chance to overfit 1,020 images. Start small, and grow it only if the validation curve says the features are the bottleneck.

not examinable — engineering The names change every year. The trade-off does not.

Two decisions, in this order

  1. Linear probe. Freeze everything, extract features once, fit a linear classifier. Minutes, and it tells you whether the backbone understands your images at all
  2. Fine-tune. Unfreeze the last block or two, small learning rate, and only if the probe was promising
Why the order is not arbitrary The probe is a measurement of the features, uncontaminated by anything you do to them. If it barely beats the trivial baseline, the features are wrong for this domain and fine-tuning is polishing the wrong object. If it is already close, fine-tuning has little left to buy and may cost you generalisation.

Freezing weights does not freeze a network

requires_grad = False stops the gradients. It does not stop batch normalisation updating its running mean and variance, which happens in the forward pass and has nothing to do with autograd.

The condition A “frozen” backbone in train() mode keeps rewriting its normalisation statistics from your batches. The weights are fixed and the network still drifts — and it drifts towards a corpus of 1,020 images, which is the opposite of what you froze it for.

The fix is model.eval() on the frozen part — the same call that switches dropout off for evaluation, and for the same underlying reason: some modules behave differently in the two modes, and the mode is not implied by anything else you have set.

The recipe

  1. Load a pretrained backbone and its preprocessing — the normalisation constants are part of the weights
  2. Freeze it, put it in eval() mode, extract features once
  3. Fit a linear head. Measure. This is the number everything else has to beat
  4. Unfreeze the last block; learning rate an order of magnitude smaller than the head’s. Measure again
  5. Add augmentation, one transform at a time, each justified by an invariance you can state. Measure each
  6. Report the ablation, not just the best number

Steps 3, 4 and 5 each end in measure. A recipe without them is a ritual.

Step 2 · let the last block move

The early layers hold edges and colours, which are not about flowers. The late layers hold parts and textures, which partly are.


for p in net.layer4.parameters():
    p.requires_grad = True

opt = torch.optim.Adam([
    {"params": net.layer4.parameters(), "lr": 1e-4},
    {"params": net.fc.parameters(),     "lr": 1e-3},
])

One optimiser, two parameter groups, two learning rates. That is all a “differential learning rate” is.

Why the two rates have to differ

Starting pointRate
new headrandomlarge — it has everything to learn
pretrained blockalready goodsmall — it has something to lose

Give the backbone the head’s learning rate and the first few batches — whose gradients come through a randomly initialised head — will damage the representation you downloaded. It does not crash; the accuracy simply comes out lower and you conclude that transfer learning does not work.

What a large learning rate destroys

Fine-tune the whole backbone at the rate you would use to train from scratch, and the first gradient step overwrites the features you came for. The network then relearns them from 1,020 images, badly.

The condition The pretrained weights are a good starting point, which is exactly what makes them fragile: a step large enough to be useful on random weights is far too large for weights that are already nearly right.

This is why differential learning rates exist, and why the usual recipe warms up the new head first while everything else is frozen: the head starts random, and its early gradients are large enough to damage anything they reach.

Batch-norm running statistics are not parameters

  • They are buffers, updated in the forward pass whenever the module is in training mode
  • requires_grad = False does nothing to them — no gradient is involved
  • So a frozen backbone in train() mode quietly replaces ImageNet’s statistics with the statistics of 1,020 flower photographs, in batches of 32

net.train()
for m in [net.bn1, net.layer1, net.layer2, net.layer3]:
    m.eval()                       # freeze the buffers too

Step 3 · manufacture more data

We cannot buy labels. We can show the network a different view of the same labelled image every epoch.

  • Random resized crop — a random sub-rectangle, scaled back to 224. Teaches scale and position tolerance
  • Horizontal flip — a flower is still that species mirrored

Each is a transformation the label is known to survive. That is the entire criterion, and it is a modelling decision, not a library call.

One photograph, seven training examples

The label is the same in all eight. That is the only thing that makes this legitimate.

Augmentation is a training-time transform

It is easy to build one pipeline and hand it to both loaders. The training loader wants random crops and flips; the evaluation loader must not have them.

The condition, and what it costs An augmented evaluation set makes the score a random variable rather than a function of the weights. Score one fixed model twice and you get two different numbers. Measured here: the reported accuracy is 1.87 points pessimistic and carries 2.16 points of noise — a third of the gain that fine-tuning added on top of the frozen probe.

Nothing raises. The only symptom is that the number moves when nothing else did.

Does it select a different model?

Not in this run: both curves are still rising and both peak at the last epoch. Where a curve flattens, 2.16 points of noise is enough to order two epochs by the evaluator’s seed, and early stopping then keeps the wrong checkpoint.

The check, in two lines


a = evaluate(model, val_loader)
b = evaluate(model, val_loader)
assert a == b, f"evaluation is not deterministic: {a} vs {b}"

Run every evaluation twice. If the two disagree, something in the evaluation path is random — augmentation, dropout still active, or a shuffled loader whose batches are averaged rather than pooled.

Why an assert and not a comment A comment saying “do not augment the validation set” is read once. This runs every time, costs two forward passes, and fails loudly at the moment the mistake is made rather than three lectures later.

The fine-tune is not faster at all: it runs a much larger network on much larger images, and scores the validation set twice an epoch while it does. The frozen probe is the one that is cheaper — and it is also the one you should run first, every time.

Solutions — Lecture 12

1. A dense layer mapping a $3\times128\times128$ input to a $32\times128\times128$ output has…

$32\times3\times7\times7 = 4{,}704$. Neither $H$ nor $W$ appears.

  • One kernel per output channel, reused at every position
  • The image size is genuinely absent — that is what weight sharing means

2. State the difference between equivariance and invariance, say which of the two pooling builds, and why a…

Equivariance: $f(T x) = T f(x)$, the output moves with the input. Invariance: $f(T x) = f(x)$, the output does not move. Pooling builds invariance, and a classifier wants it because the species is the same wherever the flower is.

  • A max pool keeps the largest value in a window, so a shift that keeps that value inside its window changes nothing; four stages tolerate more, and a global pool tolerates every shift
  • The position is a nuisance variable for naming a species, so discarding it costs nothing; a task that must say where could not afford it

Solutions — Lecture 12 (2)

3. Convolution is equivariant to translation on the interior of an image but not at the border. Give the reason.

Zero padding invents input that was not there, so a shift moves real content into invented content.

  • The identity assumes the operator sees the same neighbourhood before and after the shift
  • At the edge it does not, so the equality is an interior statement

4. Trained with Adam at its default learning rate of $10^{-3}$, the lecture’s network neither diverges nor…

It plateaus at or near chance, 0.98% on a split with ten images of every species. Two seeds at each rate, same schedule: 1.42% mean validation accuracy at $10^{-3}$ against 5.74% at $3\times10^{-4}$, 4.31 points.

  • Nothing flags it: no NaN, no divergence, just a low number
  • The gap is about twice the 2.25 points between two seeds of the good rate, so it is a finding rather than noise
