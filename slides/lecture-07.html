The stakeholder

A document-processing bureau scans incoming items and routes each one to one of ten queues.

  • Items arrive as small greyscale scans, already cropped and centred
  • A human currently sorts them; the operator wants to know how often a machine would be right
  • Misrouting is recoverable but expensive: the item goes to the wrong desk and comes back a day later

Ten classes, one label each, no ranking, no probability requirement stated. Write that down: it decides the output layer.

A pixel is a feature

We flatten each 28 × 28 image into a vector of 784 numbers and hand it to the model as an ordinary table of features.

What that throws away The model is told nothing about which pixels are neighbours. Shuffle all 784 columns with one fixed permutation and it learns exactly as well. A human could not read a single shuffled image.

That is a real loss, and repairing it is Chapter 12 — four lectures away. Today we pay it, and it is the honest starting point.

Failure condition — a model told nothing about which pixels are neighbours Flattening an image to 784 independent features discards adjacency entirely: shuffle every column with one fixed permutation and the network learns exactly as well. That is a statement about what the architecture cannot use, and no training curve reveals it.

Scale the pixels

Divide by 255, so every feature lies in $[0, 1]$.

  • Networks are trained by gradient descent, and the step size is one number shared by every weight
  • Inputs two orders of magnitude apart make one step too large for some weights and too small for others
And no, this is not a leak Dividing by a constant that is a property of the file format fits nothing. StandardScaler estimates a mean and a variance from data and must therefore be fitted after the split. x / 255 estimates nothing.

Where the idea came from

McCulloch and Pitts, 1943: a very simplified model of a biological neuron, which fires when enough of its inputs fire.

The analogy is historical and it is where the vocabulary comes from — neuron, activation, firing. It is not an argument for anything. Modern networks resemble brains about as closely as aeroplanes resemble birds.

Chapter 9 opens with this and then drops it. So do we.

The multilayer perceptron

One unit computes a weighted sum of its inputs plus a bias, then an activation: $h = \phi(\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x} + b)$. Units are arranged in layers:

  • An input layer, which holds the features and has no weights
  • One or more hidden layers, each fully connected to the previous one
  • An output layer, one unit per class

Fully connected, or dense: every unit of one layer receives every unit of the previous one. That is where the parameters are.

Two or more hidden layers and the book calls it a deep neural network. The word means nothing more than that.

Why there must be a nonlinearity

Suppose the activation were the identity. Then

$$\mathbf{W}_2\left(\mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1\right) + \mathbf{b}_2 \;=\; \left(\mathbf{W}_2\mathbf{W}_1\right)\mathbf{x} + \left(\mathbf{W}_2\mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2\right)$$

A single affine map. Two hundred stacked layers would still be a single affine map.

Depth buys you nothing without a nonlinearity between the layers. This is one line of algebra and it is the reason activation functions exist at all.

“Flat below zero” is a real defect and it has a name — dying ReLUs. It is diagnosed in Lecture 9, not here.

One layer, in matrices

A whole mini-batch at once $$\mathbf{H} \;=\; \phi\!\left(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + \mathbf{b}\right)$$
  • $\mathbf{X}$ — one row per instance in the batch, one column per input
  • $\mathbf{W}$ — one row per input, one column per unit
  • $\mathbf{b}$ — one entry per unit, broadcast down the rows
  • $\phi$ — the activation, applied element by element

The whole forward pass is a handful of matrix products. That is why a GPU helps at all: a GPU is a machine for doing exactly this.

The output layer

Ten classes, mutually exclusive. Ten output units, and a softmax across them:

$$\hat{p}_k \;=\; \frac{\exp(z_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{10}\exp(z_j)}$$
  • Every output is positive and they sum to 1
  • The largest logit $z_k$ gives the largest probability, so argmax is unchanged by the softmax

The full properties of softmax — shift invariance, the gradient, why the loss consumes logits rather than probabilities — are derived in Lecture 17.

The loss

Cross-entropy, one instance $$L \;=\; -\sum_{k=1}^{10} y_k \log \hat{p}_k \;=\; -\log \hat{p}_{\text{true}}$$

The target is one-hot, so nine of the ten terms are zero. The loss is minus the log probability the model assigned to the right answer, and it goes to infinity as that probability goes to zero.

Chapter 4 called this log loss when there were two classes. Same object.

How the weights get updated

Backpropagation: one forward pass to compute the loss, one backward pass to compute every partial derivative, one gradient-descent step.

  • Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams, 1986 — and the reason the field restarted
  • The backward pass costs about as much as the forward one, no matter how many parameters there are
Why “no matter how many” is remarkable 266,610 parameters, one number of interest, and a single sweep gets all of the derivatives. That claim is derived at the top of the next lecture, and it is the whole reason any of this is feasible.

Architecture — classification networks

Our task is the third row: ten mutually exclusive classes, so ten units, softmax, cross-entropy.

The distinction between rows two and three is not cosmetic. Multilabel asks ten independent yes/no questions; multiclass asks one ten-way question. Softmax couples the outputs; ten sigmoids do not.

An output activation paired with the wrong loss

Failure condition Softmax with mean squared error trains — slowly and badly. The gradient through a saturated softmax nearly vanishes, so the run completes, the loss falls a little, and the pairing that would have worked is never suspected.

How wide, and how deep?

  • Depth buys parameter efficiency: two narrow layers express things one wide layer needs exponentially many units for
  • Width buys capacity within a layer, at $d \times n + n$ parameters a time for $d$ inputs and $n$ units
  • In practice: pick a depth that is enough, then grow the width until validation stops improving — and stop there
The honest answer There is no formula. What there is, is a budget you can compute before fitting anything — the parameter count — and a validation curve that tells you when you have spent it badly.

not examinable — engineering The numbers depend on the corpus. The method for finding them does not.

What twenty epochs look like

The two accuracy curves separate and stay separated. That gap is overfitting: the training-versus-validation gap, drawn as lines.

Reading the curve

  • The loss falls smoothly — the optimiser is working
  • Training accuracy keeps climbing — the model has capacity to spare
  • Validation accuracy flattens — the extra capacity is being spent on memorising
The question a curve answers that a number cannot Would more epochs help? Hardly: over the last ten epochs validation moved less than a point while training gained three. Without the curve you would have guessed.

The lesson from ten fits

  1. Depth paid, a little. On 6,000 images the deepest network beat the single layer of 300 by about a point and a quarter.
  2. The learning rate mattered slightly more — on this grid. The five architectures span 2.4 accuracy points; the five learning rates span 2.8. One badly chosen scalar lost more than any structural choice gained. Which is a claim about a grid and a scale, never about the world.
  3. Ten fits is not a search. Ten points from a space with five axes, varied one axis at a time with everything else held fixed.

Point 3 is the one to remember. You have not optimised anything; you have looked at ten places.

What to tell the operator

A defensible report “Overall it is right 85.6% of the time. But it is right 63.6% of the time on Pullover, and almost all of those errors go to two visually similar queues. If those three desks are adjacent, the practical error rate is much lower than the headline. If they are in different buildings, it is much worse.”

The headline accuracy does not answer the operator’s question. The per-class breakdown does, and it took one extra function call.

Wall 1 · one CPU, and nowhere to move it

20 epochs, 12,000 images, 266,610 parameters, one CPU.

The cost grows in step with the data and the epochs, on the same single core, however large the job becomes.

There is no device= Scikit-Learn does not support GPUs, and says so in its own FAQ: it is a deliberate scope decision, not an oversight. PyTorch, from the next lecture, gives you the choice — and Lecture 8 measures when it is worth taking.

Wall 2 · you cannot change the loss


>>> MLPClassifier(hidden_layer_sizes=(300, 100), loss="mae")
TypeError: MLPClassifier.__init__() got an unexpected
keyword argument 'loss'

Cross-entropy is hard-coded. That is fine until the operator says “calling a coat a shirt is cheap, calling a bag a sandal is expensive” — a weighted loss, which is one line in a framework that lets you write one.

And that is the ordinary case, not an exotic one.

Wall 3 · you cannot see a gradient


>>> [a for a in dir(clf) if "grad" in a.lower()]
[]

Backpropagation ran 20 times per image and computed 266,610 partial derivatives on every batch. None of them was kept.

Why that matters in three lectures Lecture 9 builds a twenty-layer network whose loss barely moves. The diagnosis is a per-layer gradient statistic. You cannot compute it here.

Wall 4 · the smallest unit of control is one epoch


>>> inspect.signature(MLPClassifier.partial_fit)
(self, X, y, classes=None)

partial_fit is the finest handle there is, and one call is one complete pass over everything you hand it.

  • No per-batch loss
  • No hook between the forward pass and the backward pass
  • No way to clip a gradient, or to skip a bad batch, or to log anything

The problem underneath all four

The loop was written for you fit() contains a for loop over epochs and batches that you did not write, cannot see, and cannot enter. Every one of the four walls is the same wall.

This is not a criticism of Scikit-Learn. MLPClassifier is documented as a convenience, and for a tabular problem with a standard loss it is the right tool. It is the wrong tool from here on, and now you know precisely why.

Design exercise · in the manner of an ML system-design interview

The task: a radiology-software start-up’s chest X-ray triage

The brief A radiology-software start-up sells a plug-in for the hospital worklist: for every chest X-ray it raises a flag, may show a tumour, so a radiologist reads it first; it reorders, never diagnoses. Hospitals pay for a shorter wait on the tumours; a false flag costs radiologist minutes; a miss reaches a patient. It is a medical device: cleared for one intended use, every model change one the regulator must see.
  • Given: past scans from partner hospitals, with the radiologist’s report and any biopsy; grouped by patient and hospital
  • Scale: hundreds of thousands of scans, tumours a few per hundred; thousands a day, flagged within seconds
  • Consumers of the output: the worklist, the radiologist, the hospital’s quality team, the regulator’s file

Nine steps follow, each with the pitfall that catches a first attempt and what to do about it. The model at the centre is today’s: a multilayer perceptron on the downsampled pixels, fitted with MLPClassifier.

Step 1 — Frame it honestly: the intended use decides the metric

  • One row per scan, two exclusive classes — a tumour in the report, or not — written down first, because it decides the output layer
  • Count the classes first: tumours are a few per hundred, so “never flag” scores far above ninety and accuracy hides a useless model; the metric is recall on the tumours at the share of scans the worklist may flag, with the confusion matrix
  • Two trivial baselines: never flag, high accuracy and no recall; flag everything, full recall, nothing reordered
Pitfall The pitch says “right on more than nine scans in ten”, which never flagging also achieves. The regulator asks for sensitivity and specificity at the operating point, under an intended use in one sentence: a triage aid that reorders a queue, not a diagnosis. Written first, it decides the metric now and the device’s label later.

Step 2 — A pixel is a feature, and the split is by patient

  • Downsample every scan to one small fixed size and flatten it: one column per pixel. The model knows nothing of which pixels are neighbours — a real loss, paid today, dearest on a small nodule
  • Divide by the largest pixel value, so every feature lies in the unit interval: a property of the file format, it fits nothing and is not a leak
  • Carve a validation set from the training half with a fixed seed; test once
Pitfall A random split over scans puts one patient’s follow-up — the same lungs, the same tumour a month later — on both sides, and the model is scored on tumours it has seen. Split by patient, and hold out a whole hospital as the test set: the device will be sold to hospitals not in the archive, and that is the regulator’s number.

Step 3 — Count the parameters before you fit

  • A dense layer from $d$ inputs to $n$ units costs $d\times n + n$ parameters, and the first layer — pixels into the first hidden width — holds almost all of them
  • That count against the number of training scans is the budget: pick a depth that is enough, grow the width until validation stops improving, and stop there
Pitfall The scanner delivers millions of pixels a scan, the engineer flattens them as they come, and the first layer alone is billions of parameters for a few hundred thousand scans — nothing fits in memory or generalises. Downsample to the size the count was done for, agreed with the radiologists so the nodule survives it; write the arithmetic down and check it against the fitted model.

Step 4 — The output layer and the loss, paired from the table

TaskOutput unitsActivationLoss
Binary — this oneonesigmoidbinary cross-entropy
Multilabelone per labelsigmoidbinary cross-entropy
Multiclassone per classsoftmaxcross-entropy

Tumour or not: one unit, a sigmoid, and minus the log probability of the right answer as the loss; MLPClassifier chooses both from the labels.

Pitfall An engineer who wants “normal, benign, tumour” for the radiologist builds three sigmoids — independent yes/no questions whose answers need not sum to one, so the threshold the device is cleared at has no meaning; softmax with squared error trains slowly and badly, and the pairing that would have worked is never suspected. Take the row from the table; add classes only when the intended use says so.

Step 5 — Learning curves: when to stop, and what a subset sweep tells you

  • Record the loss and the validation recall at every epoch: training still climbing while validation flattens says the extra capacity is memorising
  • Stop where validation stops moving, and read the held-out hospital once — a validation number that chose the epoch is optimistic
  • Tune width, depth and the learning rate by hand on a subset, one axis at a time, then rerun the best few on all the scans; log every fit, as the regulator asks how the model was chosen
Pitfall Ten fits is not a search, and a ranking measured on a fraction of the archive is a measurement of that fraction: the deeper network that lost on the subset may win on all of them. On today’s grid one bad learning rate lost more than any structural choice gained — a claim about that grid, not the world.

Step 6 — Where the errors are: the confusion that matters clinically

  • One number hides the shape: build the confusion matrix and read its two errors apart. A miss leaves a tumour waiting in the queue like any other scan; a false flag costs a radiologist minutes on a normal one. Not the same price
  • Then recall per subgroup: small nodules against large, each hospital’s scanner, portable against fixed, so the misses have an address
Pitfall The headline recall is defended at the hospital’s quality meeting while the misses sit in one place — the small nodules the downsampling erased, or the one hospital with an older scanner — and those are the ones that reach a patient. Report recall per subgroup, which errors go where, and the price of each; the regulator’s file carries the per-hospital table, not the headline.

Step 7 — Abstain to the radiologist, and never say “clear”

  • The sigmoid output is the model’s score. Sweep the threshold on the validation set and draw two curves — the share flagged, and the recall among the tumours; the radiologists pick the point, since their minutes and a miss both have prices
  • Three outputs reach the worklist: flagged, not flagged, and not assessed — a scan whose metadata or reconstruction error says it is unlike the archive
Pitfall The sigmoid must give a number for every scan, so a lateral view gets a confident score of something; and a radiologist who reads “not flagged” as “normal” reads less carefully — the device has started to diagnose. Bucket the validation scans by score and check that flagged scans are tumours as often as the number claims; show the score only if it passes; abstain visibly out of scope.

What we did

  1. Wrote a layer as one matrix product and one elementwise non-linearity, $\mathbf{H} = \phi(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + \mathbf{b})$ — and saw that without the non-linearity any stack collapses to a single affine map
  2. Counted the parameters before fitting: 266,610, almost all in the first layer
  3. Read the classification table: ten exclusive classes means ten units, softmax and cross-entropy, and the pairing is not optional
  4. Checked the class counts before choosing accuracy, anchored it at a 10% baseline, fitted a network with MLPClassifier, tuned it by hand, and split the accuracy by class
  5. Named four things that object will not let you do — and saw that all four are the same thing: the training loop is written for you, and you cannot enter it
  6. Designed a radiology-software start-up’s chest X-ray triage in nine steps — recall at the operating point under a written intended use, a split by patient with a hospital held out, the parameters counted at the downsampled size, the binary row of the table, the curves read for the stopping epoch, the confusion that matters clinically, abstention to the radiologist, and the loop owned in a numbered version of a medical device run in shadow mode — each step named for the pitfall it avoids

Solutions — Lecture 6

1. A marketing team wants every customer placed in one of a handful of segments; a fraud team wants the few…

Segments: k-means, because every account must land in one of a few compact groups and the number of groups is a business choice. Anomalies: DBSCAN, because its noise label is exactly the output wanted, with no $k$ to choose.

  • Behaviour data has no empty space between groups, so DBSCAN calls most accounts noise or everything one cluster, and nobody can write to noise
  • k-means forces every transaction into a cluster, so an anomaly becomes a far-off member of the nearest one and drags its centroid towards itself

2. The explained variance ratio is sometimes called a heuristic for choosing $d$. Say why it is not one, and…

The principal subspace leaves exactly $\sum_{j>d}\sigma_j^2$, so $\sum_{j\le d} r_j$ is exactly the fraction of squared error removed. Keeping 95% of the variance leaves 5% of the total squared error of the centred data.

  • The minimum of $E(\mathbf{W})$ is the tail of the spectrum, and the total is $\sum_j \sigma_j^2$, so the ratio is the theorem normalised, not a rule of thumb
  • On the Olivetti faces 123 of 4,096 components reach 95%, so the price of a factor of 33 fewer numbers is stated before $d$ is chosen

3. A customer-segmentation job reruns every month on fresh orders, and the marketing team complains that the…

Fix n_init and random_state, and keep the scaler with the model so the columns are in the same units every month. Then cluster two resamples of the accounts, match the segments by overlap and count the accounts that keep theirs: a segment that does not survive a resample is not one.

  • Lloyd’s algorithm converges to a local minimum that depends on where it started, so an unnamed seed is a different answer on every run
  • Stability across resamples is the only evidence for a grouping when there is no label to score it against

4. Inertia always falls as $k$ rises, so it cannot choose $k$. State what silhouette adds, and one situation…

Silhouette compares within-cluster to nearest-other-cluster distance, so it is penalised for splitting a group and for merging two, and has an interior maximum. It fails to decide when the clusters overlap with no clean gaps: the curve is nearly flat near its maximum.

  • Inertia is non-increasing in $k$ by construction — refining a partition cannot lengthen a distance to the assigned centre
  • Where two people’s photographs are about as far apart as two of one person, the chosen $k$ is not decisively better than its neighbours; on the Olivetti faces the worst cluster at $k = 40$ is a lighting condition

5. You have 4,096-dimensional face vectors and are told two faces are “close”. Explain why that…

Distance is not identity — lighting and pose move a face further than identity does.

  • Pixel distance is dominated by whatever varies most, and that is illumination
  • The metric has to be one under which the thing you care about is the thing that varies
