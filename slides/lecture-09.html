There is no notebook today: an interview is a whiteboard and a conversation.

What an ML system design interview is

  • A standard round for machine learning engineers and applied scientists at Meta, Google, Amazon, Microsoft, Apple and most companies that ship models
  • One open-ended prompt — “design the system that …” — and a whiteboard. There is no single right answer
  • The candidate drives. The interviewer plays the product manager: answers questions, adds constraints, and pushes on whatever looks weak
  • It tests whether you can turn a vague business problem into a system that works, can be measured, and can be run
What it is not A quiz on the newest architecture. Candidates who open with “I would fine-tune a large model” are usually marked down: they have answered a question nobody asked yet.

Every row is something this course has measured. The interview checks that you reach for it without being asked.

The shape of a good answer

  1. Clarify the problem and its constraints
  2. Frame it as a machine learning task: input, output, decision
  3. Metrics, offline and online
  4. Data and labels
  5. Features
  6. The model, from baseline up
  7. Training, thresholds and offline evaluation
  8. Serving at scale
  9. Launch, monitoring and iteration

Cover all nine at the level of a sentence each before going deep anywhere; then go deep where the interviewer pulls. Running out of time at step four is the most common way to fail.

The interviewer

“We run a social platform with billions of users. Design a system that detects harmful content.”

What do you say first?

Step 1 · Your turn

Before you design anything: what do you ask?

Write down the questions you would ask the interviewer before drawing a single box.

Think about what is being detected, in what, how much of it, how fast, what happens next, and what you can learn from.

Step 1 — The questions a strong candidate asks

QuestionWhy it changes the design
Which harms? Violence, nudity, hate speech, harassment, self-harm, scams…each policy is a label, a threshold and a team
Which content? Text, images, video, comments, live streamsthe modality decides the features and the cost

Step 1 — What the interviewer answers

  • Harms: violence, nudity, hate speech, harassment and self-harm, each with its own written policy
  • Content: posts with text and images; hundreds of millions a day, in dozens of languages
  • When: check at upload within tens of milliseconds, and again as a post spreads
  • Actions: remove automatically when sure, send to a human reviewer when unsure, reduce distribution in between
  • Labels: past reviewer decisions and user reports; users can appeal; a regulator expects a public report on what was removed
Red flag Starting to design before asking. Second red flag: asking twenty questions and never committing. Ask the few that change the design, write the answers on the board as assumptions, and move on.

Step 2 · Your turn

State the machine learning task

What goes in, what comes out, and what is the output used for? One model or several?

Step 2 — Input, output, decision

  • Input: a post — its text and image, its author, where and when it was posted
  • Output: one probability per harm, $p(\text{violence} \mid \text{post}), p(\text{hate} \mid \text{post}), \dots$ — multi-label: a post can be violent and hateful at once
  • Decision: a separate policy layer turns the probabilities into actions with a threshold per harm and per action

Post → model → one probability per harm → policy layer → remove, send to review, reduce distribution, or leave

Red flag One softmax over the harms. A softmax makes the probabilities sum to one, so a post cannot be both violent and hateful; use one sigmoid output per harm. And keep the action out of the model: policies change weekly, weights should not have to.

Step 2 — One model, one per harm, or one with many heads?

OptionForAgainst
One binary model, harmful or notsimplest to buildpolicies differ in cost; one threshold cannot serve them; the action cannot say why
One model per harmindependent teams and releasesmany models to serve; rare harms have too few labels to learn alone
One shared model, one head per harmrare harms borrow from common ones; one pass per postretraining couples the harms; one head’s change must be checked on all

A strong candidate picks one and says what it costs: “a shared model with a head per harm, because the rare harms need the shared representation; I will re-evaluate every head on every release.”

Step 3 · Your turn

How do you know it works?

Name the metrics you would report offline, before launch, and online, after it.

Harmful posts are a tiny fraction of everything posted. What does that do to accuracy?

Step 3 — Offline: precision and recall, per harm

  • Violating posts are a fraction of a percent of all posts, so “nothing is harmful” is right almost every time: accuracy is meaningless
  • Per harm, the precision–recall curve; the area under it rather than under the ROC curve, which looks excellent when negatives dominate
  • Read it at the operating points the actions need: recall at the precision required to remove automatically, and recall at the precision reviewers can afford
  • Calibration, because the policy layer and the queue size both use the probabilities as probabilities
Red flag One number for all harms. Missing self-harm and missing spam do not cost the same; report each harm, and say which one you would trade for which.

Step 3 — Online: what users actually see

MetricWhat it measures
Prevalence of each harmthe share of views that land on violating content, estimated by having reviewers label a random sample of what was viewed
Proactive ratethe share of removed content caught before any user reported it
Appeals overturnedwrong removals: a precision signal from the field
Reviewer queuehow much reaches humans, and how long it waits
Guardrailscreators’ complaints, engagement, time to action
Red flag “Number of posts removed” as the goal. It rewards removing everything; prevalence weighted by views is what the platform owes its users.

Step 4 · Your turn

Where do the labels come from — and what is wrong with them?

List the sources of labels you can use, and one problem with each.

Step 4 — The labels, and their biases

SourceGoodWrong with it
Reviewer decisionstrained people, written policyexpensive; exist only for what was flagged — selection bias
User reportscheap, plentifulnoisy; mass-reporting campaigns; popular posts get reported more
Appeal outcomesthe hardest cases, judged twicefew; only from users who bother to appeal
Known-bad matchesexact copies of known imagescatches only what has been seen before
  • Reviewers disagree: measure agreement on a doubly labelled slice; it is the ceiling for any model
  • Policies change, and a label is only valid for the policy it was given under: store the policy version with every label

Step 4 — Spend labels well, and split honestly

  • A random sample of all traffic, labelled every week: the only unbiased estimate of prevalence and of what the model misses
  • Targeted labelling for training: the posts the model is least sure about, and representatives of new clusters — where a few labels buy the most, as with Lecture 6’s forty
  • Split by time: train on the past, validate and test on later weeks, as the system will meet them
  • Split by author and remove near-duplicate reposts across the split
Red flag A random split of posts. The same author’s style and the same viral image land on both sides; the model memorises them and the offline score is a memory test.

Step 5 · Your turn

What does the model look at?

Group the features you would use, and say which ones exist at the moment of upload.

Step 5 — Features

GroupExamplesAvailable at upload?
Texta pretrained multilingual text encoder’s vector of the caption — opened later in the course; today a black boxyes
Imagea pretrained image encoder’s vector; text read out of the imageyes
Authoraccount age, past violations, followers, verifiedyes
Contextwhere it is posted, the group, the language, the houryes
Reactionreports, shares, how fast it spreads, the commentsonly later
Red flag — leakage in time Training on “number of reports” and serving at upload, where it is always zero. Compute every feature as of the moment of the decision; use an upload-time model, and re-score with reaction features as they arrive.

Step 5 — The data fights back

  • Bad actors adapt: misspellings, characters swapped for look-alikes, hateful text written inside an image, coded words that change every month
  • Features that survive this: characters rather than whole words, text read out of images, the author’s history, the network of accounts posting the same thing
  • A benign image with a hateful caption is harmful only in combination: the model must see both together

Say this unprompted. It is what separates a candidate who has run a system from one who has trained a model.

Step 6 · Your turn

Which model — and how do you get there?

Start from the simplest thing that could work, and say what would make you move up each step.

Step 6 — From baseline to the shipped model

StageModelWhy
Baselinerules: lists of banned terms, matching against known-bad imagesships in days, explainable, the bar to beat
First modellogistic regression on the frozen vectors and the tabular featuresfast, calibrated, a real metric to improve on
Tabular strengthgradient-boosted trees on the same featuresinteractions between author, context and content
Shippeda network: the vectors and features together → shared layers → one sigmoid head per harmsees text and image together; rare harms share what common ones learn
Maybe laterfine-tuning the encoders themselvesonly if the offline gain pays for the serving cost

Every step up is justified by the offline metric of step three, measured on the split of step four.

Step 6 — The shipped model, in detail

  • Early fusion: concatenate the text vector, the image vector and the tabular features before the shared layers, so a benign picture and a hateful caption are judged together. Late fusion — one classifier per modality, scores combined — misses exactly that case
  • Loss: the sum over harms of binary cross-entropy, one term per head, weighted so the rare harms are not drowned out
  • Imbalance: keep every positive, sample the negatives, then correct the probabilities back — the log-odds shift of Lecture 5’s advertising design exercise
Red flag Class weights or resampling with no correction, then thresholds set as if the outputs were probabilities. The policy layer needs calibrated probabilities; check them per harm, as in Lecture 4.

Step 7 · Your turn

Before launch: how do you set the thresholds?

Each harm needs a rule for “remove”, “send to a human” and “reduce distribution”. Who decides, and from what?

Step 7 — Thresholds are policy, set from the curves

Band of $p(\text{harm})$ActionSet by
very highremove automatically, notify, allow appealthe precision the policy team accepts for no human in the loop
highsend to a human reviewerreviewer capacity: the expected daily volume must fit the queue
mediumreduce distribution while it is reviewedthe cost of spreading a harmful post against hiding a good one
lowleave it—

Each band is a point on the validation precision–recall curve of that harm, chosen with the policy team and signed off — as Lecture 3 put it, the threshold is a decision, not a hyperparameter.

Step 7 — Evaluate where it fails, not on average

  • Report every metric per language, per region, per content type, for new and for established accounts
  • The known failure: dialects and reclaimed words flagged as hate speech far more often than standard usage, so one community’s posts are removed more often for saying the same thing
  • Report the worst slice beside the average, and set a floor that every slice must clear before launch
  • Read a sample of false positives and false negatives by eye before any launch decision
Red flag One global number. Lecture 10 is about exactly this: what a model relies on, and for whom it fails.

Step 8 · Your turn

Hundreds of millions of posts a day

Draw the path a post takes from upload to action. Where do you spend the expensive computation?

Step 8 — Serving: cheap first, expensive where it matters

  • Stage one, at upload, on everything: known-bad matching and a small model, within the latency budget. Clearly fine: publish. Clearly violating: block
  • Stage two, in the background: the full multimodal model on the uncertain posts, published meanwhile with reduced distribution
  • Re-score as reaction features arrive and as a post starts to spread
  • The review queue is ordered by expected harm: probability of violation times predicted reach — the post about to go viral first

Compute each post’s text and image vectors once, store them, and share them with the other systems that need them.

Step 8 — The trade-offs to say out loud

DecisionThe trade-off
Check before or after publishingsafety against the delay every user feels
Size of the stage-one modelwhat it catches against what the latency budget allows
When the model is downfail open (publish unchecked) or fail closed (hold every post): a policy choice, decided in advance
How much reaches humansaccuracy on the hard cases against cost — and against the reviewers’ wellbeing, which limits how much they can see

Step 9 · Your turn

You have launched. How do you know it works — and keeps working?

How do you roll it out, what do you watch, and what makes it degrade?

Step 9 — Rolling it out

  • Shadow mode: the new model scores live traffic but takes no action; compare its decisions with the reviewers’
  • Gradual ramp: a growing share of traffic, watching prevalence, appeals overturned and the queue
  • A/B test against the current model on prevalence and appeals — but a control group cannot be left exposed to the worst harms, so for those the random-sample prevalence estimate replaces a pure holdout
Red flag Launching on an offline gain alone. The model changes what is shown, what users report and what reviewers see; only the live measurement counts.

Step 9 — What makes it degrade, and what to watch

CauseWhat to watch, and what to do
New slang, new formats, news eventsflag rates and input distributions per harm and language; relabel and retrain
Bad actors adaptingthe random sample catches what the model now misses; red teams
The feedback looplabels come only from what the model flagged, so it never learns its misses — keep labelling the random sample
A policy changeold labels become wrong; relabel under the new policy version

Retrain on a schedule, but only release a model that beats the current one on the latest weeks and clears every slice.

The whole system, on one slide

Upload → stage one: known-bad matching and a small model → publish, block, or publish with reduced distribution → stage two: the multimodal model, one head per harm → policy layer: thresholds per harm and per action → remove, send to review (ordered by probability times reach), or leave → reviewers’ decisions and appeals become labels → a random sample is labelled every week → retrain, test on the latest weeks and on every slice → shadow, ramp, A/B test.

The follow-up questions interviewers ask

“What if …”The gist of a good answer
a new kind of harm appears overnight?rules and reviewers first; a new head on the shared model; targeted labelling
you launch in a new language?a multilingual encoder, labels in that language, the slice measured before launch
bad actors misspell every word?character-level and image-text features, adversarial examples in training

a famous account is wrongly removed?appeals and a second review; the same rules for every account

The red flags an interviewer writes down

  • Designs before asking a single question — or asks and never commits
  • Accuracy, or one number for everything
  • Assumes labels are free, clean and unbiased
  • A random split of posts; features that exist only after the decision
  • Starts with the biggest model; cannot say what the baseline is
  • A softmax over harms that can co-occur
  • Stops at training: no serving path, no cost, no monitoring, no feedback loop
  • Lists techniques instead of choosing one and saying what it costs

How to practise

  • Take any product you use and run the nine steps on it, out loud, against a clock
  • Say every choice as a trade-off; write assumptions on the board
  • Prompts in the same style: video recommendations for a streaming app; the arrival time shown by a food-delivery app; ranking an e-commerce site’s search results; blocking fraudulent payments at checkout; “people you may know”
  • Every lecture from here on ends with a design exercise in this format: it is practice for this conversation

What we did

  1. Ran a mock ML system design interview, in the format used for machine learning engineers at the large technology companies: one open prompt, the candidate drives, trade-offs out loud
  2. Clarified before designing, and framed the task as one probability per harm — multi-label, sigmoid heads — with a separate policy layer that turns probabilities into actions
  3. Measured with precision and recall per harm at the action thresholds, and online with prevalence weighted by views, never accuracy
  4. Treated labels as biased evidence: random-sample labelling, targeted labelling, splits by time and by author, policy versions
  5. Built up from rules to a fused multimodal network, set thresholds as policy, evaluated per slice, served in two stages, and launched through shadow mode and a ramp, watching for drift, adversaries and the feedback loop

Solutions — Lecture 8

1. Reverse-mode automatic differentiation costs one sweep for all partial derivatives; forward mode costs one…

Many inputs, one output — which is exactly a loss.

  • Forward mode’s cost scales with the number of inputs, reverse mode’s with the number of outputs
  • A neural network has millions of parameters and one scalar loss

2. Test accuracy on 10,000 images is computed as the plain mean of the per-batch accuracies, in batches of 384.…

The last batch holds 16 images but is weighted as heavily as a full batch of 384. Count the hits over the whole set, or weight each batch by its size.

  • 10,000 = 26 × 384 + 16, so there are 27 batches and the last is short
  • Measured, the error was 0.067 points — small here, but you cannot tell from the number whether it was

Solutions — Lecture 8 (2)

3. The five lines of the training loop are reordered so that opt.step() comes before loss.backward(). State what…

Nothing raises, and the training loss stays at its starting level: the parameters are never changed.

  • The step is taken with the gradients zeroed at the top of the iteration
  • The gradients computed afterwards are cleared before they are ever used

4. A network with dropout is evaluated without calling model.eval(). Describe the symptom precisely, and the…

The score wobbles between identical calls. Evaluate twice and assert the two agree.

  • Dropout keeps sampling masks in training mode, so the metric is a random variable
  • A deterministic function of fixed weights and fixed data does not change between calls

Solutions — Lecture 8 (3)

5. In the measured comparison the accelerator barely beat the CPU on the smallest network, and its lead grew as…

Dispatch overhead: on a small matrix product much of each step is the cost of dispatching it, not the arithmetic. The crossover is set by how much arithmetic each batch carries — measured, near-parity at the smallest width and a clear lead at the widest.

  • A GPU is a machine for large matrix products, and a narrow layer at a batch of 128 is not a large one
  • The device pulls ahead once each batch carries enough arithmetic to cover the dispatch, and that grows with the layer widths
