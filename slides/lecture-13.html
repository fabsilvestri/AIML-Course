Which backbone?

The rule that matters more than the family A bigger backbone is not automatically better on a small corpus. You are fitting a head on frozen features; more features means more parameters in the head and more chance to overfit 1,020 images. Start small, and grow it only if the validation curve says the features are the bottleneck.

not examinable — engineering The names change every year. The trade-off does not.

Two decisions, in this order

  1. Linear probe. Freeze everything, extract features once, fit a linear classifier. Minutes, and it tells you whether the backbone understands your images at all
  2. Fine-tune. Unfreeze the last block or two, small learning rate, and only if the probe was promising
Why the order is not arbitrary The probe is a measurement of the features, uncontaminated by anything you do to them. If it barely beats the trivial baseline, the features are wrong for this domain and fine-tuning is polishing the wrong object. If it is already close, fine-tuning has little left to buy and may cost you generalisation.

How much data before fine-tuning pays?

Labelled images per classWhat is usually worth doing
fewer than about 20linear probe only; fine-tuning overfits
tensprobe, then unfreeze the last block with a small rate
hundredsunfreeze progressively, differential rates
thousands and upfine-tune everything; consider training from scratch

Flowers102 gives us ten per class in the training split. That is the first row, and it is why the frozen probe does so much of the work today.

These are orders of magnitude, not thresholds. The measurement that settles it for your corpus is the probe against the fine-tune, on the same validation split.

Freezing weights does not freeze a network

requires_grad = False stops the gradients. It does not stop batch normalisation updating its running mean and variance, which happens in the forward pass and has nothing to do with autograd.

The condition A “frozen” backbone in train() mode keeps rewriting its normalisation statistics from your batches. The weights are fixed and the network still drifts — and it drifts towards a corpus of 1,020 images, which is the opposite of what you froze it for.

The fix is model.eval() on the frozen part, which is the same call as Lecture 10’s dropout bug and for the same underlying reason: two modules behave differently in the two modes, and the mode is not implied by anything else you have set.

The recipe

  1. Load a pretrained backbone and its preprocessing — the normalisation constants are part of the weights
  2. Freeze it, put it in eval() mode, extract features once
  3. Fit a linear head. Measure. This is the number everything else has to beat
  4. Unfreeze the last block; learning rate an order of magnitude smaller than the head’s. Measure again
  5. Add augmentation, one transform at a time, each justified by an invariance you can state. Measure each
  6. Report the ablation, not just the best number

Steps 3, 4 and 5 each end in measure. A recipe without them is a ritual.

Transfer learning, as a sentence

Take a network trained on a large labelled corpus, keep everything except its classifier, and train a new classifier on your own small corpus.

  • The kept part is the backbone or feature extractor
  • What it produces for an image is that image's representation — a vector, learned by somebody else

What is frozen and what moves

Step 1 · freeze everything, replace the head

for p in net.parameters():
    p.requires_grad = False

net.fc = nn.Linear(512, 102)      # a fresh module: requires_grad is True
print(sum(p.numel() for p in net.parameters() if p.requires_grad))
# 52326

opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.fc.parameters(), lr=1e-3)

Replacing fc after the loop is not an accident of ordering — a newly constructed module has requires_grad=True, so the head is trainable and nothing else is.

What requires_grad = False actually does

  • It stops the tensor being a leaf that accumulates a gradient, so backward() does not fill p.grad
  • The backward pass still travels through those layers if anything downstream needs it — here nothing does, so it stops at the backbone's output
  • It is not the same as eval(): dropout and batch-norm behaviour are a separate switch

Point three is the trap. Freezing the weights of a batch-norm layer does not freeze its running statistics.

If nothing before the head is training, run it once


body = nn.Sequential(*list(net.children())[:-1]).eval()

with torch.no_grad():
    F_train = torch.cat([body(xb).flatten(1) for xb, _ in train_loader])

# F_train is (1020, 512). Now train a linear model on a table.

Why a linear model on somebody else’s features beats your network

Step 2 · let the last block move

The early layers hold edges and colours, which are not about flowers. The late layers hold parts and textures, which partly are.


for p in net.layer4.parameters():
    p.requires_grad = True

opt = torch.optim.Adam([
    {"params": net.layer4.parameters(), "lr": 1e-4},
    {"params": net.fc.parameters(),     "lr": 1e-3},
])

One optimiser, two parameter groups, two learning rates. That is all a “differential learning rate” is.

“Only the last block” is not a small thing

Lecture 12’s memory calculation, mirrored in a design decision: parameters concentrate in the deep, low-resolution layers, so freezing the general layers freezes very few of them. What we froze is the part that does general work, not the part that is large.

Why the two rates have to differ

Starting pointRate
new headrandomlarge — it has everything to learn
pretrained blockalready goodsmall — it has something to lose

Give the backbone the head's learning rate and the first few batches — whose gradients come through a randomly initialised head — will damage the representation you downloaded. It does not crash; the accuracy simply comes out lower and you conclude that transfer learning does not work.

Batch-norm running statistics are not parameters

  • They are buffers, updated in the forward pass whenever the module is in training mode
  • requires_grad = False does nothing to them — no gradient is involved
  • So a frozen backbone in train() mode quietly replaces ImageNet's statistics with the statistics of 1,020 flower photographs, in batches of 32

net.train()
for m in [net.bn1, net.layer1, net.layer2, net.layer3]:
    m.eval()                       # freeze the buffers too

Step 3 · manufacture more data

We cannot buy labels. We can show the network a different view of the same labelled image every epoch.

  • Random resized crop — a random sub-rectangle, scaled back to 224. Teaches scale and position tolerance
  • Horizontal flip — a flower is still that species mirrored

Each is a transformation the label is known to survive. That is the entire criterion, and it is a modelling decision, not a library call.

One photograph, seven training examples

The label is the same in all eight. That is the only thing that makes this legitimate.

What augmentation is not

  • It is not more information. The mutual information between the corpus and the labels is unchanged
  • It is a constraint: it tells the network that the answer must be the same across a family of inputs
  • Which puts it in the same box as weight sharing and as ridge — Lecture 5's second cure, arriving for the third time

And a transformation the label does not survive is a labelling error you manufactured. A vertical flip is fine for flowers and wrong for handwritten digits.

The recipe, in order

  1. Load a pretrained backbone and its preprocessing
  2. Freeze everything, replace the head, train the head alone. This is your new baseline and it takes seconds
  3. Unfreeze the last block. Backbone learning rate an order of magnitude below the head's
  4. Put the frozen batch-norm layers in eval()
  5. Augment the training set only, with transformations the label survives

Steps 2 and 3 in that order, always. A frozen-head number you can compute in ten seconds is what tells you whether step 3 helped at all.

What a large learning rate destroys

Fine-tune the whole backbone at the rate you would use to train from scratch, and the first gradient step overwrites the features you came for. The network then relearns them from 1,020 images, badly.

The condition The pretrained weights are a good starting point, which is exactly what makes them fragile: a step large enough to be useful on random weights is far too large for weights that are already nearly right.

This is why differential learning rates exist, and why the usual recipe warms up the new head first while everything else is frozen: the head starts random, and its early gradients are large enough to damage anything they reach.

Every augmentation is a claim about the task

TransformWhat it assertsTrue for flowers?
horizontal flipmirror image is the same classyes
random resized cropa part identifies the wholeusually
colour jitterhue is not diagnosticdangerous — colour is a flower feature
vertical flipupside-down is the same classoften, but not for scenes
rotationorientation carries no informationyes here

A flipped digit is not the same digit, and a colour-jittered flower may not be the same species. Augmentation that asserts something false about the task does not regularise the model — it teaches it something wrong, quietly, and the validation set agrees because it is augmented too.

How much is too much

  • Augmentation trades bias for variance: it enlarges the effective training set and blurs the target
  • Too little and the model memorises 1,020 images. Too much and the training distribution stops resembling the test one
  • The symptom of too much: training accuracy stops rising and validation accuracy stops with it — both curves flat and low, which Lecture 5 named as bias
How to set it Start with flip and crop, measure, and add one transform at a time. Each addition is a hypothesis about an invariance of the task, and each can be tested the same way anything else is: change one thing, measure the difference, and check it is bigger than the fold-to-fold spread.

Augmenting at test time, deliberately

The opposite of the mistake two slides ago, and it is not a contradiction: augment the test image several times, predict each, and average the predictions.

  • It usually buys a point or so, for a proportional increase in inference cost
  • It is deterministic if you fix the set of transforms — a centre crop and its mirror, not a random crop
  • It is a prediction technique, not an evaluation one: the score it produces is still measured once, on the same held-out rows

Say which you are doing. “We used augmentation at test time” is ambiguous between a legitimate ensemble and the bug on the previous slide.

Where the time goes once the backbone is frozen

  • Feature extraction — one forward pass per image, once. This dominates, and it is embarrassingly parallel
  • Fitting the head — a linear model on a few thousand vectors; trivial by comparison
  • Augmentation — undoes the caching, because a different crop each epoch means a different feature vector each epoch
The trade you are actually making Augmentation and caching are incompatible. Cache the features and augmentation buys nothing; augment and you pay a full forward pass every epoch. On a small corpus the cache usually wins; on a large one it does not.

Augmentation is a training-time transform

It is easy to build one pipeline and hand it to both loaders. The training loader wants random crops and flips; the evaluation loader must not have them.

The condition, and what it costs An augmented evaluation set makes the score a random variable rather than a function of the weights. Score one fixed model twice and you get two different numbers. Measured here: the reported accuracy is 0.86 points pessimistic and carries 1.57 points of noise — larger than most of the differences this lecture asks you to judge.

Nothing raises. The only symptom is that the number moves when nothing else did.

The first is harmless. The third is not, and it is the one nobody notices, because a pessimistic number feels like the safe kind of wrong.

The check, in two lines


a = evaluate(model, val_loader)
b = evaluate(model, val_loader)
assert a == b, f"evaluation is not deterministic: {a} vs {b}"

Run every evaluation twice. If the two disagree, something in the evaluation path is random — augmentation, dropout still active, or a shuffled loader whose batches are averaged rather than pooled.

Why an assert and not a comment A comment saying “do not augment the validation set” is read once. This runs every time, costs two forward passes, and fails loudly at the moment the mistake is made rather than three lectures later.

One line to keep

Before you train a vision model from scratch, spend 20 seconds measuring what a frozen pretrained backbone and a linear head already give you.

On this application that number was 81.2% against 15.3%. You are entitled to train from scratch. You are not entitled to do it without knowing what you turned down.

When transfer fails

  • The domains genuinely differ. ImageNet is photographs. Medical scans, satellite bands, spectrograms and line drawings are not, and the middle blocks stop being useful sooner
  • The input is not an image at all. Reshaping tabular data into a square does not make convolution appropriate; there is no locality to exploit
  • You have plenty of labels. With enough data, training from scratch catches up and then passes a frozen backbone. Transfer is strongest exactly where labels are scarce
  • The task needs resolution the backbone discarded. Pooling bought invariance, and Lecture 14 has to put that resolution back
How to tell, cheaply Fit a linear head on frozen features and compare it with the trivial baseline. If the frozen features barely beat the baseline, the backbone does not understand your images and no amount of fine-tuning will hide it.

What each option costs

ApproachWhat is fittedRelative cost
From scratch4.8 M parameterslongest, and worst here

The frozen probe is cheap for a specific reason: with the backbone fixed, its output for a given image never changes, so you compute it once and fit the head on the cached features. That is not an optimisation, it is a consequence of freezing.

What this still cannot do

  • Say where the flower is. The model gives a label for a whole image and no location at all
  • Handle more than one object. Two species in one photograph is a question this architecture cannot express
  • Say how confident it is, defensibly. Softmax outputs are not calibrated probabilities, as Lecture 4 showed on a much simpler model

The first two are the subject of Lecture 14, and they need a different notion of what a correct answer is — which is why that lecture opens by deriving one.

If you keep one line from today

The line Do not learn what somebody has already learned for you. 1,020 images cannot pay for 4.8 million parameters, and they do not have to: the bottom of the stack is about photographs, not about flowers, and it transfers.

The measured version of that sentence is the ablation table: from 15.3% to well past it, on the same corpus, the same split and the same evaluation, by not training most of the network.

What we did

  1. Named the condition that made Lecture 12 fail: 1,020 images paying for 4.8 million parameters, most of which encode general facts about photographs
  2. Reused a backbone trained on a much larger corpus, and measured how far down the stack those features stay useful
  3. Froze it and fitted a linear head; then unfroze the last block with a smaller learning rate for the parts that were already good
  4. Met the batch-norm trap: running statistics keep updating even in a frozen block unless you stop them
  5. Added augmentation, and stated what each transform asserts about the task — a flipped flower is a flower, a flipped digit is not
  6. Ablated all three, and named the conditions under which transfer fails outright

Solutions — Lecture 12

1. A dense layer mapping a $3\times128\times128$ input to a $32\times128\times128$ output has…

$32\times3\times7\times7 = 4{,}704$. Neither $H$ nor $W$ appears.

  • One kernel per output channel, reused at every position
  • The image size is genuinely absent — that is what weight sharing means

2. State the difference between equivariance and invariance, and say which of the two segmentation cannot afford.

Equivariance: $f(T x) = T f(x)$. Invariance: $f(T x) = f(x)$. Segmentation cannot afford invariance.

  • Invariance discards the position, and the answer to a segmentation question is the position
  • Classification wants invariance for the same reason

Solutions — Lecture 12 (2)

3. Convolution is equivariant to translation on the interior of an image but not at the border. Give the reason.

Zero padding invents input that was not there, so a shift moves real content into invented content.

  • The identity assumes the operator sees the same neighbourhood before and after the shift
  • At the edge it does not, so the equality is an interior statement

4. A training run fails with out-of-memory. State which of parameters and activations usually dominates, and…

Activations dominate; halve the batch.

  • Activation memory is linear in the batch size and parameter memory does not depend on it at all
  • Reducing the parameter count is near the bottom of the list

Solutions — Lecture 12 (3)

5. Parameters and activations are anti-correlated across a convolutional stack. State where each is…

Activations are largest near the input, parameters near the output. Dense intuition points the wrong way.

  • Early layers have large maps and tiny kernels; late layers the reverse
  • In a dense network both grow together, so the habit transfers badly
