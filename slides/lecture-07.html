The idea

Ask a difficult question of a thousand random people and aggregate their answers. The aggregate is often better than an expert’s. The same is true of models, and it has a name: an ensemble.

A group of weak learners can form a strong learner — provided there are enough of them and they are sufficiently diverse.

“Sufficiently diverse” is doing all the work in that sentence, and it is the informal name of a quantity we are about to define. Before long it will have a symbol, a formula and a measured value on this dataset.

Why this lecture follows the one on trees

  • Averaging attacks variance, and trees are the high-variance model we have just measured: 9.1% of predictions move when 10% of the rows do
  • A tree is cheap to fit, so two hundred of them are affordable
  • An unconstrained tree has low bias — and averaging does nothing whatever to bias, so the members had better not have any to spare
That third point is why the ensembles in this lecture use unconstrained members. It looks reckless after a lecture spent limiting depth, and by the end of the mathematics it will look obvious.

The question

Forget trees for a moment.

You have $n$ predictors of the same quantity. Each has variance $\sigma^2$. You average them.

$$\bar{f} \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n} f_t$$

What is $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{f})$?

Ask for the answer before showing it. Almost everyone says σ²/n, which is the special case they were taught and is not the case in front of us.

Start with two, then take $n$

With $\operatorname{Var}(f_1) = \operatorname{Var}(f_2) = \sigma^2$ and $\operatorname{Corr}(f_1, f_2) = \rho$:

$$\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\tfrac{f_1 + f_2}{2}\right) = \tfrac{1}{4}\left[\sigma^2 + \sigma^2 + 2\rho\sigma^2\right] = \frac{\sigma^2(1+\rho)}{2}$$

$\rho = 0$ halves the variance; $\rho = 1$ leaves it at $\sigma^2$, and averaging achieves nothing. For $n$ identically distributed predictors with a common pairwise correlation $\rho$, the same expansion — $n$ variances and $n(n-1)$ covariances $\rho\sigma^2$, over $n^2$ — gives

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar{f}) \;=\; \rho\sigma^2 \;+\; \frac{(1-\rho)\,\sigma^2}{n}$$

The formula, in one picture

Everything above the dashed line can be averaged away. Nothing below it can.

The same formula, plotted

Four curves, four floors. The floor is set entirely by $\rho$ — which is why every ensemble variant you are about to meet is an attempt to lower it.

What this says before we build anything

  1. Averaging many models reduces variance, but only the part they do not share
  2. The return on more members collapses quickly. There is little point past the point where $(1-\rho)\sigma^2/n \ll \rho\sigma^2$
  3. Therefore the way to improve an ensemble is not more members. It is less correlated members
  4. And anything that lowers $\rho$ by making individual members worse is a trade, not a free lunch: $\sigma^2$ appears in both terms, so it can rise while $\rho$ falls

Point 4 is the one that gets forgotten, and it is the one we will measure — and be embarrassed by — later today.

Voting — the simplest ensemble

Combine different algorithms fitted to the same data.


from sklearn.ensemble import VotingClassifier

vote = VotingClassifier(
    [("tree",   DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=42)),
     ("logreg", LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000)),
     ("knn",    KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5))], voting="soft")
  • voting="hard" — majority of the predicted labels
  • voting="soft" — average of the predicted probabilities; usually better, because a confident member outweighs several hesitant ones

Different algorithms make different kinds of errors, which is the informal way of saying $\rho$ is small. The formula applies unchanged.

Bagging

Bootstrap aggregating. Draw $m$ rows from the training set with replacement, fit a tree, and repeat $n$ times.

  • Each bootstrap sample has the same size as the training set, but about 63% of its distinct rows — the rest are duplicates
  • Aggregate by majority vote for classification, by the mean for regression
  • Each member is individually worse than one tree fitted to the whole set. The ensemble is better — that is the formula, not a paradox

Pasting is the same thing without replacement. It produces less diverse members, so bagging usually wins — but it is a cheap thing to cross-validate rather than assume.

Bagging, in code


from sklearn.ensemble import BaggingClassifier

bag = BaggingClassifier(
    DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=42),   # unconstrained members
    n_estimators=200, bootstrap=True, oob_score=True,
    random_state=42, n_jobs=-1)

bag.fit(X_train, y_train)
print(f"out-of-bag {bag.oob_score_:.1%}")

The members are unconstrained trees. We want each one to have low bias, because averaging cannot remove bias — and we are about to average the variance away, so there is no reason to restrict their depth.

A validation set you get for nothing

Each member never sees about 37% of the rows — its out-of-bag instances. Score each member on its own out-of-bag rows and you have a generalisation estimate with no validation split and no extra fit.

Why 37% A given row is missed by one draw with probability $1 - 1/m$, so by all $m$ draws with probability $\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{m}\right)^{m} \to e^{-1} \approx 0.368$ as $m$ grows. Hence about 63% distinct rows in, 37% held out.

Cheap, and it does not replace the test set. It replaces the validation set. If out-of-bag comes out much better than the test score, something in your pipeline saw the out-of-bag rows anyway.

Reading an out-of-bag score

  • It is an average over members, each scored on a different 37% of the rows — so it estimates the generalisation of one member’s procedure, not of the ensemble you ship
  • It is available only where the members see bootstrap samples. With bootstrap=False — the extra-trees default — there are no out-of-bag rows and oob_score=True raises
  • Use it to compare configurations cheaply, then confirm the one you pick with cross-validation or the test set
When it bites If the out-of-bag score comes out much better than the test score, something in the pipeline saw the out-of-bag rows anyway — a scaler fitted outside the ensemble, or a feature built from the whole frame. The gap is a leakage detector you get for free.

Random forests

Bagging, plus one more source of difference: at every node of every tree, only a random subset of the features is considered for the split.


from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier

rnd = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=200, max_features="sqrt",
                             random_state=42, n_jobs=-1).fit(X_train, y_train)

max_features="sqrt" is the classification default: $\lfloor\sqrt{54}\rfloor = 7$ of the 54 columns, drawn afresh at each node. A tree with 200 nodes therefore draws 200 different subsets.

Extra-trees

One further step. Having chosen the candidate features, do not search for the best threshold — draw one at random for each candidate and keep the best of those.

  • Much faster: the threshold search is the expensive part of training a tree
  • On bootstrap samples, each member comes out a little worse than a forest’s (72.8% against 73.6%)
  • Scikit-Learn’s default is bootstrap=False — the random thresholds replace the bootstrap rather than joining it

That default is a decision somebody else made, it is not what most descriptions of extra-trees imply, and we are going to measure what it costs rather than argue about it.

What each variant randomises

Rows, columns, thresholds. Three knobs, and all three are turned for the same reason.

The claim this lecture is making

One formula, three algorithms Bagging randomises the rows. Random forests also randomise the columns. Extra-trees also randomise the thresholds. All three exist to reduce one quantity: $\rho$, the correlation term in $\operatorname{Var}(\bar{f}) = \rho\sigma^2 + (1-\rho)\sigma^2/n$.

That is the payoff of the derivation, and it is the thing to remember if you remember one thing from this lecture.

It is also a claim, and this course does not accept claims. Measure it.

Now the bill

We removed the variance. Ask what happened to the thing the agency asked for.


total = sum(t.get_n_leaves() for t in rnd.estimators_)
print(f"{total:,} leaves across the 200 trees")
  

1,497,919 leaves across the 200 trees

The justification for one prediction is now 200 decision paths and a vote.

The trade, drawn to scale

Fifteen points of accuracy, and four orders of magnitude more model to read.

What the vote share is, and is not

A single tree’s predict_proba returns the class proportions of one leaf: piecewise constant, identical for every patch reaching that leaf, and often built on a handful of training rows.

An ensemble averages 200 such vectors, each from a different leaf of a different tree. The result varies smoothly across the feature space and is better calibrated — one thing the agency asked for that averaging improves rather than destroys.

When it bites Better calibrated is not calibrated. A forest’s vote share is systematically pulled towards the middle, because unanimity is rare. If a downstream decision uses the number as a probability, check it against observed frequencies before believing it.

Feature importance, as the library offers it


imp = pd.Series(rnd.feature_importances_, index=X_train.columns)
print(imp.sort_values(ascending=False).head(5).round(3))

feature_importances_ sums, over every node that split on a feature, the weighted impurity reduction that split achieved — the $\Delta I$ of the previous lecture, accumulated.

It runs, it is fast, and the top of the list is entirely sensible: elevation first, then the distances. Nothing invites suspicion.

Before trusting any ranking, ask what it would look like on a variable you know carries no information. If you cannot answer, add one and find out.

Boosting — a different idea entirely

Everything so far trains members in parallel and averages them. Boosting trains them in sequence, each one correcting its predecessor.

The members are not identically distributed and are certainly not exchangeable, so today’s formula does not apply to boosting. Bagging reduces variance; boosting reduces bias.

Two consequences: boosting cannot be parallelised across members, and it is much easier to overfit with. Both follow from the sequence.

AdaBoost

  1. Fit a weak learner — typically a tree of depth 1, a decision stump
  2. Increase the weight of the instances it got wrong
  3. Fit the next learner on the reweighted data; repeat
  4. Predict by a weighted vote, each learner weighted by its own accuracy

from sklearn.ensemble import AdaBoostClassifier

ada = AdaBoostClassifier(DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=1),
                         n_estimators=200, learning_rate=0.5,
                         random_state=42)

If it overfits, reduce n_estimators or learning_rate, or regularise the base estimator.

Animation

Gradient boosting

Same sequence, different correction. Instead of reweighting the instances, fit each new learner to the residual errors of the ensemble so far.

  • learning_rate scales each member’s contribution. A low rate needs more members and generalises better — this is shrinkage
  • Too few members underfits; too many overfits. Use early stopping with n_iter_no_change
  • The number of members is therefore not a “more is better” parameter, which is exactly the opposite of bagging
When it bites Adding trees to a bagged ensemble can only help, because $(1-\rho)\sigma^2/n$ falls monotonically. Adding trees to a boosted ensemble eventually hurts. Carrying the first intuition into the second is one of the commonest mistakes with these models.

What practitioners actually reach for

HistGradientBoostingClassifier bins each feature into at most 255 buckets before growing anything, so a split search costs $O(\text{bins})$ rather than $O(m)$.


from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier

hgb = HistGradientBoostingClassifier(max_iter=100, learning_rate=0.05,
                                     early_stopping=False,
                                     random_state=42).fit(X_train, y_train)
  • Dramatically faster on a dataset this size, and it is the same algorithm LightGBM and XGBoost implement
  • Handles missing values and categorical columns natively — no imputer, no encoder
  • On tabular data it is the usual first choice, and on most benchmarks it is the strongest single model

On this dataset, at a budget that fits a lecture, it lands several points below the bagged forest. The notebook measures both, and measures what happens when you raise the learning rate. Usual is not always.

Stacking

Rather than voting or averaging, train a model to do the aggregation. The base learners’ predictions become the input features of a final estimator, the blender.

  • The blender must be trained on predictions the base learners made for instances they did not see — otherwise it is learning from memorised answers
  • StackingClassifier does this with cross-validation internally, which is the only reason it is safe to use
When it bites Build stacking by hand and the blender sees in-sample predictions, which are far too good. It then learns to trust whichever base learner overfits hardest, and the whole ensemble is worse than its best member — with no error and no warning.

Solutions — Lecture 6

1. A node holds 100 training patches: 50 of one species, 30 of a second and 20 of a third. Compute its Gini…

$G = 1 - (0.5^2 + 0.3^2 + 0.2^2) = 1 - 0.38 = 0.62$. A pure node scores 0.

  • The class distribution is $(0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$; the sum of squares, 0.38, is the probability that two draws with replacement are the same species, so 0.62 is the probability that they differ
  • A pure node has one $p_k = 1$, so the sum of squares is 1 and $G = 0$

2. On a two-class problem, entropy halved and Gini never differ by more than 0.055 at any node. Two candidate…

No. Each split’s weighted entropy halved is its weighted Gini plus a gap between 0 and 0.055, so the 0.08 difference shrinks to at least 0.025 and keeps its sign.

  • Halving entropy multiplies every candidate’s impurity by the same factor, so it changes no ranking
  • A weighted average of gaps that each lie between 0 and 0.055 also lies between 0 and 0.055; the two criteria can disagree only when the Gini difference is smaller than that — a close call
