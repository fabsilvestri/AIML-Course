Retrieval is not prediction The input is a query and a corpus; the output is a ranking of the corpus. The task

A claim arrives. Find the scientific abstracts that bear on it.

  • “0-dimensional biomaterials lack inductive properties.”
  • “Breast cancer development is determined exclusively by genetic factors.”

Nobody wants a yes or no. They want the evidence, ranked, so a human can read the top few and decide. That is the whole shape of the problem, and it is why the output is an ordering.

Why this corpus SciFact is small enough that we can score every document for every query exactly, with no approximate index in the way. Every number in this lecture and the next is therefore a property of the method, not of an index we did not examine.

The shape of every search system

1 Retrieve millions → hundreds. Must be cheap.
2 Re-rank hundreds → tens. Can be expensive.
3 Present tens → ten. Diversity, freshness, policy.

Today is stage 1, and it is stage 1 that decides the ceiling: a document the first stage never returns cannot be recovered by any later stage. Stage 2 is Lecture 20. Stage 3 is mostly not machine learning, and mostly not in this course.

This is why we will keep looking at recall@100 for a system whose output is ten results. The first stage is not judged on what the user sees; it is judged on what it makes possible.

Attention falls off a results page

Whatever we build, its output is read by a person, and that person’s attention is not uniform:

  • The first result is examined far more often than the second
  • Almost nobody reaches the tenth
  • The second page effectively does not exist
Consequence for the mathematics A metric that treats ranks 1 and 10 alike is measuring something nobody experiences. Every metric in this lecture’s derivation is an attempt to encode that decay — and the differences between them are differences of opinion about its shape.

Nothing in this lecture measures user attention: it is an assumption we import, and I am flagging it as one.

First: what is a term?

Before any weighting, a decision nobody writes up: how the text becomes a list of things to count. On a real claim from our corpus:

“0-dimensional biomaterials show inductive properties.”

0dimensionalbiomaterialsshowinductiveproperties

6 tokens, lower-cased, split on non-letters. Which is already three decisions — and 0-dimensional has just become 0 and dimensional, two terms that mean nothing apart.

The obvious first idea: Boolean retrieval

Return the documents that contain all the query terms. It is exact, it is fast with an inverted index, and it was how search worked for thirty years.

$$\text{result} = \bigcap_{t \in q} \text{postings}(t)$$

Over all 300 test claims, 98.0% match no abstract at all: an empty page, not a bad ranking. One word of the claim missing from an otherwise perfect abstract excludes it, and the failure is silent — an empty result looks like “no such document exists”.

The lesson that reorganises everything Stop filtering and start scoring. Every document gets a number, the user gets the largest, and a missing term costs something rather than everything.

Why retrieval is cheap

Scoring 5,183 documents per query by reading each one is hopeless at scale. The trick is 50 years old and it is a data structure, not a model.

The inverted index For every term, store the list of documents containing it, with the count. A query of four words then touches only the documents containing at least one of those four — not the corpus.
Vocabulary (distinct terms)35,734

The postings list is 0.3% of the dense matrix. Sparsity is not an optimisation here; it is the reason the whole field is possible.

Term frequency, and why it is not enough

A document mentioning “biomaterials” five times is probably more about biomaterials than one mentioning it once. So score by how often the query terms occur.

Two failures, immediately Common words dominate. Every abstract contains “of” and “the”, so a query containing them matches everything equally.

Long documents win. A 900-word abstract contains more of everything than a 100-word one, and is not thereby more relevant.

The rest of the method is two corrections, one for each failure — and each has a parameter, so each can be measured.

Saturation, and the parameter k₁

The first correction to raw counting: the tenth occurrence of a word should not count ten times the first.

$$\frac{\text{tf}\,(k_1+1)}{\text{tf} + k_1} \;\longrightarrow\; k_1 + 1 \ \text{ as tf} \to \infty$$
tf12351020
factor1.0001.3101.4621.6101.7431.818

From 1 to 2 occurrences the factor rises 0.310; from 10 to 20 it rises 0.075. The curve is bounded, so no single word can dominate a score by repetition — which is also, incidentally, the first anti-spam device in search.

Stop words, derived rather than declared

Classical systems shipped a hand-written list of words to delete: the, of, and, in, to … It works, and it is a liability:

  • The list is language-specific, and someone must maintain it
  • It is corpus-blind — in a corpus about the band The Who, deleting the and who destroys the query
  • The decision is binary: a word is deleted or kept, with nothing in between
idf replaces the list A weight of 0.0020 is not deletion, it is near-deletion — and it is computed from this corpus. If a word is common here, it is discounted here. Nobody maintains anything.

This is the same move as learning a representation instead of engineering features, arriving thirty years earlier.

Length normalisation, and the parameter $b$

$$\text{tf}' = \frac{\text{tf}}{\text{tf} + k_1\left(1 - b + b\,\frac{|d|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}$$
  • $b = 0$ — no normalisation; long documents keep their advantage
  • $b = 1$ — length divided out completely

BM25, assembled

$$\text{BM25}(q,d) \;=\; \sum_{t \in q} \mathrm{idf}(t)\; \frac{\text{tf}_{t,d}\,(k_1+1)}{\text{tf}_{t,d} + k_1\!\left(1-b+b\frac{|d|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}$$
  • The idf factor: rare terms count for more
  • The saturating tf: the tenth occurrence adds far less than the second, because $k_1$ bounds the growth
  • The length term: $b$ decides how much of the document’s length is divided out

Every piece is there because a specific failure demanded it. That is why BM25 has survived thirty years of replacement attempts, and it is the baseline every neural retriever is measured against — including the one in Lecture 20.

What the ablation actually says

  • idf is most of the method. Removing it costs 0.1261; without it, common words drown the rare ones that identify the document
  • Saturation is the one we did not isolate. Dropping saturation and length together costs 0.1809, and length alone costs 0.0120 — so most of that gap is saturation, but an experiment that removes two things at once cannot say how much
  • Length normalisation is nearly free here, worth 0.0120 — consistent with the $b$ sweep, and with abstracts being of similar length
The transferable part Not the ordering — that is a fact about SciFact. It is that each term of a formula can be removed and priced, and that a component nobody has priced is a component nobody has justified. The same procedure applies to every architecture in Part IV.

Precision and recall, at a cutoff

For one query we are given two things: the system’s ranked list $d_1, d_2, \ldots$ and the set $R$ of relevant documents. Throughout, one ranking: $|R| = 3$, and the relevant ones sit at ranks 1, 2 and 8.

✓✓·····✓··
12345678910
  • $\text{P}@k = \dfrac{|R \cap \text{top-}k|}{k}$ of what I showed you, how much was good
  • $\text{R}@k = \dfrac{|R \cap \text{top-}k|}{|R|}$ of what was good, how much I showed you

With $k=10$, all three are inside the top ten: $\text{R}@10 = \tfrac{3}{3} = 1$, perfect recall. Yet the third sits at rank 8, where a user may never look. Recall counts a set; it does not read an order.

Reciprocal rank: where is the first one?

$$\text{RR} = \frac{1}{\text{rank of the first relevant document}}$$

Our example finds one at rank 1, so $\text{RR} = 1/1 = 1.0000$. Averaged over queries this is MRR, and over all 300 claims BM25 scores 0.6350.

Smooth, bounded, and it moves when the order moves. But it stops reading after the first hit — our ranking would score 1.0000 whether the other two abstracts were at ranks 2 and 8 or nowhere at all.

Which makes MRR right for one kind of question — “is there a good answer near the top?” — and wrong for “did I find the evidence?”

NDCG: discounted gain, normalised

$$\text{DCG}@k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{g_i}{\log_2(i+1)}$$

A gain $g_i$ for how relevant the document at rank $i$ is (here 1 or 0), and a discount for how far down it sits. The $+1$ keeps rank 1 from dividing by zero; the logarithm itself is a convention about users, not a derived result.

The rule Choose the metric from the question, before you see any scores. Choosing it afterwards, from the numbers, is how a paper reports recall@100 for the system that lost on NDCG@10 — and it is the single commonest dishonesty in this literature.

Averaging over queries

One more choice, and it is usually made silently. Given per-query scores, do you average the scores, or pool the counts?

  • Macro — mean of the per-query metric. Every query counts equally, including one with a single relevant document
  • Micro — pool hits and totals across queries first. Queries with many relevant documents dominate

Every number in this lecture is macro: the mean over 300 claims of that claim’s own metric. MRR and NDCG are essentially always reported that way; precision and recall sometimes are not, and the two can differ substantially.

The variance across queries is large and almost never reported. A mean NDCG of 0.6611 contains claims scoring 1 and claims scoring 0.

Measured on one run of this lecture’s notebook; hardware, library versions and random seeds can make a re-run differ slightly.

What none of these metrics measure

  • Latency. A ranking that takes a minute has a real score of zero, and no metric here notices
  • Redundancy. Ten identical relevant documents score perfectly and serve the user once
  • The unjudged. Anything the pool never saw is counted as irrelevant — the subject of the last section
  • Harm. A confidently ranked wrong answer and a merely absent one score identically
Report the failure cases, in this setting Every one of these is a real requirement that the reported number is silent about. A metric is a choice of what to look at, and it is therefore also a choice of what to ignore.

What a relevance judgement is

Every metric so far needed the set $R$. Where does it come from? A person decided. A relevance judgement is one recorded decision about one pair:

querydocumentjudgement
a claiman abstract1 = relevant, 0 = not
  • Relevant means: a reader with this query would want to see this document. In SciFact, an abstract is relevant to a claim when it contains evidence about it — for or against. Relevant does not mean “agrees”
  • The judgements for a test set are stored as a table of such rows, called the qrels (query relevance judgements)
  • $R$ for a query is simply the documents its qrels mark relevant
So every score is a comparison NDCG, AP, MRR: each compares the system’s ranking with somebody’s judgements. If the judgements are wrong or incomplete, the score is too — and nothing in the arithmetic will tell you.

Nobody judges every pair

To know $R$ exactly, someone would have to read every abstract against every claim:

300 claims × 5,183 abstracts1.55 million pairs
Pairs judged relevant in SciFact’s test qrels339
As a fraction of all pairs0.0218%

That is for a small corpus. For a web collection, judging every pair is not expensive; it is impossible. So in practice only a small set of pairs is ever looked at, and a convention covers the rest:

The convention under every number in this lecture A pair that nobody judged is scored as not relevant. Not because anyone read it and said no — because nobody read it at all.

So the real question is which pairs get looked at. The next two slides show the standard answer, pooling, and the one SciFact used.

Pooling, and what it misses

The standard way large benchmarks choose what to judge:

1 Run several existing systems on every query
2 Pool take each one’s top $k$, and merge
3 Judge people label only the pooled pairs
  • A relevant document that no pooled system ranked highly is never judged — so it is scored as irrelevant
  • A new method that finds it is then charged with a miss: it is penalised for succeeding
  • The pool was built from the systems of its day, so an old benchmark quietly favours methods that resemble them
Failure condition — everything unjudged counted as irrelevant The metric cannot tell “retrieved an unjudged relevant document” from “retrieved junk”. Gains on a mature benchmark are partly gains at matching the pool, which is why a leaderboard difference of a point or two is rarely worth defending.

How SciFact’s judgements were made

SciFact did not pool. Its judgements come from citations:

  1. Start from a sentence in a published paper that cites other papers
  2. An expert annotator rewrites that sentence as a short, checkable claim
  3. Annotators read the abstracts that sentence cited, and mark each one that supports or refutes the claim
  4. Those marked abstracts become the claim’s relevant documents
Reliable Experts read each cited abstract and marked the sentences carrying the evidence: a judgement records evidence, not a hunch from a title.
Sparse Only the cited abstracts were ever read: 1.13 relevant per claim, and 92.3% of claims have exactly one. An uncited abstract with the same evidence counts as a miss.

So read BM25’s 0.6611 as agreement with what the claims’ sources cited, not as how useful its ranking is to a reader. An uncited abstract carrying the same evidence, ranked first, is scored exactly like junk.

Graded relevance: when relevant has degrees

SciFact’s judgements are binary: 1 or 0. Many collections instead ask judges for a grade, because an abstract that directly tests a claim and one that mentions it in passing are not equally useful.

The grade becomes the gain $g_i$ in DCG. Two common choices:

Grade $\text{rel}_i$Linear gain $\text{rel}_i$Exponential gain $2^{\text{rel}_i}-1$
0 — not relevant00
1 — fair11
2 — good23
3 — excellent37

With binary judgements the two coincide, which is why our NDCG never had to choose. With grades they do not: the exponential makes one excellent document worth seven fair ones, the linear only three.

Graded relevance changes the answer

One query, two systems, top three results. Discounts $1/\log_2(i+1)$ are $1$, $0.63$ and $0.5$ at ranks 1, 2 and 3.

Rank 1Rank 2Rank 3DCG, linearDCG, exponential
System Afairfairnot relevant1 + 0.63 = 1.631 + 0.63 = 1.63
System Bnot relevantnot relevantexcellent3 × 0.5 = 1.57 × 0.5 = 3.5

Same query, so the same ideal DCG divides both, and the order of the NDCGs is the order of the DCGs. Linear gain prefers A; exponential gain prefers B. Nothing about the systems changed — only the gain function.

What to do with that The exponential says one excellent result at rank 3 beats two fair ones at the top. That is a claim about users, not about mathematics. Whoever reports NDCG on graded judgements must say which gain they used, and be ready to defend it.

Five questions to ask of any retrieval result

  1. Which metric, and at what cutoff? “Better retrieval” with no metric named is not a claim
  2. Against which baseline? If BM25 is absent from the table, ask why — it is free, and it is often close
  3. Whose judgements, produced how? Pooled from which systems, or taken from citations? How many per query? Graded or binary, and with which gain?
  4. Same corpus, same queries, same preprocessing? A different tokeniser is a different experiment
  5. How large is the difference against the spread across queries? A gain of 0.005 on 300 queries is not a finding

The third question is the one this section added: the ground truth is itself a measurement, made by someone, under constraints.

Solutions — Lecture 18

1. Scaled dot-product attention divides by $\sqrt{d_k}$. State what the variance of the unscaled dot product is,…

$\operatorname{Var}(s) = d_k$, so $s$ has standard deviation $\sqrt{d_k}$; without the division the softmax saturates and the gradient vanishes.

  • A sum of $d_k$ independent zero-mean products, each of variance 1, has variance $d_k$ — at $d_k = 64$ typical scores are already of order 8
  • Large logits make the distribution nearly one-hot, so almost no gradient flows

2. A decoder must not attend to positions after the one it is predicting. State the mechanism that enforces it,…

A mask applied to the attention scores before the softmax, setting future positions to $-\infty$. Without it the model sees the token it is being asked to predict.

  • Attention is all-to-all by construction, so nothing in the mechanism itself respects order
  • The training loss would fall and the model would be useless at generation time, when the future genuinely does not exist

Solutions — Lecture 18 (2)

3. A pretrained body is fine-tuned at $10^{-3}$, the rate that worked for the from-scratch model. Predict what…

The first steps overwrite the pretrained features, and the model is left to relearn them from the labels alone, badly. The lecture fine-tunes at $2\times10^{-5}$.

  • A pretrained block already encodes something; a step tuned for random initialisation is far too large for weights that are already nearly right
  • Fifty times smaller than the $10^{-3}$ that trained the model from scratch — large steps destroy what was pretrained

4. A tf-idf vectoriser is fitted on every document of a 400-review corpus, and the matrix is split afterwards.…

On the training rows only, inside the pipeline. A null result, and a leaked score can equal an honest one, so the number cannot tell you which you are holding — only the procedure can.

  • 0.30 points on 400 reviews is not distinguishable from zero: the seeds fall on both sides of it
  • A small number is not a clean one: the procedure is what separates a leaked score from an honest one, which is why the procedure is what gets checked in review

Solutions — Lecture 18 (3)

5. A corpus contains duplicate documents across the train/test split. State why no pipeline fixes it, and say…

Nothing was fitted wrongly — the rows were separated wrongly. Split by entry, so that every copy of a document falls on the same side.

  • fit and transform were used correctly throughout
  • The difference is entirely which rows the split separates: measured, the random split reads 7.83 points higher than the split grouped by entry
