Where we are

Three lectures of vision, and every one of them answers the same question: what is in this image?

  • Lecture 12 — a convolutional network, built and trained from scratch, gives one label per image
  • Lecture 13 — a backbone pretrained on someone else’s labels gives a much better one, cheaply: probe it frozen first, then let the last block move at a smaller rate, with batch-norm statistics left alone and augmentation applied to the training loader only
What neither can express Where the object is, and how many there are. A photograph with three cyclists in it has one label and three answers, and nothing in the architecture can say so.

Today the output stops being a label and becomes a set of boxes — which means the first thing we need is a new notion of what a correct answer is. That is the derivation.

Failure condition — two box formats in one notebook COCO stores [x, y, w, h] and torchvision expects corners. Read [100, 50, 20, 30] as corners and every box wider than its own left edge gets $x_2 < x_1$ — almost all of them, with no error. Intersection over union

$$\mathrm{IoU}(A,B) \;=\; \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|} \;=\; \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A| + |B| - |A \cap B|}$$ also called the Jaccard index
  • The second form is the one you compute: the union is never built
  • Subtracting the intersection is not an optimisation — add the two areas and you have counted the overlap twice
  • Range $[0,1]$, and it is exactly 1 only when $A = B$

Past 100 pixels there is nothing to descend

Use $1 - \mathrm{IoU}$ as a loss. Fix one $100 \times 100$ box, slide a second one right by $d$ pixels, and ask autograd for the gradient.

Once the boxes are disjoint the intersection is clamped at zero, so IoU is constant: gradient exactly $0$, not merely small.

The repair: put the empty space back in

Let $C$ be the smallest axis-aligned box containing both $A$ and $B$.

$$\mathrm{GIoU}(A,B) \;=\; \mathrm{IoU}(A,B) \;-\; \frac{|C| - |A \cup B|}{|C|}$$ generalised IoU
  • The subtracted term is the fraction of the enclosing box that is neither prediction nor truth
  • When the boxes are disjoint and move apart, $|C|$ grows and $|A \cup B|$ does not, so the penalty grows
  • Range $(-1, 1]$, and it equals IoU whenever $C = A \cup B$

Average precision, from a ranking

Rank one class’s detections by score. Each is a true positive if its IoU with a still-unmatched annotation is at least $t$. AP is the area under the running maximum of precision, $p_{\text{env}}(r) = \max_{\tilde r \geq r} p(\tilde r)$: detection’s interpolated AP, which COCO’s evaluator samples at 101 recall levels. Scikit-learn’s average_precision_score, used in Lecture 3, takes no maximum: it averages the raw precision at each positive.

Precision is not monotone (right, ranks 51 to 140: 7 steps down and 77 up), as Lecture 3 proved; the maximum is what repairs it.

The first mean: over classes

$$\mathrm{mAP}(t) \;=\; \frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{c \in \mathcal{C}} \mathrm{AP}_c(t)$$ $\mathcal{C}$ = the classes present in the corpus
  • Unweighted. A class with one annotation counts as much as person with 350
  • We average over the 73 classes that appear in our 128 images. The other 7 of COCO’s 80 have no annotations here and no AP to average

That choice — which classes are in the mean — is a decision, it changes the number, and papers do not always say which one they made.

The second mean: over IoU thresholds

$$\mathrm{mAP}_{[.5:.95]} \;=\; \frac{1}{10}\sum_{t \in \{0.50,\,0.55,\,\ldots,\,0.95\}} \mathrm{mAP}(t)$$
  • At $t = 0.50$ a box that half-covers the object counts as correct
  • At $t = 0.95$ it must be very nearly exact
  • Averaging the ten is COCO’s headline number, and it is what “mAP” means unqualified in a modern paper

Step 2 · The detector, off the shelf

import torch
from torchvision.models.detection import (
    fasterrcnn_resnet50_fpn, FasterRCNN_ResNet50_FPN_Weights)

weights = FasterRCNN_ResNet50_FPN_Weights.COCO_V1
model = fasterrcnn_resnet50_fpn(weights=weights)
model.eval().to(DEVICE)                   # eval(), every time
preprocess = weights.transforms()

print(len(weights.meta["categories"]), "label slots")
print(weights.meta["_metrics"])

Step 5 · There is already a cut-off, and it is not yours

Step 6 · The count is a function of the threshold

Nothing in the model chose 0.5. Move the cut-off and the answer to the stakeholder’s question changes by a factor of ten.

Pick one, and write down why

Our operating point 0.5, because it is the conventional reporting threshold and because it was chosen before looking at the error curve.

The minimum of the right-hand panel is at 0.75, with MAE 1.92. Adopting that would be choosing a hyperparameter on the same data you then report — Lecture 2's error, in a new costume. We will quote it as a diagnostic and not as a result.

Someone always asks why we do not just use 0.75. This is the answer, and it is worth two extra minutes: the 128 images are the only data we have, so tuning on them and reporting on them is the same set twice.

Non-maximum suppression: the rule

The box_nms_thresh of Step 5. Greedy, per class, and it deletes information that cannot be recovered afterwards.

Animation

Suppression, in code

from torchvision.ops import batched_nms

keep = batched_nms(torch.tensor(boxes),      # (N, 4), corners
                   torch.tensor(scores),     # (N,)
                   torch.tensor(labels),     # (N,) — per class
                   iou_threshold=0.5)

assert len(keep) <= len(boxes), "suppression cannot add boxes"
boxes, scores, labels = boxes[keep], scores[keep], labels[keep]

batched_nms rather than nms: a bottle overlapping a glass must not suppress it, because they are different classes and both are true.

Before and after, on one image

Same model, same image, same score threshold. The only difference is one IoU comparison between pairs of boxes.

The left-hand count is 300 because that is where the un-suppressed run’s candidate cap sits, and every one of them clears score 0.5. It is a floor, not a total, and the figure says so.

And it is another knob

The default of 0.5 happens to be near the best value on our corpus. That is a fact about this corpus, not a law.

Tracking: one identity across frames, in four lines

  1. Predict where each existing track’s box will be in this frame
  2. Compute IoU between every prediction and every new detection
  3. Solve the assignment: pair them up to maximise total IoU
  4. Unmatched detections start new tracks; unmatched tracks age and die
Where it fails, and why it is IoU’s fault again An object that is occluded for ten frames reappears somewhere its predicted box does not overlap. IoU is zero, the assignment finds nothing, and the object is issued a new identity. The same flatness, in a new place.
Failure condition — identity carried by overlap alone A tracker that matches boxes by IoU loses an object the moment it is occluded for long enough to reappear somewhere else. It does not report a lost track; it reports a new one, and the count it hands the stakeholder is wrong in a way the video makes obvious and the number does not.

Semantic segmentation: a label on every pixel

A box was always an approximation: a bicycle’s box is mostly not bicycle, and for a picking arm “somewhere in this rectangle” may not be good enough.

  • Output: one class label per pixel. Shape $H \times W$, not $N \times 4$
  • Two people standing together are one person region. You cannot count them
  • The architectural problem is that pooling destroyed the resolution the answer needs — which is Lecture 12's invariance, arriving as a cost
  • The repair is upsampling with skip connections: transposed convolutions that recover spatial detail from the earlier, higher-resolution layers

Instance segmentation

  • Output: one binary mask per detected object, plus its class and score
  • Mask R-CNN is Faster R-CNN with one extra head that predicts a small mask inside each proposed box
  • Two lines of code away from what you already ran
from torchvision.models.detection import (
    maskrcnn_resnet50_fpn, MaskRCNN_ResNet50_FPN_Weights)

model = maskrcnn_resnet50_fpn(
    weights=MaskRCNN_ResNet50_FPN_Weights.COCO_V1).eval().to(DEVICE)
out = model([preprocess(img).to(DEVICE)])[0]
print(out["masks"].shape)      # (N, 1, H, W), soft masks in [0, 1]

Scoring a mask is the same argument again

  • Mask AP replaces box IoU with mask IoU: intersecting pixel sets instead of rectangles
  • Everything above — the ranking, the envelope, the area, the two means — is unchanged
  • torchvision reports 34.6 mask mAP for its Mask R-CNN weights on the full 5,000, against 37.9 box mAP for the same model

A mask is harder than a box, which is what those two numbers say. We do not measure mask AP on our corpus; the pixel-level annotations are in the file and the code is an exercise.

Design exercise · in the manner of an ML system-design interview

The task: a parking-analytics company’s occupancy product

The brief A parking-analytics company flies drones over car parks and sells operators and cities, per image, how many vehicles are parked and where each one is — a count and boxes — so an operator checks bays against bookings and a city plans. The business measures contracts kept, their accuracy clause, and the cost of a count proved wrong.
  • Given: a few hundred drone images from a dozen sites, each vehicle boxed and classed — car, van, lorry, motorcycle — by a contractor; a pretrained detector
  • Scale: a few hundred vehicles per full site; images several thousand pixels across; several flights a day per site
  • Consumers of the output: the operators’ dashboard and booking check, the cities’ planners

Nine steps follow, each with the pitfall that catches a first attempt and what to do about it. The model at the centre is today’s: an off-the-shelf detector, scored by IoU matching and average precision.

Step 1 — Frame it: boxes, a count, and what a correct answer is

  • Output per image: boxes in corner form, a class and a score for each, and one integer count
  • A predicted box is correct when its IoU with a still-unmatched annotation of the same class clears a threshold stated in advance; the count is a separate number with its own error, absolute and signed
  • Fix the box convention once, at the edge: the tool exports a corner and a size, the detector returns two corners; convert on load and assert that the second corner lies below and to the right
Pitfall — the count alone as the metric A detector that misses the cars under the trees and doubles the ones in the sun returns the right total: the count error is near zero, the boxes are wrong, and the operator’s bay check fails. Score the boxes by IoU matching, and the count beside them with its sign.

Step 2 — The annotations: what the contractor’s boxes are worth

  • A box drawn on a drone image is a judgement: does it include the shadow, the open door, the roof rack; is a car half under a canopy annotated at all; is a trailer a vehicle
  • Write the rule down, give the same images to two annotators, and measure their IoU on the same vehicles
Pitfall — a threshold the annotators cannot meet Two careful people boxing the same car from the air disagree by more than the strict thresholds allow, so at those thresholds a perfect detector is scored wrong by the annotation’s own noise, and the headline mean over thresholds is capped by the annotators, not by the model. Report the inter-annotator IoU beside the evaluation, take the loose threshold as the operating number, and send the disagreements back before anything is scored.

Step 3 — Split by site, and keep the empty tarmac in

  • Hold whole sites out, as a new customer’s is: tiles from the same flight share the vehicles, light and paint, so a split by tile scores memory
  • Most of every image is background — tarmac, painted bays, drain covers, shadows — and the evaluation set must hold it in its true proportion; report the baselines of no boxes and one box per tile
Pitfall — the background as the dominant class, dropped A first attempt keeps the tiles with vehicles and discards the empty ones as “nothing to annotate”. The false positives on empty bays — the markings and drain covers a detector likes — are never counted, and the dashboard overstates quiet sites. Keep the empty tiles, annotated as empty, and report the boxes returned on them.

Step 4 — Representation: the vehicle at the detector’s input size

  • The detector’s transform resizes the image to its own working size: a drone frame several thousand pixels across is shrunk several-fold, and a car that was forty pixels long becomes a handful
  • The backbone’s pooling discarded resolution on purpose; a detector keeps what it can, but not an object a few pixels across
Pitfall — small objects at that resolution Resize the whole frame and the vehicles at the far edge of an oblique shot vanish below what the feature maps can resolve. The AP is fine on the near, large vehicles and zero on the far, small ones, and the mean hides the second. Tile the frame at native resolution, or fly lower, and check it with an AP per object size.

Step 5 — The model: the detector as it ships, and its three defaults

  • Load the pretrained detector in eval() mode; nothing is trained; run under inference mode
  • Three settings nobody chose: a score cut-off, a suppression threshold, and a cap on detections per image. A full site holds more vehicles than the default cap, so the count saturates: raise it before measuring
  • Pick the score cut-off before looking at the error curve, and say so in the contract; the minimum of that curve on your own images is a diagnostic, not a result
Pitfall — non-maximum suppression Greedy, per class: a car parked bumper to bumper with the next, seen obliquely, overlaps it by more than the threshold and the second is deleted; the count comes out one short per row, and nothing raises. Suppress per class, raise the threshold, and check it on the packed rows — the sweep on your own corpus says what it does here, not in general.

Step 6 — Failure condition: where it fails, by size and by place

  • The detector misses what is small, shaded or partly covered: the near half of an oblique frame is easy and the far half is not, and one AP over the whole site averages them
  • Bucket the annotations by box area and compute AP for each bucket, as the reference evaluator does; and the count error by region of the frame
Pitfall — evaluation per object size, skipped The single AP is carried by the large near vehicles, the small-object AP is a fraction of it, and the customer’s complaint — the far rows are always under-counted — is invisible in the number reported. Report AP per size bucket and the signed count error per region, and fix the small ones at the input, not the threshold.

Step 7 — Evaluation: what the one number hides

  • mAP is a mean over classes of a mean over thresholds: the motorcycle class, with a handful of boxes, counts as much as cars with thousands, and the strict thresholds are the ones the annotators cannot meet
  • Check your implementation against the reference evaluator on the same predictions before quoting it
  • A few hundred images flatter a detector, in the easy direction more often than not: say the sample size beside every number
Pitfall — a headline from your own evaluator on a small set A mAP computed by code you wrote, on a few hundred images, several points above the published figure for the same weights, goes in the contract as a gain; the gain is the sample, and possibly the evaluator. Match the reference to within rounding first, then state the sample size, the operating threshold and the per-class APs with each figure.

Step 8 — In production: tiles, seams, and the barrier count

  • A site image is cut into overlapping tiles at native resolution, each run through the detector, the boxes mapped back to the frame
  • The barrier’s ticket count is a free ground truth for the total: a new detector runs in shadow beside the old on every flight, its signed error against the barrier read before it takes over, then watched for a step — new paint, snow, a canopy
Pitfall — tiled inference and its seams A car cut by a tile edge is two half-cars, one in each tile, and both are detected — or neither, if each half is too small to clear the cut-off; the count doubles along every seam. Overlap the tiles by more than a vehicle’s length, map the boxes to the frame, run suppression once more across it; then check the count against the barrier’s.

Step 9 — Ship it: the pipeline, and the pitfalls in one table

What we did

  1. Established that a label is not an answer when the question is where and how many
  2. Defined IoU, and saw it is exactly zero for every pair of disjoint boxes — so as a loss it gives no gradient however far apart they are; GIoU repairs that with a term that keeps moving
  3. Built average precision on a ranking, with a maximum in its definition because precision is not monotone (Lecture 3)
  4. Read mAP as a mean over classes of a mean over IoU thresholds, and saw how much each averaging hides
  5. Ran a detector, chose a score cut-off before looking and defended it, checked our mAP against a reference evaluator, and saw 128 images flatter it
  6. Applied non-maximum suppression, tracked by IoU, and looked at what a box cannot express: semantic against instance segmentation
  7. Designed the occupancy product a parking-analytics company sells to operators and cities in nine steps — boxes scored by IoU beside a signed count, an annotation rule measured between annotators, empty tarmac kept in the evaluation, tiles at native resolution for the small vehicles, suppression checked on packed rows, AP per object size, the evaluator matched to the reference with the sample size stated, and tiles merged with suppression across the seams — each step named for the pitfall it avoids

Solutions — Lecture 13

1. State the order in which a pretrained network’s head must be replaced and its body frozen, and what…

Freeze first, then replace. In the other order the new head is frozen too.

  • A newly constructed module has requires_grad=True, so the freezing loop would turn it off
  • Nothing is left to train: counting the trainable parameters gives 0 instead of 52,326

2. Fine-tuning uses $10^{-4}$ on the pretrained block and $10^{-3}$ on the new head. Justify the difference in…

The block already encodes something and the head starts from noise; one rate cannot suit both.

  • A large step destroys what the block knows
  • A small step leaves the random head untrained

Solutions — Lecture 13 (2)

3. Freezing a batch-norm layer’s weights does not freeze the layer. State what still changes, and the call…

The running statistics are buffers, updated in the forward pass; .eval() stops them.

  • requires_grad=False governs gradients, not buffers
  • A frozen feature extractor left in train mode still drifts

4. An augmented validation loader is used to select the epoch to keep. State the two things this costs, and the…

The score becomes a random variable and the chosen epoch can change. Evaluate twice and require identical answers.

  • Augmentation makes the metric depend on the evaluator’s seed
  • A deterministic function of fixed weights and fixed data does not wobble

Solutions — Lecture 13 (3)

5. A frozen-backbone probe is both more accurate and faster than training from scratch on 1,020 images. Explain…

The backbone is a fitted function reused for free; the feature extraction pass must still be counted in the total.

  • Only the head is fitted, so the variance problem moves to a dataset large enough to absorb it
  • The probe is not free just because the head is
