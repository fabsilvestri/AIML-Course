Regularisation and the bias–variance trade-off

The decomposition from today, measured the only way it can be: by drawing training set after training set from the same source and watching the fits disagree. Then the two curves that diagnose a model, and the geometry that makes lasso select and ridge merely shrink. Press Step to advance one unit and read what happened; press Play to let it run.

All data on this page is synthetic, generated in your browser from a fixed seed, so Reset always gives back the same picture. Being synthetic, it has something the Titanic never will: a known truth $f^{*}$, so bias and noise can be separated.

1Bias and variance, made visible

One truth $f^{*}(x) = \sin(1.5\pi x)$, plus noise of standard deviation $\sigma = 0.3$. Each step draws a new training set $D$ of $m$ points from that source and fits a polynomial of the chosen degree to it by least squares. The thin curves are the fits $\hat f_D$; the thick one is their average $\bar f$; the green one is the truth. On the right, the three terms of the decomposition beside the test error they add up to.

The mathematics

Fix a test point $x$ and write $\bar f(x) = \mathbb{E}_D[\hat f_D(x)]$. Adding and subtracting $\bar f$ and then $f^{*}$, exactly as in the deck, gives

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbb{E}_{D,y}\bigl[(y - \hat f_D)^2\bigr] = {} & \underbrace{(f^{*} - \bar f)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} \\ & + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_D\bigl[(\hat f_D - \bar f)^2\bigr]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}.\end{aligned}$$

Both cross terms vanish because $\mathbb{E}_D[\hat f_D - \bar f] = 0$ by the definition of $\bar f$, and $\mathbb{E}_y[y] = f^{*}$. Here the label is $y = f^{*}(x) + \varepsilon$ with $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2$, so $\operatorname{Var}(y \mid x) = \sigma^2$. The widget averages each term over 1,000 test points, which is legitimate because the identity holds at every $x$ and is linear.

The expectation over $D$ is done by brute force: the average over the training sets drawn so far. With one fixed label per test point, the first step of the derivation is an exact identity about those averages, and the side panel prints its residual. The two bars differ only by the sampling error of the 1,000 test labels, whose squared noise does not average to exactly $\sigma^2$.

Watch for
  • At degree 1 every fit is nearly the same straight line, and their average is far from the truth: small variance, large bias.
  • At degree 12 the average $\bar f$ sits almost on the truth while the individual fits swing wildly at the edges: small bias, large variance.
  • The stacked bar and the measured bar agree to within about four per cent at every degree and every $m$. The decomposition is not a model of the error; it is the error, rearranged.
Try this
  1. Step through degrees 0 to 12 on the bottom plot. The total is smallest at degree 3: an interior minimum, where falling bias and rising variance cross.
  2. Play to 200 sets at degree 9, then again with $m = 80$: the variance falls more than tenfold. Do the same at degree 1: the bias² reads 0.181 at both sizes. More data cures variance, not bias.
  3. Step slowly at degree 3 and watch the variance in the readout. It is an estimate from the sets drawn so far: about 0.0025 after two sets, 0.015 after ten, 0.0175 after 200. A brute-force expectation needs many draws.

2Learning and validation curves

The same kind of source, now with noise $\sigma = 0.5$, so the floor no model can beat is $\sigma^2 = 0.25$. Each step gives the model five more training rows. The left-hand plot is the learning curve: error against the number of rows, for the chosen degree. The right-hand one is the validation curve: error against degree, at the current number of rows. Below them, the first training set and its fit. Every point on the curves is an average over 40 independent training sets; validation error is measured on 2,000 fresh points.

The mathematics

The validation error estimates the left-hand side of the decomposition, $\text{bias}^2 + \text{variance} + \sigma^2$. The training error is measured on the rows that were fitted, so the fit has already bent towards their noise and the variance term barely shows in it. Hence the reading the deck gives:

$$\underbrace{\text{validation} - \sigma^2}_{\text{excess over the floor}} \approx \text{bias}^2 + \text{variance},$$

while the gap, validation minus training, tracks the variance.

More rows shrink the variance of a least-squares fit and leave its bias where it is, because $\bar f$ — the best the family can do on average — does not depend on how many rows you have once there are enough of them.

Watch for
  • Degree 1: the two curves meet by about 75 rows and stay together, both about 0.18 above the floor. That is bias; more rows change nothing.
  • Degree 12: the validation curve starts off the top of the scale and is still falling at 150 rows, with a gap still open. That is variance; more rows would help.
  • On the validation curve, the right-hand side flattens as rows arrive: high degree costs less when there is more data to pin it down.
Try this
  1. Play to 150 rows at degree 1, then switch to degree 12 and play again. Compare the two diagnoses in the narration.
  2. At 15 rows, read the validation curve: its minimum is at degree 3 and everything from degree 8 up is off the scale. Play to 150 and read it again.
  3. Degree 3 is close to the floor at 150 rows: its validation error is less than $0.01$ above $\sigma^2$. That is all any model, of any family, could still win on this source.

3Ridge against lasso

A linear model with two standardised features and no intercept to worry about. The ellipses are contours of the $\mathrm{MSE}$, centred on the unpenalised answer $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$ (green cross). Each step raises the penalty $\alpha$. The shaded region is the set of coefficient vectors no larger than the current answer — a disc for ridge, a diamond for lasso — and the answer is where the smallest contour touches it. Below, the coefficient paths so far.

The mathematics

The deck's two objectives, with columns standardised and $y$ centred so that there is no bias term to exclude:

$$\begin{aligned}J_{\text{ridge}} &= \mathrm{MSE}(\boldsymbol\theta) + \frac{\alpha}{m}\sum_j \theta_j^2,\\ J_{\text{lasso}} &= \mathrm{MSE}(\boldsymbol\theta) + 2\alpha\sum_j |\theta_j|.\end{aligned}$$

Ridge has the closed form $\boldsymbol\theta = (\mathbf X^{\intercal}\mathbf X + \alpha\mathbf I)^{-1}\mathbf X^{\intercal}y$. Lasso is solved one coordinate at a time: with $z_j = \tfrac1m\mathbf x_j^{\intercal}(y - \sum_{k\ne j}\theta_k\mathbf x_k)$ and $\tfrac1m\mathbf x_j^{\intercal}\mathbf x_j = 1$, setting the subgradient to zero gives $\theta_j = \operatorname{sign}(z_j)\max(|z_j| - \alpha, 0)$ — exactly zero whenever the data's pull $|z_j|$ is at most $\alpha$.

Why the picture is honest. If $\boldsymbol\theta_\alpha$ minimises $\mathrm{MSE} + \alpha\,\Omega$, then for every $\boldsymbol\theta$ with $\Omega(\boldsymbol\theta) \le \Omega(\boldsymbol\theta_\alpha)$, $\mathrm{MSE}(\boldsymbol\theta) \ge \mathrm{MSE}(\boldsymbol\theta_\alpha) + \alpha\bigl(\Omega(\boldsymbol\theta_\alpha) - \Omega(\boldsymbol\theta)\bigr) \ge \mathrm{MSE}(\boldsymbol\theta_\alpha)$. So $\boldsymbol\theta_\alpha$ is the best point of the region $\Omega \le \Omega(\boldsymbol\theta_\alpha)$: the region drawn.

Watch for
  • The lasso answer runs into a corner of the diamond and stays there while $\alpha$ grows: $\theta_2$ is exactly zero from then on. A contour meets a corner far more easily than it meets a point on a circle where one coordinate is zero.
  • Ridge never reaches zero. Its paths approach the axis and never arrive, at any $\alpha$.
  • With correlated features ridge does not shrink each coefficient on its own: $\theta_2$ first grows slightly while $\lVert\boldsymbol\theta\rVert_2$ falls.
Try this
  1. Switch to uncorrelated features. The contours become circles; ridge now scales both coefficients by the same factor, and lasso subtracts the same $\alpha$ from both.
  2. Tick the gradient-descent path. It starts at $\boldsymbol\theta = \mathbf 0$, the smallest model, and walks out to $\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$; stopping it early picks a point on the way — early stopping is a penalty by another route.
  3. Step until lasso's $\theta_2$ hits zero and read the narration: the pull $|z_2|$ has just dropped below $\alpha$.
