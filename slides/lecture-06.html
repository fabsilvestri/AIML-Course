Part one

The brief

15 minutes

You have just been hired again

You work for the agency that manages a national forest. It has to publish a map of forest cover type — which species dominates each 30 by 30 metre patch of ground — over an area far too large to survey on foot.

The task From cartographic measurements alone — elevation, slope, aspect, distances to water, road and fire ignition points, hillshade, soil type — predict which of seven tree species covers the patch.

No satellite imagery, no remote sensing. Numbers a surveyor could in principle read off a contour map.

What the output feeds

The map is not an internal artefact. It goes into the public record, and it is used to decide:

  • where logging is permitted and where it is not
  • which parcels qualify for habitat protection
  • how fire risk is modelled for the whole district

A landowner who is refused a permit is entitled to ask why. So is a court.

Ask the room what happens if the answer is "a random forest with 200 trees said so". Someone will say "so what". Let them argue about it for a minute; the next slide settles it.

The constraint, stated by the regulator

Not negotiable Every individual prediction must be accompanied by a human-readable justification: a statement, in terms of the measured quantities, of why this patch was classified as that species.

Note the word individual. Not “which variables matter in general”. Not “the model is 88% accurate”. This patch, this answer, this reason.

Turn the constraint into a specification

“Human-readable” is not a testable condition. Before writing any code, negotiate it into one. We asked, and this is what came back:

RequirementTestable form
Readable by a non-specialiststated as conditions on the measured columns, not on transformed ones
Short enough to checkat most 8 conditions per prediction
Auditablethe same patch gives the same justification, and the rule can be applied by hand

Eight is the number the agency chose. It is arbitrary — and it is now a hyperparameter, fixed by law rather than by cross-validation. That is unusual and worth noticing.

What that rules out, before we start

ModelCan it justify one prediction?
Logistic regressionpartly — a weighted sum of 54 terms is a reason, but not a short one
k-nearest neighbours“because these 5 other patches looked similar”
SVM with an RBF kernelno — the decision surface has no finite description
Neural networkno
Decision treeyes — the path from root to leaf is the justification

This is the first application in the course where the model family is chosen by the constraint rather than by the data.

It is also the answer to “why are we learning trees now?” — they enter the course at the moment nothing else will do.

Frame the problem

The same four questions as in the first application:

  1. What kind of training supervision?
  2. Classification, regression, or something else?
  3. Batch or online?
  4. What assumptions are we making?

Answer them before the next slide. They take one minute and they have saved projects.

Vocabulary Multiclass (one label out of seven), not multilabel (any subset of seven) and not multioutput (several labels at once). The distinction changes the metric, the loss and the confusion matrix.

4 · Check the assumptions

Two are worth stating out loud, because both would change what we build.

  • The seven classes are exhaustive and exclusive. A patch with two species in equal measure has no correct answer here
  • The training survey is representative of the mapped area. If surveyors walked the accessible valleys, the model learns valleys
The one that would sink us If the agency intends to publish a probability per species rather than a single label, the metric changes and so does the model. We asked. They want a label, with a reason.

Subsampling for speed is a legitimate choice and a reportable one. Subsampling and not mentioning it is not.

Part two

The metric

15 minutes · and a number in writing

Metric before model — again

Seven classes, no natural ordering, no asymmetric cost supplied by the agency. The default is accuracy: the fraction of patches whose species is called correctly.

$$\text{accuracy} \;=\; \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\hat{y}^{(i)} = y^{(i)}\right]$$

One number, easy to state to a stakeholder, and — on the evidence of the second application — the metric most likely to flatter a useless model.

Is accuracy defensible here?

For

  • All seven errors cost the agency roughly the same
  • The two largest classes are 85% of the map, so the headline number is dominated by the case that matters most
  • It is what the agency already reports

Against

  • A model can score 48.8% while never once predicting five of the seven species
  • Cottonwood/Willow is 0.5% of the data and, plausibly, the species a habitat rule protects

So: report accuracy as the headline, and always beside a per-class breakdown. We will look at both today, and the breakdown is the more interesting of the two.

Before you commit: what does nothing score?

The cheapest possible classifier. Predict the commonest species for every patch in Colorado, forever.


from sklearn.dummy import DummyClassifier

dummy = DummyClassifier(strategy="most_frequent").fit(X_train, y_train)
print(f"{dummy.score(X_test, y_test):.1%}")     # 48.8%
ModelTest accuracySpecies it can ever predict
Always “Lodgepole Pine”48.8%1 of 7

Anything below 48.8% is worse than a constant. The distance above it is the only part you actually earned.

Show of hands on the guess before revealing it. Most guess far too low, because seven classes feels like 14%.

What number would count as success?

  • The constant model scores 48.8%
  • The agency’s surveyors are stated to agree with one another on roughly nine patches in ten — an assumption we were handed, not a measurement we made
  • So the useful range is somewhere between those two, and the constraint may well stop us short of the top of it
A model that is 5 points worse and can be defended in a hearing may be worth more to this agency than one that is 5 points better and cannot. That is not a machine learning judgement, and it is not yours to make alone.

The plan

  1. Fit one decision tree, unconstrained, and read what it gives us
  2. Ask an assistant for the justification, and watch it fail
  3. Constrain the tree until the justification fits the brief
  4. Tune what is left, inside the constraint
  5. Read the tree, trace a prediction, look at the errors
  6. Measure, and commit the result

Steps 3 and 4 are in that order deliberately. The constraint is not a hyperparameter we get to optimise.

What a decision tree is

A binary tree. Each internal node asks one question about one feature; each leaf carries a prediction.

  • A question is always of the form $x_k \le t_k$ — one feature, one threshold
  • Prediction: start at the root, answer the question, go left or right, repeat until a leaf
  • The leaf predicts the majority class of the training instances that reached it
Trees are the building block of random forests, which are among the most powerful algorithms available today. They are also, on their own, one of the very few models whose reasoning a human can follow end to end.

How it trains — CART

Scikit-Learn uses the Classification And Regression Tree algorithm. At each node it searches over every feature $k$ and every threshold $t_k$ for the split minimising

$$J(k, t_k) \;=\; \frac{m_{\text{left}}}{m}\,G_{\text{left}} \;+\; \frac{m_{\text{right}}}{m}\,G_{\text{right}}$$

the impurity of the two children, weighted by their size

Then it recurses on each child, and stops when a stopping condition fires or no split reduces impurity.

The impurity being minimised

$$G_i \;=\; 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_{i,k}^{\,2}$$

Gini impurity of node $i$, over $K$ classes

  • $p_{i,k}$ — the fraction of the training instances in node $i$ that belong to class $k$
  • All of one class $\Rightarrow G_i = 0$. The node is pure
  • Seven classes in equal measure $\Rightarrow G_i = 1 - 7\cdot(1/7)^2 = 6/7$

There is a second impurity measure, criterion="entropy", and a good reason to ask whether it makes any difference. That question is the mathematical thread of the next lecture, and we are deliberately not answering it now.

CART is greedy, and that is not a detail

It picks the best split at this node, then never reconsiders it. A split that looks mediocre now but enables two excellent splits below is never found.

Why nobody does better Finding the optimal tree is NP-complete. The greedy algorithm returns a reasonably good solution in $O(n\,m\log m)$ and no known algorithm returns the best one in reasonable time.

So the tree you get is a function of the algorithm as much as of the data. Remember that; it is the seed of the next lecture’s diagnosis.

What it costs

OperationCostWhy
Predict one instance$O(\log_2 m)$traverse one root-to-leaf path of a roughly balanced tree
Train$O(n \times m \log_2 m)$at every node, sort and scan all $n$ features

Prediction does not depend on the number of features, which is why a 54-column tree is as fast as a 5-column one. Training does.

This is the property that makes the justification cheap: reading a prediction costs the same as making it.

Ask an assistant for the justification

Weak

“Train a decision tree on the covertype data and make it interpretable, so I can explain each prediction to a regulator.”

Under-specified in exactly one place, and it is not the word “interpretable”.

Usable

“… the justification is per prediction: the list of conditions that instance satisfied on its path to a leaf, at most eight of them. Print that list for one test instance so I can check the length.”

What the weak prompt returns

# interpretable land-cover classifier
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
import pandas as pd

print(importances.sort_values(ascending=False).head(5).round(3))

It runs. It imports nothing exotic. It prints a ranked list of features and a believable accuracy, under a heading that says why the model predicts what it predicts.

The review question that catches it

Reviewer question 3: what is the shape here?

One vector for the whole model feature_importances_ has 54 entries — one per column, not one per prediction. It is the same for every patch in Colorado.

The regulator asked why this parcel was refused. The answer on offer is “elevation matters a lot, in general”. That is not a justification; it is a description of the training run.

Nothing in the code is wrong. The specification did not say per prediction, so the assistant answered the easier question.

Now measure the damage — do not guess

The tree can justify a prediction: the path from root to leaf is a list of conditions. Count them.


# decision_path marks every node visited, root and leaf included,
# so the number of conditions applied is that count minus one
path = tree.decision_path(X_test)
lengths = path.sum(axis=1).A1 - 1

print(f"mean {lengths.mean():.2f}   max {lengths.max()}")
  

mean 17.88   max 33

The brief allows eight conditions. The average justification this tree produces is 17.88, and the worst is 33.

How long a justification actually is

Two models, the same 12,000 predictions. One produces a reason a person can check; the other produces a paragraph nobody will read.

The corrected specification

What to ask for instead “Fit a DecisionTreeClassifier with max_depth=8 on X_train. For a given test instance, return the list of (feature, comparison, threshold, value) tuples along its decision path, and the class distribution of the leaf it lands in. Assert that the list has at most eight entries. Do not use feature_importances_: it is one vector for the whole model.”

Three additions: the shape of the output, the check, and an explicit prohibition on the plausible wrong answer.

This is the transferable lesson: when you know which wrong answer is tempting, name it in the prompt. It costs one clause.

What that example is really teaching

  • The assistant was obedient. “Interpretable” has a common meaning in the literature, and it used it
  • The number it printed — 82.6% — was correct
  • The failure was in the shape of the output, which no accuracy check would ever catch

A specification that names only the metric will be satisfied by a model that meets the metric and fails the brief.

Why the tree grew to depth 33

Because nothing stopped it. A decision tree is a nonparametric model: the number of parameters is not fixed before training, so the structure is free to follow the data as far as the data goes.

That freedom is exactly what let it isolate almost every training patch. It is also exactly what we now have to take away — not to reduce overfitting, but to meet the brief.

A linear model cannot do this. Its parameter count is decided by the number of columns before it sees a single row.

The regularisation hyperparameters

ParameterEffect
max_depthhard ceiling on the length of every path — and therefore on the justification
min_samples_splita node with fewer instances than this is never split
min_samples_leafa split that would leave a child smaller than this is rejected
max_leaf_nodesceiling on the total number of leaves — on the number of rules
max_featureshow many features are considered at each split

Increasing the min_* parameters or decreasing the max_* ones regularises the model.

Two of these control accuracy. One of them — max_depth — is fixed for us by the regulator.

The one hyperparameter we may not tune

Read this carefully Cross-validation says max_depth=None. The brief says max_depth=8. Cross-validation does not get a vote on this one, because it is not optimising the thing the agency is buying.

This is the first time in the course that the honest answer to “what does the search say?” is “the search is answering the wrong question”.

Bring the 7.9-point figure to the regulator. Perhaps they will trade a condition or two for it. That is a conversation, not a GridSearchCV.

Tune what is left, inside the constraint


from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV

        "min_samples_leaf": [1, 5, 20, 50, 200]}

We search the whole grid anyway — including the depths we are not allowed to use — because the rows we cannot pick are exactly what tells the agency what its constraint costs.

Thirty combinations, and only one row is available to us

The red cell is the best model. The green row is the set of models we are allowed to ship.

Two things to read off that grid

  • Within the green row, min_samples_leaf barely matters — 73.8% at 1, 71.5% at 200. At depth 8 the depth limit binds first, so the leaf-size limit has almost nothing left to do
  • Down the first column, it matters enormously — 82.1% at min_samples_leaf=1 falling to 71.9% at 200. With no depth limit, the leaf size is the regularisation
Two hyperparameters that both restrict the tree do not act independently. The 2-D grid shows that; two separate 1-D sweeps would not have.

So the grid’s answer under the cap is min_samples_leaf=1. We are not going to ship it — next slide.

Why we ship min_samples_leaf=20

The model states its justification as “90% of the 481 training patches in this leaf”. At min_samples_leaf=1 that becomes “100% of the 1” — a single surveyed patch wearing the grammar of evidence. The brief requires a justification a regulator can audit; that is not one.

The threshold is 3,046.5 rather than 3,046 because CART puts the split midway between two adjacent observed values. Nothing in the data sits at 3,046.5 metres.

Trees need no feature scaling at all. Splitting on $x_k \le t_k$ is invariant under any monotone transformation of $x_k$, so standardising the columns changes the thresholds and nothing else.

Trace one prediction, all the way down


def justify(tree, x, names):
    t, node, out = tree.tree_, 0, []
    while t.children_left[node] != -1:
        f, thr = t.feature[node], t.threshold[node]
        left = x[f] <= thr
        out.append((names[f], "<=" if left else ">", thr, x[f]))
        node = t.children_left[node] if left else t.children_right[node]
    return out, node

Twelve lines, no library beyond what is already imported. The entire justification mechanism is tree_.children_left, tree_.feature and tree_.threshold.

What the regulator actually receives

The path is the justification. No extra machinery, no approximation, no second model explaining the first.

Read the leaf carefully

90% of the 481 training patches in that leaf were Krummholz.

  • That is a statement about 481 training patches, not a probability that this patch is Krummholz
  • predict_proba returns exactly those leaf proportions — so a tree’s “probabilities” are piecewise constant, and identical for every patch reaching the same leaf
  • A leaf holding 3 patches, all of one species, reports 100%
The honest sentence “Of the training patches that satisfied these eight conditions, 90% were Krummholz.” Not “this patch is 90% likely to be Krummholz.”

How many patches is a leaf built on?


leaves = tree.apply(X_train)                 # leaf id per training patch
sizes = np.bincount(leaves)
sizes = sizes[sizes > 0]

print(f"leaves {len(sizes)}   smallest {sizes.min()}   median "
      f"{int(np.median(sizes))}")

A leaf built on four patches and a leaf built on four thousand produce the same kind of sentence and deserve very different amounts of trust. The count belongs in the justification, which is why it is in ours.

Which is why the shipped tree carries min_samples_leaf=20. Every justification it produces now rests on at least twenty surveyed patches, so “100% of the 1” cannot be written — and the smallest leaf here holds 20, not one.

Why the tree cannot see Aspen

  • Aspen is 1.6% of the training set. A split that isolates it improves the weighted Gini by very little, so CART never chooses one
  • With max_depth=8 there are at most 256 leaves to allocate across seven species. The greedy algorithm spends them where the instances are
  • Both effects point the same way: the impurity criterion is a weighted average, and a rare class carries almost no weight
This is the second application’s lesson arriving in a new costume. Accuracy under imbalance rewards ignoring the rare class, and here the model obliges on two species out of seven.

Fixes exist — class_weight="balanced" reweights the impurity. Try it in the notebook and see what it does to the headline number.

What goes in the report

One sentence the agency needs “This map is 73.3% accurate overall, but it identifies fewer than one Aspen stand in ten. Do not use it to decide anything about Aspen or Douglas-fir.”

A model can be fit for one purpose and unfit for another, in the same deployment. Saying which is which is the job.

This is the fairness slice from the first application, applied to classes rather than to districts. Same idea, same obligation.

The same algorithm, for regression

You already used one, in the first application, and it scored zero on its training data. DecisionTreeRegressor is CART with the impurity replaced by the mean squared error of the node:

$$J(k, t_k) \;=\; \frac{m_{\text{left}}}{m}\,\text{MSE}_{\text{left}} \;+\; \frac{m_{\text{right}}}{m}\,\text{MSE}_{\text{right}}$$

A leaf predicts the mean of the training targets that reached it, so a regression tree’s output is a step function — piecewise constant, with one step per leaf.

That is why an unconstrained regression tree can drive its training error to exactly zero: enough leaves and every training row gets its own step.

One weakness worth knowing about

Every split is of the form $x_k \le t_k$, so every decision boundary is perpendicular to an axis.

  • A boundary that genuinely runs at 45° has to be approximated by a staircase of axis-aligned steps
  • Rotate the data and the tree changes completely, even though the problem did not
  • Géron’s remedy: apply PCA first, which tends to orient the axes better — that is Chapter 8, and Application 5

Sensitivity to a transformation that does not change the problem is a warning sign. Keep it in mind for the next lecture, where a related sensitivity turns out to be the whole diagnosis.

What we deliberately did not do

  • Scale anything. Trees are invariant under monotone transformations of individual features
  • Encode anything. Wilderness area and soil type arrived one-hot; a tree splits a 0/1 column at 0.5 and reads as “is it soil type 26?”
  • Touch the test set more than once. Every choice was made on cross-validated folds of the training set

Three of the four steps of the first application’s pipeline turned out to be unnecessary for this model. Knowing which preprocessing a model needs is worth more than applying all of it by reflex.

What we learned

  1. A stakeholder can constrain the shape of the answer, not only its quality — and that constraint selects the model family before the data does
  2. A decision tree predicts by traversing a path of $x_k \le t_k$ tests; the path is the justification, and it costs nothing extra
  3. CART is greedy, minimises weighted Gini impurity, and is used because the optimal tree is NP-complete
  4. feature_importances_ is one vector for the whole model. It cannot justify an individual prediction, and asking for “interpretability” will get you it anyway
  5. max_depth bought 7.9 points of accuracy between 8 and 18 — measure the price of a constraint and report it
  6. 73.3% overall, and 3.1% recall on Aspen. Never ship the first number without the second
