Where we are

Lecture 11 took a deep fully connected stack on flattened 32×32 colour images (CIFAR-10) that would not train, and repaired it:

  • Two probes located the fault: the forward signal looked healthy, the backward one shrank by a constant factor per layer
  • One variance recursion explained it, and Glorot’s and He’s initialisations are the two ways of setting that factor to one
  • Six repairs — initialisation, activation, batch normalisation, clipping, optimiser, schedule — added one at a time, and an ablation saying which of them paid
What breaks when the image gets bigger Flattening a 224×224 colour image gives 150,528 inputs. A single dense layer of 1,000 units on that is 150 million parameters — for one layer, on one image size. Every one of them has to be learned from data, and none of them knows that two adjacent pixels are related.

Today’s method fixes both problems at once, and the derivation says exactly how much it saves.

The stakeholder

A horticultural wholesaler receives mixed pallets of cut stems and has to sort them by species before they reach the auction floor.

  • One overhead camera per lane, one photograph per stem
  • A human inspector currently does this and is the bottleneck
  • They want the machine to name the species from the photograph

Same shape of problem as the garment sorter in Lecture 7. Everything that makes it harder is in the pixels.

What they actually asked for

In their words “Tell us which of our species it is. If you cannot tell, say so and we will look at it ourselves — but do not tell us the wrong one.”

Two requirements hiding in one sentence: a classifier over a fixed list of species, and a usable notion of confidence.

We build the first today. The second waits until the numbers are good enough to be worth calibrating.

Failure condition — a classifier with no way to abstain The stakeholder asked to be told when the model cannot tell. A softmax always returns a class, and its largest probability is not a confidence you have calibrated. Deliver it as one and you have answered a different requirement while appearing to meet this one.

What the camera sees

Different scales, different backgrounds, different lighting — and several of these species are hard to tell apart by eye.

What is hard here, precisely

  • Position — the flower is not centred, and not at a fixed scale
  • Background — leaves, soil, other flowers, a gardener’s hand
  • Between-class similarity — several of the 102 species are separated by petal count and little else
  • Within-class variation — the same species at two stages of opening

Only the first of those has a structural answer. It is the answer this chapter is about.

What weight sharing saves

Input $3 \times H \times W$, output $32 \times H \times W$. A dense layer connects every output to every input: $n_{\text{in}} n_{\text{out}} = 96\,H^2W^2$ weights. A convolution connects each output to a $k \times k$ window of every channel, with the same weights at every position: $C_{\text{out}} C_{\text{in}} k^2 = 32 \times 3 \times 7 \times 7$, which is 4,704 weights.

$$\frac{n_{\text{in}} n_{\text{out}}} {C_{\text{out}} C_{\text{in}} k^2} \;=\; \frac{H^2 W^2}{k^2}$$

5,478,275

times fewer weights at $H = W = 128$, for the same output shape.

With the 3 × 3 filters used everywhere else in the network the factor is larger again, by $(7/3)^2$: the dense count never contained $k$.

The same output shape, two ways

A logarithmic axis, and the two bars still barely fit on the same figure.

Equivariance, precisely

Sharing the weights assumes that a pattern means the same thing wherever it appears. Let $T_{\mathbf{v}}$ be the operator that shifts an image by the vector $\mathbf{v}$:

$$(T_{\mathbf{v}} x)(\mathbf{p}) \;=\; x(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{v})$$

A map $f$ is equivariant to translation when

$$f(T_{\mathbf{v}}\, x) \;=\; T_{\mathbf{v}}\, f(x) \qquad \text{for every } \mathbf{v}$$

Shift then compute, or compute then shift — the same answer. A convolution has this property because its weights do not depend on the position; it holds exactly away from the border, where zero padding invents input that was not there.

How pooling manufactures invariance

A map $g$ is invariant to translation when

$$g(T_{\mathbf{v}}\, x) \;=\; g(x) \qquad \text{for every } \mathbf{v}$$

The output does not move at all, so it no longer says where. Classification wants that; equivariance alone does not give it.

A $2 \times 2$ max pool is invariant to any shift that keeps the maximum inside its own window — so half the one-pixel shifts, in each direction.

  • Stack four pooling stages and the tolerated shift grows to $\pm$ 8 pixels in the last feature map
  • A global pool over the whole map is invariant to every shift — there is no position left to be sensitive to

The invariance is approximate and it is built, one factor of two at a time. Nothing in the architecture declares it.

The idea, in one sentence

A feature worth detecting in the top left corner is worth detecting in the bottom right, so use the same weights everywhere.

  • That is the whole of it. Everything else is bookkeeping
  • It buys two things at once: far fewer parameters, and a network that does not have to relearn a petal edge at every position

Both halves were stated in the mathematics block. Here we use them.

A convolution, as arithmetic

For an input $x$ with $C$ channels and a kernel $w$ of size $k \times k$, the output at position $(i, j)$ of filter $f$ is

$$z^{(f)}_{ij} \;=\; b^{(f)} \;+\; \sum_{c=1}^{C}\;\sum_{u=0}^{k-1}\;\sum_{v=0}^{k-1} w^{(f)}_{c,u,v}\, x_{c,\,i+u,\,j+v}$$

A dot product between the kernel and a window of the input, repeated at every position. The sum over $c$ is why a filter reads all the input channels at once.

nn.Conv2d, and the defaults you did not ask for

nn.Conv2d(
    in_channels=3,       # must match the previous layer's output
    out_channels=32,     # how many filters -> how many output maps
    kernel_size=7,
    stride=1,            # the default
    padding=3,           # NOT the default; the default is 0
    bias=False,          # a batch-norm follows, and it has its own shift
)

The default you did not ask for: padding defaults to zero, which silently shrinks every feature map by $k-1$ and will eventually give you a shape error twelve layers later.

Pooling

After a couple of convolutions, halve the height and width by keeping only the largest value in each $2 \times 2$ block.

  • Three quarters of the activations disappear, and with them three quarters of the memory and the arithmetic in every later layer
  • It has no parameters — nothing about it is learned
  • Each later unit therefore sees a larger piece of the original image

There is a fourth consequence — invariance — and it is the one the mathematics block ended on.

Max pooling, on numbers

Sixteen numbers in, four out, and the four are the largest of each block.

Why every convolution is followed by a batch norm

Because ten layers of untreated convolutions on 1,020 images do not train. This is Lecture 9's $\rho \ne 1$ in a new architecture — and here, unlike there, initialisation alone does not reach it. Lecture 9 measured batch normalisation making a repaired network worse, by 4.74 points over five seeds; the size of an effect depends on what else is in the system.

  • Ten layers of untreated convolutions on 1,020 images do not train: the loss sits at $\log 102 \approx 4.62$ and the network predicts one class
  • BatchNorm2d normalises across the batch and both spatial axes, so it holds one mean and one variance per channel

nn.Conv2d(c_in, c_out, 3, padding=1, bias=False)
nn.BatchNorm2d(c_out)     # 2 * c_out parameters, not 2 * c_out * H * W
nn.ReLU()

Channels double as the map halves — the textbook’s own rule of thumb: what pooling takes away in resolution, the extra filters give back in channels.

The architecture, in code

def conv_block(c_in, c_out, k=3):
    return [nn.Conv2d(c_in, c_out, k, padding=k // 2, bias=False),
            nn.BatchNorm2d(c_out), nn.ReLU()]

net = nn.Sequential(
    *conv_block(3, 32, k=7), *conv_block(32, 32), nn.MaxPool2d(2),
    *conv_block(32, 64),     *conv_block(64, 64), nn.MaxPool2d(2),
    *conv_block(64, 128),  *conv_block(128, 128), nn.MaxPool2d(2),
    *conv_block(128, 256),                        nn.MaxPool2d(2),
    nn.Flatten(),
    nn.Linear(256 * 8 * 8, 256), nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Dropout(0.5),
    nn.Linear(256, 102),
)

Thirty modules. Nothing in it that was not on a slide in this lecture or the previous one.

The weights moved. No structure appeared — the right-hand block is still noise, and thirty epochs of training bought it.

Two reasons, and both are worth knowing

  1. The gradient that reaches it is small. Ten layers of chain rule separate the loss from these weights, and 1,020 images give 960 optimiser steps in total
  2. Its scale does not matter. A batch norm follows immediately, and normalising the output makes the overall size of these weights irrelevant to the function — so there is no gradient pressure on it at all

The textbook picture of a first layer — colour blobs, oriented edges — is real, and you will see it next lecture. It comes from a corpus a thousand times this size.

What a filter does to one photograph

Early maps still look like the photograph. Four layers down they do not, and that is the point.

Reading a feature map

  • Bright means this filter fired strongly here — nothing more mystical than that
  • The spatial layout survives: a bright patch is where in the image the pattern was found
  • After four pooling stages the map is 8 × 8, so one cell answers for a 256-pixel square of the original

That last line is a loss of information we chose. It is the right choice for naming a species, and the wrong one for any task that must say where.

Design exercise · in the manner of an ML system-design interview

The task: a recycling-robotics company’s camera sorter

The brief A recycling-robotics company sells a sorting unit for its customers’ conveyors: a camera photographs every object, an air jet blows it into the bin for its material. It names the material of each object — paper, cardboard, two plastics, glass, aluminium, residual — in time for the jet. Users: the plant operators. The business warrants the purity of the bales its customers sell, and pays for returns.
  • Given: a few thousand photographs from installed units, one object each, labelled by the customers’ operators
  • Scale: several objects a second per belt; the jet fires a fraction of a second later
  • Consumers of the output: the unit’s diverter, the customer’s purity report per bale, the support team

Nine steps follow, each with the pitfall that catches a first attempt and what to do about it. The model at the centre is today’s: a convolutional network trained from scratch.

Step 1 — Frame it: one class per object, scored per material

  • One row per object; the label is its material; the output is one class from a fixed list. The customers’ “if you cannot tell, send it to residual” is a confidence requirement a softmax does not meet — say so in the brief rather than hand over the largest probability as if it were one
  • The metric is per material: the recall of each, and the purity of each bin, read off a confusion matrix; the baseline is “everything is cardboard”
Pitfall — class imbalance between materials Cardboard and paper are most of a belt; aluminium is perhaps one object in fifty. Accuracy rewards a network that never names aluminium, and the aluminium bale — the one worth most per tonne — is the one the customer’s buyer rejects: a warranty claim. Score every material separately, weight the loss by the inverse frequency or oversample the rare ones, and report bin purity, never one accuracy.

Step 2 — The data: one crop per object, and what a pixel costs

  • The camera frame is mostly belt: crop each object out of it, so the network reads the object and not the conveyor
  • The weight count of a convolution does not contain the image size, but the activations and the arithmetic do: every feature map is as large as its input, and the dense head grows with the last map
  • Choose the side length by looking: the smallest at which an operator can still tell the two plastics apart on the crop — then measure
Pitfall — resolution chosen by habit Fed at the camera’s full resolution, the early maps are hundreds of times larger than at today’s crop size, and one object takes longer than the belt allows. Shrunk to a thumbnail, the label on the bottle and the texture that separates the plastics are gone, the two merge in the confusion matrix, and the plastic bale downgrades. Pick the resolution with the confusion matrix and the clock both open.

Step 3 — Split by plant, then augment the training side only

  • Consecutive frames show the same object twice, and a plant’s belt is one distribution: split by plant, never by frame, so the validation plants are ones the network never saw; standardise with the training side’s channel statistics only
  • Manufacture views of the training crops: an object lies on the belt at any angle, so rotations and flips keep the label; a shift of position the convolution already handles by equivariance
Pitfall — augmentation that does not respect the object A hue shift turns green glass into brown, and the label did not survive. A crop that drops the bottle’s neck or the can’s rim removes the feature the operators used. Each transform is a claim that the material is unchanged under it: state the claim, apply the transform to the training loader only, and leave the validation and test crops as the camera took them.

Step 4 — Representation: the crop as a tensor, read by shared weights

  • The input is channels × height × width, not a flattened vector: a dense layer producing a map from a flattened crop needs a weight count that contains the square of the image area
  • A convolution reads a window with the same weights at every position: a crushed can’s rim is the same evidence wherever it lies on the belt, which is exactly the assumption weight sharing makes — and here it holds
  • Pooling then builds invariance: the diverter needs the material, not where on the crop the rim was
Pitfall — the first attempt flattens A multilayer perceptron on flattened crops has to learn the same edge at every position from a few thousand examples, and its first layer does not fit in memory at any resolution that shows the material. Weight sharing is the reason a network fits this belt at all: the same filter at every position, far fewer weights, and the rim learned once.

Step 5 — The model: convolution, batch norm, pooling — and the shapes first

  • Blocks of convolution, batch norm and ReLU; a pool after every pair; channels doubling as the map halves; a small dense head with dropout over the materials
  • Batch norm after every convolution, because a stack of untreated convolutions on a few thousand crops sits at the loss of a uniform guess and names one material
  • Push a dummy batch through the modules one at a time and print every shape before the first training step
Pitfall — padding left at its default The default is zero: every map shrinks by the kernel size less one, and the error surfaces as a size mismatch at the dense layer, many layers after the cause, in the middle of a training loop. Set the padding to half the kernel so each map keeps its size, and assert the head’s output shape before anything is trained.

Step 6 — Failure condition: a few thousand crops against millions of parameters

  • Nine parameters in ten sit in the dense layer after the flatten, and it can memorise a few thousand crops
  • Symptom: the training curve climbs towards the purity the company warrants and the validation curve parts from it and flattens
  • Two seeds of the same run differ by points: a comparison smaller than the seed spread is not a comparison
Pitfall — overfitting a small set, read as success The first attempt puts the training accuracy in the release note, and the first customer finds otherwise. Shrink the head — a global pool in place of the flatten — keep the dropout, augment, label more crops from the plants the model gets wrong, and read every number on held-out plants, with the seed spread beside it.

Step 7 — Evaluation: the curve against the clock, the test plants once

  • Plot training and validation accuracy against wall-clock seconds, not epochs: the comparison for every new customer’s belt is “how good, in how long”
  • The learning rate is not a free choice: at the optimiser’s default the network plateaus at chance and nothing raises; choose it on the validation plants
  • Choose everything on the validation plants; read the test plants once
Pitfall — curves compared by epoch A wider network, or one fed a larger crop, looks better at the same epoch and costs several times the seconds per epoch. The budget is commissioning time, so the x-axis must be time: against the clock the small network has often reached the same point long before the large one, and the comparison reverses.

Step 8 — On the line: latency, release, and the operators’ audit

  • The crop, the preprocessing and one forward pass must fit inside the fraction of a second before the jet, on the unit’s controller — one object at a time, no batch
  • Early pooling removes three quarters of every later layer’s arithmetic; the convolutions are where the time goes
  • Release: new model on one lane, old on the other, bin purity compared on the customer’s audit; retrain on the audits as packaging changes; the engineer on call reads each bin’s purity against the launch matrix
Pitfall — latency measured on the development machine, in batches Throughput on a graphics card at a batch of sixty-four says nothing about one crop on the unit’s controller: a model that clears the batch benchmark misses the jet. Time a single crop end to end on the unit’s own hardware, and put that number in every comparison and the specification.

Solutions — Lecture 11

1. A linear autoencoder $784 \to d \to 784$ with squared loss is trained to convergence on centred data. State…

$\sum_{j>d}\sigma_j^2$, the tail of the spectrum; it reconstructs through the projection onto the span of the leading $d$ right singular vectors — the principal subspace.

  • Every reconstruction is a combination of the $d$ rows of the decoder, so it lies in one subspace of dimension at most $d$, and the closest point of a subspace is the orthogonal projection
  • Over all $d$-dimensional subspaces the projection error is smallest on the principal subspace, where it equals the tail sum; the choice $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{V}_d$, $\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{V}_d^{\intercal}$ attains it

2. Two linear autoencoders are trained from different seeds to the same loss, and their codes for the same image…

Not a bug. For any invertible $\mathbf{T}$, the encoder $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}$ with the decoder $\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{B}$ gives the same reconstruction and a different code: the subspace is unique, its basis is not.

  • The loss depends on the product $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}$ only, and the product does not change under the change of basis
  • Gradient descent lands on one basis of the principal subspace; the principal components themselves are one basis among all of them

Solutions — Lecture 11 (2)

3. The code size is set equal to the number of pixels and nothing else constrains the network. State what the…

The identity, with zero loss. The code is a re-encoding of the pixels that loses nothing and learns nothing.

  • With $d = n$ the tail of the spectrum is empty, so the floor is zero and the identity reaches it
  • A bottleneck narrower than the input is the only thing that forbids copying; without it the code carries exactly the pixels, differently arranged

4. An anomaly detector scores images by their reconstruction error from an autoencoder trained on nine classes;…

Reconstruction error measures how well the training classes can imitate an image, not how rare it is. A held-out class the training classes resemble is reconstructed as well as they are, so its errors sit on top of theirs. Examples of the anomalies, to measure the detector on.

  • A coat held out from pullovers, shirts and T-shirts decodes as a dark pullover; nothing in the score knows it was never seen
  • Whether the method works is a property of what the anomalies look like, which cannot be read from the training data alone

5. Nearest-neighbour retrieval in a 32-number autoencoder code scores the same precision at ten as in the 784…

A twenty-fourfold saving in storage and in the cost of every comparison, at equal precision. It could not beat the pixels because the reconstruction loss contains no information about which images are similar: the code is a compressed account of pixel distance.

  • A nearest-neighbour search compares the query with every item in the catalogue, so the length of the code is the cost of the search
  • A representation that is better than its input at a task needs a training signal about that task, and reconstructing the input supplies none
