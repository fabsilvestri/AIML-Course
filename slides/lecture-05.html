The brief

The stakeholder

Marine safety planning unit “We are building an evacuation-assistance model from historical passenger manifests. During an evacuation we have 80 crew who can be assigned to escort individual passengers. We need to know, for each passenger profile, how likely that person is to get off unaided — and we need to be able to explain the assignment afterwards.”

Historical data, modern decision. The data are from 1912; the question is not.

Read the brief as four requirements

#What they asked forWhat that forces
1“how likely”a probability, not a label
2the probability is used to allocateit must be calibrated

3ba fixed number of crewa capacity, which the costs cannot see
4“explain the assignment”weights a human can read

Requirement 1 alone rules out most of the models in this book.

3a and 3b were one row until somebody checked. They are two constraints, they disagree, and section 12b is what happens when only one of them reaches the code.

A label is not a probability

What a label says

“This passenger survives.”

Two passengers with the same label are indistinguishable. There is nothing to rank, nothing to allocate against, and nothing to be more or less sure about.

What a probability says

“This passenger survives with probability 0.31.”

Now the sentence is checkable: take everyone the model puts near 0.31 and count how many survived. If it is not about a third, the model is lying.

Requirement 2 is what makes requirement 1 more than a formatting choice.

So: logistic regression

  • It estimates $P(y=1 \mid \mathbf{x})$ directly, by maximising the likelihood of the labels — not as an afterthought
  • Its loss is a proper scoring rule: the minimiser is the true probability, so calibration is what it is trying to do
  • Its parameters are one number per feature, on a scale that has a name
Géron, Chapter 4. Same chapter as the training algorithms, and for a reason: this is the first model in the course whose training we look at rather than only its output.

Part two

Choose a metric,
and commit

15 minutes

Why accuracy cannot be the headline here

  • It reads a probability of 0.51 and a probability of 0.99 as the same answer
  • It cannot be computed at all until you have chosen a cut-off — so quoting it hides the decision the brief asked you to make explicit
  • It weighs the two mistakes equally, and the stakeholder does not

Accuracy will still be reported. It will not be optimised, and it is not the number you commit to.

What “calibrated” means, precisely

$$P\!\left(y = 1 \;\middle|\; \hat p(\mathbf{x}) = q\right) \;=\; q \qquad \text{for every } q \in (0,1)$$

Take every passenger the model scored near $q$. The fraction who actually survived should be about $q$.

This is a property of a set of predictions, not of any one prediction. You cannot check it on a single passenger, which is exactly why it is so easy to ship a model that fails it.

The metric: log loss

$$\mathcal{L} \;=\; -\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \Big[\, y^{(i)} \log \hat p^{(i)} \;+\; \bigl(1-y^{(i)}\bigr)\log\bigl(1-\hat p^{(i)}\bigr) \Big]$$
  • Only the probability you gave the true class enters each term
  • It is unbounded above: claim 0.001 for a passenger who survived and the term is $-\log 0.001 \approx 6.9$
  • Lower is better; a perfect, confident model scores 0

Natural logarithm throughout, so the units are nats. This is the quantity logistic regression minimises, which makes it the honest thing to report.

Why this metric and not something friendlier

A scoring rule is proper if the expected score is optimised by reporting your true belief.

Log loss — proper. Reporting anything other than $p^\ast$ is expected to score worse.

Brier score — $\frac{1}{m}\sum (\hat p - y)^2$. Also proper, bounded, and easier to read. We report it too.

Accuracy is not proper Under accuracy, a model that reports 0.51 scores exactly the same as one that reports 0.99 — so accuracy gives you no reason at all to tell the truth about your uncertainty.

One remark about the Brier score, for later

$$\text{Brier} \;=\; \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(\hat p^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\bigr)^2$$

That is mean squared error, with a target that happens to be 0 or 1.

Everything you know about squared error therefore applies to it unchanged — including a decomposition we have not met yet. Keep the observation; it is the whole of the next lecture's first twenty minutes.

The cut-off is a separate decision

The model produces $\hat p$. The rule is

$$\text{assign an escort} \iff \hat p(\mathbf{x}) < t$$

and $t$ is not part of the model. It comes from the costs.

MistakeWhat happensCost
Not escorted, did not survivethe one the brief exists to prevent10
Escorted, would have survived anywaya crew member spent where they were not needed1

Escort-units, stated by the stakeholder, and the ratio is the only part that matters.

Your anchor

The log loss of a model that has learned nothing except the base rate. It is the entropy of the label, in nats.

The model

Take the linear model from Thread 1 and pass it through one function.

$$\hat p \;=\; h_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \sigma\!\left(\boldsymbol\theta^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}\right), \qquad \sigma(t) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + e^{-t}}$$

$\boldsymbol\theta^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}$ is exactly the quantity least squares fitted in the previous application. Everything new today is in $\sigma$ and in what we ask $\boldsymbol\theta$ to minimise.

One function, and the whole difference

Monotone, so the ranking is the linear model's ranking. Saturating, so beyond about $|t| = 5$ more evidence changes almost nothing.

What $\boldsymbol\theta^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}$ is

Invert $\sigma$:

$$t \;=\; \log\frac{\hat p}{1 - \hat p} \;=\; \boldsymbol\theta^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}$$

The linear model does not predict the probability. It predicts the log-odds, and the odds are what the coefficients act on.

This is the sentence that makes requirement 4 answerable, and it is the sentence students most often get wrong in the examination.

Three scales, one number

Add 1 to $t$ and the odds are multiplied by $e \approx 2.72$, wherever you started. The probability has no such rule.

What it minimises

$$J(\boldsymbol\theta) \;=\; -\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \Big[\, y^{(i)} \log \hat p^{(i)} + \bigl(1-y^{(i)}\bigr)\log\bigl(1-\hat p^{(i)}\bigr)\Big]$$

The metric and the training objective are the same function. That is unusual and worth noticing: in the first application we minimised squared error and reported its root.

$J$ is convex in $\boldsymbol\theta$, so gradient descent reaches the global minimum — but there is no closed form. Unlike least squares, no normal equation exists here.

The gradient, and why it looks familiar

$$\frac{\partial J}{\partial \theta_j} \;=\; \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \left(\sigma\!\left(\boldsymbol\theta^{\intercal}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\right) - y^{(i)}\right) x_j^{(i)}$$

Prediction minus target, times the feature. Identical in form to the gradient of the mean squared error for a linear model.

That coincidence is not one. It comes back as a general fact about these losses in Thread 11, and you may take it on trust until then.

The review question that catches it

Reviewer question 5: what is the default I did not ask for?

.predict() is .predict_proba() >= 0.5 Nothing in the prompt mentioned 0.5. Nothing in the code mentions 0.5. The cut-off — the one thing the brief asked to be explicit and defensible — was chosen by a library default and never written down.

And 0.5 is the cost-optimal cut-off only when the two mistakes cost the same. The stakeholder said they cost 10 and 1.

So measure the damage — do not guess

Twenty splits. On each: fit, choose the cut-off on out-of-fold training predictions, then apply both cut-offs to the held-out passengers and add up the cost.

The default cut-off costs 110 escort-units more per evacuation, on average — and it is worse on 20 of the 20 splits.

The more accurate model is the more expensive one

Accuracy goes down when you do the right thing. If accuracy is your headline you will reject the better model.

Why accuracy falls, and why that is correct

Raising the cut-off flags more passengers as needing help. Some of them would have survived unaided, so those are now counted as errors.

  • Accuracy counts every error once
  • The stakeholder counts one kind of error ten times
  • The two rank the cut-offs differently, and only one of them is the brief
The corrected specification State the loss, state the cost ratio, state where the cut-off is chosen, and require the confusion matrix at that cut-off. Then “accurate” cannot be filled in for you.

What that example is teaching

  • The assistant was obedient. The word “accurate” was the bug
  • The defect is not in any line of the code — it is in a default that no line mentions
  • Nothing crashed, and the number it printed was true

A true number answering the wrong question is the hardest kind of error to see, because there is nothing to debug.

Six columns of the design matrix are linear combinations of the others.

Where the six come from

Five from the encoder. Each one-hot block sums to 1 in every row — and the intercept column is also 1 in every row. Five categorical columns, five dependencies.

One from us.

FamilySize = SibSp + Parch + 1, exactly, for all 891 passengers. We wrote that line ourselves an hour ago.


resid = (X_train["SibSp"] + X_train["Parch"] + 1
         - X_train["FamilySize"]).abs().max()

Standardising does not help: an affine map of a linear dependence is still a linear dependence.

You proved this two threads ago

$$\mathbf{X}^{\intercal}\mathbf{X}\ \text{is invertible} \iff \mathbf{X}\ \text{has full column rank}$$

Logistic regression has no normal equation, but the same rank condition governs it: if $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ for some $\mathbf{v} \neq \mathbf{0}$, then $\boldsymbol\theta$ and $\boldsymbol\theta + c\,\mathbf{v}$ produce identical logits for every passenger, for every $c$.

The likelihood is flat along $\mathbf{v}$. There is not one best $\boldsymbol\theta$ — there is a whole affine subspace of them, and the solver returned one point of it without mentioning the fact.

Prove it, on our own matrix

Take the fitted weights. Add 2.5 to Sex_female and to Sex_male, subtract 2.5 from the intercept. Nothing else.

Now the coefficients can be read

Each bar is a log-odds. The label beside it is what the odds get multiplied by.

Reading one of them out loud

Pclass_3 = −1.169 “Holding every other recorded feature fixed, travelling third class multiplies the odds of survival by $e^{-1.169} = 0.31$, relative to first class.”
  • “Holding every other feature fixed” is doing real work — drop it and the sentence is false
  • Odds, not probability. Going from 0.5 to $0.31 \times$ the odds gives 0.24, but from 0.9 it gives 0.73
  • Relative to the reference level, always

An honest caveat on that table

Sex and Title carry the same information twice. Title_Mr is nearly a synonym for Sex_male.

  • The design is now full rank, so the coefficients are unique
  • They are not stable: the columns are strongly correlated, so how the effect is split between them moves with the sample
  • Uniqueness and stability are different properties, and we only fixed the first

Bring that sentence to the next lecture. It is one of the two things the repair there is for.

The decision boundary, on two features

Every contour is a straight line, because the log-odds are linear in the features. The sigmoid bends the colours, never the lines.

What a straight boundary cannot express

“Children and the elderly were helped first” is not a monotone statement about age. A linear term in Age cannot say it.

The obvious fix, and it is the book's Add $\text{Age}^2$. Then the log-odds are quadratic in age and the boundary in the original coordinates is a conic — still a linear model, in transformed features.

cross_val_predict, not predict: every probability here was produced by a fit that had never seen that passenger. A reliability diagram drawn on training predictions always looks excellent.

The reliability diagram

Close to the diagonal in the bins that hold most of the passengers — the two ends, where the model is confident.

How to read that diagram without fooling yourself

  • Look at the marker area first. A bin with six passengers has an observed rate that swings by a sixth on one person
  • Deviations in the middle bins are mostly sampling noise; deviations in the crowded end bins are not
  • The held-out curve is the one that counts. The training curve is closer to the diagonal by construction
Why it is roughly calibrated at all Because log loss is a proper scoring rule and we minimised it directly. The calibration is not a happy accident — it is what the objective was asking for.

Requirement 3: choose the cut-off


COST_FN, COST_FP = 10.0, 1.0          # stated by the stakeholder

died = (y_train.values == 0)
for t in np.linspace(0.02, 0.98, 97):
    flag = p_oof < t                  # assign an escort
    cost = (COST_FN * (died & ~flag).sum()
            + COST_FP * (~died & flag).sum())

Swept over p_oof, the out-of-fold probabilities. Choosing a cut-off on predictions the model has already seen is the same mistake as scoring a tree on its own training rows, one level up.

The cut-off, and what it buys

The cost curve has a clear minimum, and it is nowhere near 0.5.

Defending the choice, in one paragraph

For the report “We assign an escort whenever the estimated probability of unaided survival falls below 0.83. That value minimises expected cost under the stated 10 : 1 ratio, and was selected on out-of-fold predictions, never on the held-out set. At that cut-off we flag 605 of the 712 training passengers and miss 4 of those who did not survive.”

Every clause in that paragraph is a decision someone can disagree with, which is what makes it a defence rather than a claim.

The requirement was there the whole time

  • It was in the stakeholder’s first sentence: 80 crew
  • It got its own row in the requirements table
  • Not one line of code has ever referred to it

Every cell since has optimised against the cost ratio. The costs say what a mistake is worth; the capacity says how many escorts there are to spend. No price makes a person appear.

Nothing errored. The cost curve is correct. The cut-off really is optimal — for a question nobody asked.

This is the most ordinary failure in applied work, and it does not look like a failure. It looks like a finished analysis.

Three things change once capacity binds

  • The rule becomes a ranking. Not is this passenger below 0.83 but are they in the worst 80. The cut-off lands at 0.062 and nobody chose it — it is whatever the 80th smallest probability happened to be, and it moves with the passenger mix while the model sits still. Lecture 8 is about exactly that.
  • Calibration stops mattering; ordering starts. Under a hard cap, any strictly increasing relabelling of the probabilities gives the same 80 people and the same cost. Calibration is what you need to choose a cut-off from costs; ranking is what you need to spend a fixed budget.
  • Requirement 1 and requirement 3b want different things from the same model — and the brief asked for both.

What the report should have said

For the report, second attempt “With 80 crew we escort the 80 passengers ranked least likely to survive unaided. That reaches 72 of the 439 at-risk passengers — 16.4% — at a cost of 3,678. Eight escorts go to passengers who would have survived anyway. Lifting the cap is worth up to 3,468 in avoided cost, which is a budget question and we have priced it.”

A cost curve with the infeasible region shaded beats a cost curve with a circle on its minimum, because it tells the stakeholder what to go and change.

What if the stakeholder’s ratio is wrong?

It probably is — 10 : 1 was an estimate. So report the cut-off as a function of the ratio rather than as a single number, and let them argue with the ratio instead of with you.

  • The cost-minimising cut-off for a calibrated model is $t^\ast = c_{\text{FN}} / (c_{\text{FN}} + c_{\text{FP}})$
  • At 10 : 1 that is $0.909$; at 1 : 1 it is $0.5$
  • Our measured optimum sits below the formula’s value, because the model is not perfectly calibrated and the sweep is on finite data

That gap between the theoretical cut-off and the measured one is the calibration error, expressed in a unit the stakeholder cares about.

Part three, continued

Now push it

20 minutes

Why push at all?

Because the boundary is straight and the world is not, and because 0.468 is not obviously good.

  • A linear model in the log-odds cannot represent “very young or very old”
  • It cannot represent “third class and travelling alone” as anything other than the sum of two effects
  • Both are expressible as polynomial features, and they leave the model linear in its parameters

This is the same device as Chapter 4's polynomial regression, applied to a classifier.

Polynomial features


num = make_pipeline(SimpleImputer(strategy="median"),
                    StandardScaler(),

Applied to the four numeric columns only. Squaring a one-hot indicator returns the indicator, so expanding the categorical block would add columns that are exact copies — and we have just spent twenty minutes removing exact copies.

degree=2 on Age, Fare, SibSp, Parch gives every square and every pairwise product: $\text{Age}^2$, $\text{Age}\cdot\text{Fare}$, and so on.

How fast the column count grows

At degree 6 the model has 227 weights to determine from 712 passengers — about three rows per weight.

Degrees 1 and 2 differ by 0.0010 in held-out log loss, and the fold-to-fold spread is two hundred times that. Those two are a tie, and reporting degree 2 as the winner would be reporting noise.

Log loss saw it first

From degree 2 to degree 5, held-out accuracy falls by about 7 points. Held-out log loss is multiplied by more than four.

  • Accuracy only notices when a prediction crosses the cut-off
  • Log loss notices a confident prediction becoming a confidently wrong one
  • The failure is not that the model gets more passengers wrong — it is that it becomes certain about the ones it gets wrong

Requirement 1 was for probabilities. The metric that matches the requirement is the metric that saw the damage.

The reflex is to raise max_iter. It will not help, and understanding why is the point.

Why more iterations cannot help

In a space with 87 columns and 712 rows, the two classes eventually become linearly separable. Suppose some $\boldsymbol\theta$ separates them perfectly. Then for every passenger

$$c \to \infty \;\Longrightarrow\; J(c\,\boldsymbol\theta) \to 0$$

so the likelihood has no maximum: scaling the weights up always improves it. There is no optimum for lbfgs to find.

The warning is not a numerical nuisance. It is the solver telling you the estimate you asked for does not exist.

Summary

  • A brief that says “how likely” forces a probability, and a probability forces a proper scoring rule
  • Logistic regression predicts the log-odds linearly; coefficients multiply the odds
  • A rank-deficient design gives coefficients that are not unique — Thread 1, on your own matrix
  • The cut-off is a decision made from costs, on out-of-fold predictions, and written down
  • Accuracy and log loss disagree, and the brief says which one wins
  • Capacity improves the training curve monotonically and the honest curve for exactly one step
