The task: the next day’s riders

You are a data scientist at the transit authority of a large city.

The task Forecast how many people will board the rail network the next day, from daily boarding totals going back twenty years. One number, one day ahead. It decides how many trains and crews are scheduled.

Too high

Empty trains run: fuel and crew hours, measurable and survivable.

Too low

Passengers are left on platforms at peak, a cost that appears in no table you will be shown.

We will use a symmetric metric anyway, and say so out loud. The label is free: the next day’s value is the label of today’s window.

Plot it before you do anything else

Bus falls, rail rises and then falls, and March 2020 is a cliff, not a dip. We model one regime: 2016 to the end of May 2019, 1,247 days.

Zoom in: a seven-day cycle

Working days near the top, weekends at about half, every seven days. The extra trough at the end of May is a public holiday: a day_type column gives it the weekend’s code. A second, slower cycle repeats once a year around a slowly moving level: that is trend, and the weekly cycle is seasonality.

The prediction, and the metric

$$\hat{y}_{t+1} \;=\; f\!\left(y_{t-55},\, y_{t-54},\, \dots,\, y_{t}\right) \qquad \mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_i \left|\hat{y}_i - y_i\right|$$
  • Eight weeks in, the next day out. A whole number of weeks, so every position in the window sees the same weekday each time
  • MAE, in riders: the unit the operations team uses, and a handful of holidays cannot dominate it as they would a squared error
The trap in MAPE A percentage error divides by the truth. On a holiday ridership collapses, so the same miss counts several times over — exactly where every model is weakest. Use it as a second opinion, never as the objective.

What nothing scores: the number to beat

Copying the same weekday last week: 55,399 over 1,191 days, 12.0% of a typical day, and 2.4 times better than copying the day before. The seven-day cycle is worth more than recency.

The simplest thing that could work

$$\hat{y}_{t+1} \;=\; b + \sum_{k=0}^{55} w_k\, y_{t-k}$$

Cut the series into overlapping windows, 56 days in and the next day out, and fit fifty-six weights and a bias. With $w_6 = 1$ and every other weight zero it is copy last week: the linear model contains the baseline.

44,925

Its MAE under shuffled five-fold cross-validation, averaged over twenty shuffles: 18.9% better than copying last week on the same rows. Do not write that down yet.

That score came from a shuffled split

Cross-validation estimates the error on new data only if the held-out rows are independent of the training rows, and come from the situation the model will meet. A shuffled series breaks both: a held-out day has its neighbours in training, and half the training days lie in its future.

The upper protocol asks the model to fill a gap. The lower one asks it to forecast. Only one of those is the job.

The split the system will actually face

Fit on everything up to the end of 2018; forecast the 151 days that follow, one at a time, never seeing any of them. Copy last week on the same 151 days, because those days are harder for every forecast:

The margin over doing nothing is 12.9%, not 18.9%.

One cut is one sample. A rolling-origin backtest moves the cut forward, refits, and scores the block that follows, several times: a spread you could not get from one cut.

Put the baseline beside every protocol

The margin falls from 18.9% to 14.7% to 12.9% as the protocol becomes honest. A third of the case for the linear model was an artefact of the evaluation. The networks have to beat 12.9%, not 18.9%.

The recurrent cell, written out

$$\mathbf{h}_t \;=\; \phi\!\left(\mathbf{W}_x^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}_t \;+\; \mathbf{W}_h^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{b}\right)$$

one step of a recurrent layer

  • $\mathbf{x}_t$ — the input at step $t$; $\mathbf{h}_t$ — the state after it
  • $\mathbf{W}_x$ and $\mathbf{W}_h$ do not depend on $t$: the same weights at every step
  • Compare Lecture 7: this is $\phi(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + \mathbf{b})$ with one extra term, and that term is the whole idea

The state is the only route by which anything before step $t$ reaches step $t$. Its width is therefore a budget: whatever the network needs to remember has to fit in $\mathbf{h}$.

Training it: backpropagation through time

Unroll the network over the window and it becomes a deep feed-forward network whose layers share their weights. Then differentiate it exactly as Lecture 8 taught — nothing new is needed.

Which is also the problem Unrolling 56 steps gives a 56-layer network, and by the chain rule a gradient descending 56 layers is multiplied by one factor per layer, 56 times over. Here the factor is the same matrix every step, because the weights are shared — so the gradient is governed by a power of one matrix, and powers of a matrix either vanish or explode unless its spectral radius sits almost exactly at one.

Scaling the initial weights so that each factor is about one — the repair for a stack of independent layers — cannot fix this. Something has to change in the cell itself.

Animation

The model, in eleven lines


import torch.nn as nn

class SimpleRnn(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, input_size=1, hidden_size=32, output_size=1):
        super().__init__()
        self.rnn = nn.RNN(input_size, hidden_size, batch_first=True)
        self.head = nn.Linear(hidden_size, output_size)

    def forward(self, X):                      # X: [batch, time, series]
        outputs, last_state = self.rnn(X)
        return self.head(outputs[:, -1])       # only the final step

batch_first=True because our batches are [batch, time, series]. Without it the module silently reads the first dimension as time.

Training it


model = SimpleRnn()

        loss_fn(model(Xb), yb).backward()

Why the Huber loss

$$L_\delta(e) \;=\; \begin{cases} \tfrac{1}{2}e^{2} & |e| \le \delta\\[2pt] \delta\left(|e| - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta\right) & |e| > \delta \end{cases}$$
LossBehaviour
MSEsmooth everywhere; a handful of shock days dominate the gradient
MAErobust; its gradient is constant, so it converges slowly near the optimum
Huberquadratic near zero, linear beyond — both properties, one knob

We optimise a proxy for MAE. The metric is what the operator reads, the loss is what the optimiser can descend, and they are allowed to differ.

The recurrent network, on the same shuffled folds

The simple RNN scores 38,224 on five shuffled folds, seed 42: 31.0% better than copying last week on the same folds (55,385) and 15% better than the linear model (44,925).

These folds are shuffled, so none of these numbers is a forecast. Every forward number in this lecture is scored on the same 151 days.

Windows overlap, and that matters

Consecutive training windows share almost all their content: a 56-day window starting on day $t$ and one starting on day $t+1$ differ in two values out of fifty-six.

Two consequences Shuffle them. Otherwise a mini-batch is near-identical examples, and averaging near-copies cancels almost none of one example’s noise: the step is noisier, and training stalls.

Do not let that shuffling cross the split. Windows are shuffled within the training period only; the evaluation period is still strictly later in time. Shuffling and leakage are different operations and it is easy to conflate them.

Normalising a series

  • Scale by a constant computed on the training period only — the same rule as every other lecture, and easier to break here because the series looks like one object
  • Divide rather than standardise if the series is positive and multiplicative: ridership is counts, and a ratio is more natural than a difference
  • If you modelled the day-to-day change rather than the level, turn the prediction back into a level before reporting it. The metric is in the units the operator uses
Failure condition — a model scored on day-to-day changes A model evaluated on day-to-day changes can look excellent and mean nothing: predicting no change at all scores well in those units. The units the metric was computed in decide what it is able to praise, and the report is usually silent about which they were.

What to log while it trains

  • Training and validation loss per epoch — the gap is Lecture 5's
  • The gradient norm — along time here rather than depth. A plain RNN on a long window shows it decaying
  • The forecast itself, plotted against truth, on a held-out stretch. A number cannot tell you the model has learned to output last week’s value with a one-day lag; a picture can
The failure a plot catches and a metric does not A model that simply repeats the previous value scores well on almost any error metric and is worthless. On a plot it is unmistakable: the prediction is the truth, shifted right by one step.

Forecasting $k$ steps ahead: three strategies

StrategyHowWeakness
Recursivepredict one step, feed it back, repeaterrors compound; the model never trained on its own output
Directa separate model per horizon$k$ models, and none of them shares what the others learned
Sequence-to-sequenceone model, $k$ outputs at oncemore to fit, and the outputs are not independent

All three are defensible and all three must be evaluated the same way: forward in time, at the horizon you actually need. A model that is excellent one step ahead and useless seven steps ahead is not a good model if the operator plans a week in advance.

Teacher forcing, and what it costs

Training a sequence-to-sequence model, you can feed the decoder the true previous token or its own previous prediction.

TrainsAt prediction time
True token (teacher forcing)fast and stablethe model has never seen its own mistakes
Own predictionslow, and early training is noisematches deployment
Exposure bias A model trained only on true prefixes meets a distribution at prediction time that it never saw in training: its own errors, compounding. The forecast drifts, and the further ahead you go the worse it gets — which is precisely the recursive strategy’s weakness on the previous slide.

Wait — that is a shift(-1)

The shuffled split at the start let a model train on the rows surrounding what it was scored on. This one reads the future deliberately.

The test that separates them Is the value available at prediction time? The type of the day we are forecasting is on the public calendar and has been for a year. Its ridership is not.

This is the one question about leakage that no static check can answer for you. It is not a property of the code; it is a property of the world.

Improvement 3 · Forecasting further than one day

The operations team also wants a fortnight. Two ways:

Feed the forecast back

Predict day $t{+}1$, append it to the window as though it had happened, predict $t{+}2$. Errors accumulate.

Predict all fourteen at once

Change the output layer to fourteen units and the target to a vector. One model, no accumulation.


target = self.series[end : end + 14, 0]     # 14 values, not 1
model = GruModel(input_size=5, hidden_size=32, output_size=14)

And a third way — predict fourteen at every step

At step 0 the model outputs the forecasts for steps 1 to 14; at step 1, for steps 2 to 15; and so on. The target is a sequence of windows rather than a single vector.


def forward(self, X):
    outputs, _state = self.rnn(X)
    return self.head(outputs)        # every step, not outputs[:, -1]
Is that cheating? No. A recurrent cell at step $t$ has seen steps $1$ to $t$ and nothing more — it is causal. The loss simply gains a term at every step, so many more gradients flow and they travel a shorter distance.

After training, only the last step’s output is used.

How far ahead can it see?

The error grows with distance, from 35,997 at one day to 46,586 at fourteen — and stays below the no-model baseline throughout.

Why a gate helps, in one line

A plain cell multiplies the state at every step:

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{t-1}} \;=\; \text{diag}(\phi')\,\mathbf{W}_h^{\mathsf T}$$

so over $T$ steps the gradient carries $T$ copies of the same matrix: a deep stack’s product of factors, with the extra force that it is one matrix rather than many independent ones.

What a gated cell does instead An LSTM keeps a separate cell state $\mathbf{c}_t$ updated additively: $\mathbf{c}_t = \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t$. When the forget gate $\mathbf{f}_t$ is near one, $\partial\mathbf{c}_t/\partial\mathbf{c}_{t-1}\approx\mathbf{1}$ and the gradient passes through almost unchanged. That is the entire trick: a path along which the gradient is added to rather than multiplied.

LSTM, gate by gate

GateEquationWhat it decides
forget $\mathbf{f}_t$$\sigma(\cdot)$how much of the old cell state to keep
input $\mathbf{i}_t$$\sigma(\cdot)$how much of the candidate to write
candidate $\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t$$\tanh(\cdot)$what to write, if anything
output $\mathbf{o}_t$$\sigma(\cdot)$how much of the cell state to expose as $\mathbf{h}_t$

Every gate is a sigmoid of the same two inputs $(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{h}_{t-1})$ with its own weights, so a sigmoid in $[0,1]$ is a soft switch and the network learns when to open it. Four gates means roughly four times the parameters of a plain cell.

Animation

GRU: the same idea, two gates

  • Update gate $\mathbf{z}_t$ — merges LSTM’s forget and input into one: what is kept is what is not written
  • Reset gate $\mathbf{r}_t$ — how much of the previous state to use when forming the candidate
  • No separate cell state: $\mathbf{h}_t$ does both jobs
$$\mathbf{h}_t = (1-\mathbf{z}_t)\odot\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{z}_t\odot\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t$$

Still additive, still a near-identity path when $\mathbf{z}_t$ is small — the property that mattered, kept, with about three quarters of the parameters.

Which to use On most problems they are within noise of one another. GRU is smaller and trains faster; LSTM has the longer track record. Measure, on your own protocol, rather than inheriting an opinion.

Is 34,639 good?

It is 47% better than the system a transit authority could run with no data scientist at all, and its typical miss is around 10.4% of the day it is forecasting — against 16.5% for copying last week on those same 151 days. A naive percentage measured over any other set of days is not the comparison to make here. (As a share of a mean day on those same 151 days — 583,030 riders — the miss is 5.9%, which is a third quantity again.)

What a defensible report says “Forecasting the five months after the training cut, and never seeing them, the model’s mean absolute error is 34,639 boardings a day against 64,815 for copying the same day of the previous week. Both figures are on identical rows. The gain is concentrated on holidays.”

Note what that sentence contains: the protocol, the baseline, the rows, and where the gain comes from. Four things the number alone does not carry.

Failure condition — a regime the training window never contained Scored on the four months from March 2020, the model is 2.3 times worse than doing nothing and its error is six tenths of the level of the series. Nothing in training could have shown this: the failure is that the future stopped resembling the window the model was fitted on.

To be fair to the model

March 2020 is not a leak and it is not the model’s fault. No forecaster could have produced it from eight weeks of history, and copying last week wins only because it adapts within a week of the collapse.

The lesson is not “the model is bad”. It is that an honest protocol told you the model had failed, on the day it failed. A shuffled split, with those same days spread across its training folds, could not have.

Which is why a deployed forecaster is monitored against its own naive baseline, continuously. If the margin disappears, something has changed in the world.

Temporal leakage checklist — keep this

  • Is the split by date, not by row?
  • Does every feature exist at the moment the forecast is made? Name the moment
  • Were any statistics — means, scalers, encodings — computed over the whole series before the cut?
  • Do the training windows overlap the scored windows? Purge them
  • Is the baseline computed on identical rows to the model?
  • Does the reported score come from more than one origin?

Six questions. The third is the oldest leak in the course — a statistic fitted before the split — and the fourth is the one only a series of overlapping windows has.

A recipe for a forecasting model

  1. Plot the series. Seasonality and trend are usually visible, and cost nothing to find
  2. Compute the naive baselines: the day before, and the same day last week. These are the numbers to beat
  3. Fix the protocol before fitting anything: horizon, rolling origin, number of folds, metric
  4. Fit a linear model on lags. It is fast, and it is often close
  5. Only then a recurrent model, scored on the same protocol
  6. Report the ladder with its spread, not the best row alone

Steps 2 and 3 come before step 4 for one reason: after you have a number, you will not choose the protocol that makes it look worse.

Design exercise · in the manner of an ML system-design interview

The task: an energy retailer’s next-day demand forecast, which decides what it buys

The brief An energy retailer sells electricity to households and buys what it delivers on the wholesale market the day before. The purchasing desk’s order is what the model drives: for each hour of the next day, a forecast of its customers’ demand, in the market’s units. Buy short, and the gap costs the balancing price; buy long, the surplus is sold at a loss. Finance reads the imbalance bill against margin.
  • Given: years of hourly meter readings summed over the customers; archived weather forecasts; holidays
  • Scale: tens of thousands of hourly rows per tariff group, a few groups; one order a day
  • Consumers of the output: the purchasing desk, the risk team, and finance, which pays the bill

Nine steps follow, each with the pitfall that catches a first attempt and what to do about it. The model at the centre is today’s: a gated recurrent network on windows of the series.

Step 1 — Frame it: the horizon, the metric, and what an error costs

  • The horizon is the market’s: every hour of the next day, issued before the auction closes — a day ahead, not one step ahead
  • The metric is the mean absolute error in the units the desk buys in, read beside the no-model forecasts on identical hours; the training loss may be Huber, the metric is not
  • What an error costs is not symmetric — the balancing price on a shortfall, a loss on the surplus — and that is finance’s question, asked before the loss is chosen
Pitfall The first number reported is the one-step-ahead error, because that is what the training loop produces, and it looks splendid: the next hour is nearly the last hour. The desk buys a day ahead. Fix the protocol before fitting anything — horizon, rolling origin, folds, metric — and score every model at the horizon the order needs.

Step 2 — The numbers to beat, before any network

  • Plot the series: a daily cycle, a weekly one, an annual one, and holidays that look like weekends
  • Compute the naive forecasts: the same hour the day before, the same hour the week before. Demand repeats, so copying last week is hard to beat — it is what the desk orders on now
  • Then a linear model on lagged hours. It contains the seasonal copy as one weight of one, beats it modestly and cheaply
Pitfall Reporting the recurrent network against the mean of the series, or against nothing. The seasonal copy is the bar, on the same hours as the model; a network that beats it by less than the fold-to-fold spread has not beaten it. And the baselines come first for a reason: once you have a number, you will not choose the protocol that makes it look worse.

Step 3 — Split by date, window inside the split, scale on the training period

  • Training years up to a cut, validation after it, test after that; several origins rolled forward, so the score is a spread
  • Each example is a window of recent hours with the next day’s hours as target; consecutive windows share almost everything, so shuffle them — within the training period only
  • Divide the series by a constant fitted on the training period: demand is positive and multiplicative, so a ratio is more natural; report the error in the market’s units
Pitfall — the oldest leak, and the one only windows have The scaler fitted on the whole series, because the series looks like one object — and the windows whose target hours lie past the cut, which put the scored days into training. The release promises a margin the desk never sees. Fit every statistic before the cut, and purge every window that straddles it.

Step 4 — Inputs: the history, and what is known in advance

  • The window of recent demand, one series per tariff group, as the recurrent input
  • What the calendar already says about the day being forecast — its type, holiday or not — as extra columns: the legitimate look ahead, because it is public a year early
  • The weather forecast for that day — temperature drives heating and cooling — as the forecast issued that afternoon, from the archive, not the weather later observed
Pitfall Training on the observed temperature of the forecast day. The join is one line, and the model learns to read a number the desk will never have when it orders: the offline error falls, the live error does not, and finance pays the difference. The test is not in the code — is the value available at the moment the order is placed? — and the fix is to train on the archived forecasts, errors included.

Step 5 — The model: a gated cell, and every hour of the next day at once

  • A GRU over the window, with demand, day type and forecast temperature at each step; a linear head with one output per hour of the next day — sequence to vector, the whole horizon at once
  • Huber loss, so a few shock days do not own the gradient; the metric stays the absolute error
  • The same training loop as every network in this course, a few thousand parameters, and the recipe chosen on the validation stretch
Pitfall — exposure bias Forecasting the day recursively: predict one hour, append it as though it had happened, predict the next. The model was trained only on true history, meets its own errors for the first time in service, and they compound across the day — the evening peak is forecast from a morning the model invented. Predict the whole day at once, or a day at every step with a loss at every step — and evaluate at the horizon either way.

Step 6 — What breaks along time: the gradient, and what to do about it

  • A window of a week of hourly steps unrolls into a network a few hundred layers deep with one matrix shared by all of them: the gradient carries a power of that matrix, and vanishes or explodes
  • The gated cell is the structural answer — an additive path along which the gradient is added to rather than multiplied
  • Log the gradient norm each epoch, beside both losses: a plain cell on a long window shows it decaying; a loss that spikes shows it exploding
Pitfall A training loss that sits flat for ten epochs, or jumps to a number with too many digits and stays, read as “needs more epochs” or “needs a smaller network” — as the release slips. Read the norm first. Clip the gradient — rescale it whenever its norm passes a threshold chosen from its distribution — a clip below the median turns the optimiser into something else — and if a plain cell still decays, change the cell, not the learning rate.

Step 7 — Read the result honestly: seeds, origins, and a picture

  • Every model on the same forward days, from several origins, under several seeds: report the ladder with its spread, never the best row
  • The gap between training and forecast error is overfitting, and it is what to attack before a bigger cell
  • Plot a held-out week, forecast against truth. A model that outputs the previous day shifted by an hour scores well on almost any metric and is worthless; on the plot it is unmistakable
Pitfall A deeper network beats the shallow one on the first run, and that goes in the release note. Change only the seed and the order flips; the gap at any one seed is smaller than either model’s swing across seeds. A ranking that rests on one run is not a finding — report it as unresolved, and spend the compute on seeds before depth.

Step 8 — In production: shadow first, then the day the world changes

  • Shadow first: scored beside the desk’s old method on identical hours, and only then writing the order. Truth arrives with the next day’s meter data, so the margin over the seasonal copy is a running number
  • Retrain on a rolling window at a fixed cadence; the data team is on call for the days the margin vanishes
  • A price rise that sends customers to a rival, a tariff that moves use to cheap hours, rooftop solar, a lockdown: none is in the window
Pitfall The model keeps forecasting the old customer base and is worse than copying last week within a week of the change — the copy adapts, the model does not, and finance reads it in the imbalance bill. Monitor the margin over the naive forecast continuously; when it vanishes, fall back to the copy, page the person on call, and refit on the regime that has begun.

Solutions — Lecture 15

1. A crop-disease classifier is fine-tuned on a few field photos per disease, with random hue shifts among its…

Yellow against green is the symptom: shifting the hue can turn a chlorotic leaf into a healthy-looking one and keep the “diseased” label. An augmentation is a claim that the label does not change; make it only where an agronomist would agree.

  • Flips, rotations, crops and mild brightness changes keep the label; a change to the colour that defines the class does not
  • Augmentation belongs on the training loader only

2. A network trained and tested on a large public set of leaves photographed one at a time on a lab table…

The test set shares the lab’s background, light and sessions, so a shortcut the network learned from them scores as well as the symptom would. Test on field photos split by farm and season; paste leaves onto other backgrounds; check what the network attends to.

  • A network takes whatever separates the classes most cheaply
  • A test set that shares a shortcut cannot detect it

Solutions — Lecture 15 (2)

3. Solution A finds the leaf by thresholding green pixels, then measures colour, spots and texture on that mask.…

The segmentation: other leaves, soil and dead brown tissue fall on the wrong side of the threshold. Every feature is measured on the mask, so the classifier receives wrong numbers with no sign they are wrong. Fix the capture (one leaf against a plain background, a quality check) or find the leaf with a learned detector.

  • The user interface is part of the feature engineering
  • Solution B keeps this step, done by a detector instead of a threshold

4. Two cooperatives want the same diagnosis app. One photographs leaves at a fixed station with the same light…

The station: hand-made features. Segmentation is reliable under fixed capture, and few labels favour few numbers to fit. The field: a pretrained network fine-tuned on the field photos, which learns cues nobody named and copes with clutter it was trained on.

  • The choice depends on how controlled the capture is and how many field labels exist, not on which method is newer
  • In both, the other method is the baseline to beat

Solutions — Lecture 15 (3)

5. The diagnosis app ends in a softmax over the diseases of the selected crop. Say why it must be able to…

A softmax always names a disease, even for a blurred photo or a fruit, and a confident wrong diagnosis sends a farmer to spray the wrong product. Below a threshold the photo goes to an agronomist; the threshold is chosen with the agronomists from accuracy against coverage on field photos.

  • Abstention is part of the output, not a failure of it
  • The probabilities must be calibrated before a threshold on them means anything
