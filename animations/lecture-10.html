Regularising deep networks

Two widgets for today's thread. The first samples thinned networks one mask at a time and watches their average find the full network. The second fits a polynomial that has more coefficients than points, and shows what weight decay and early stopping each do to it.

All data on this page is synthetic, generated in your browser from a fixed seed, so Reset always gives back the same picture.

  • 1 · Dropout: one thinned network per step, averaged
  • 2 · Weight decay and early stopping on a fit that overfits

1Dropout: one thinned network per step, averaged

One hidden layer of eight ReLU units with fixed random weights, and one fixed input. Each step draws a mask that keeps every unit with probability $1 - p$, computes the thinned network's output $\tilde z$, and adds it to a running average. On the left, the network: a kept unit is blue, a dropped one is hollow. On the right, every $\tilde z$ as a dot and the running average as a line, against the full network's $z$, the dashed line. Untick the rescaling and watch where the average goes instead.

The mathematics

The hidden activations $a_j = \operatorname{ReLU}(\mathbf w_j^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x)$ are fixed, because the input and the weights are. The full network outputs $z = \sum_j v_j a_j$. A mask draws $m_j \sim \text{Bernoulli}(1-p)$ independently for each unit, so $\mathbb E[m_j] = 1-p$ and $\operatorname{Var}(m_j) = p(1-p)$. The thinned network outputs $\tilde z = \sum_j m_j v_j a_j / (1-p)$, and by linearity of expectation

$$\mathbb E[\tilde z] = \sum_j \frac{\mathbb E[m_j]}{1-p}\, v_j a_j = z .$$

Without the division the expectation is $(1-p)\,z$. For the spread, independent terms add their variances, so $\operatorname{Var}(\tilde z) = \sum_j \frac{p(1-p)}{(1-p)^2} v_j^2 a_j^2 = \frac{p}{1-p}\sum_j v_j^2 a_j^2$, which is what the readout's predicted standard deviation is the square root of. With eight units there are $2^8 = 256$ thinned networks; each step trains one of them, and the network run without masks at test time computes their average.

Watch for
  • Single dots scatter widely — a thinned network with half its units gone is a different function — but the running average settles on the dashed line within a few dozen steps, and the measured standard deviation of $\tilde z$ approaches the predicted one.
  • Raise $p$ and the dots spread further: the spread grows like $\sqrt{p/(1-p)}$, so $p = 0.8$ is four times as wide as $p = 0.2$. The average still finds $z$, more slowly.
  • With the rescaling off, the average settles at $(1-p)\,z$, the dotted line: a network trained this way and run without masks would see activations $1/(1-p)$ times too large.
Try this
  • Set $p = 0.5$, press Play, and count how many distinct masks the readout has seen by step 100. Out of 256, most have been drawn once or not at all: the average is over networks that were mostly never trained.
  • Untick rescale by 1/(1 − p) at $p = 0.5$: the average halves. Tick it again and it recovers. That tick is the only difference between dropout as first published and inverted dropout, the one nn.Dropout implements.
  • Set $p = 0.1$ and read the predicted spread; then $p = 0.9$. The second is nine times the first, a factor of eighty-one on the second moment — $p/(1-p)$ went from a ninth to nine — and on a deep stack that factor compounds once per dropout layer.

2Weight decay and early stopping on a fit that overfits

Twelve training points from a smooth curve plus noise, and thirty validation points from the same curve. The model is a polynomial of degree twelve — thirteen coefficients for twelve points — fitted by gradient descent from zero. Each step is five gradient iterations with the update $\boldsymbol\theta \leftarrow (1-\eta\lambda)\,\boldsymbol\theta - \eta\nabla L$. On the left, the points, the fit and the true curve. On the right, the training and validation errors against the step, with the best validation step marked. The readout keeps the best step and its error: that pair is early stopping.

The mathematics

The features are Chebyshev polynomials $T_0(x), \dots, T_{12}(x)$ on $[-1, 1]$, with $T_0 = 1$, $T_1 = x$ and $T_{k+1} = 2xT_k - T_{k-1}$, so every feature lies in $[-1, 1]$ and gradient descent is stable at one learning rate. The loss is the mean squared error $L(\boldsymbol\theta) = \tfrac1n\sum_i (\boldsymbol\theta^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\phi(x_i) - y_i)^2$, whose gradient is $\tfrac2n\boldsymbol\Phi^{\mathsf T}(\boldsymbol\Phi\boldsymbol\theta - \mathbf y)$. Adding $\tfrac\lambda2\lVert\boldsymbol\theta\rVert^2$ adds $\lambda\boldsymbol\theta$ to the gradient, and one step becomes

$$\boldsymbol\theta \leftarrow \boldsymbol\theta - \eta(\nabla L + \lambda\boldsymbol\theta) = (1 - \eta\lambda)\,\boldsymbol\theta - \eta\nabla L :$$

shrink every coefficient by the factor $1-\eta\lambda$, then take the usual step. With $\eta = 0.05$ and $\lambda = 0.05$ the factor is $0.9975$ per iteration; over the five iterations of one step it is $0.9875$. The validation error is the same mean squared error on points the fit never sees, and early stopping keeps the coefficients from the step where it was lowest.

Watch for
  • With $\lambda = 0$ both errors fall together at first; then the validation error turns and climbs, several times over by the end, while the training error keeps falling. The fit on the left starts to wiggle between the points: thirteen coefficients can pass through twelve points.
  • The best validation step is early. From then on every iteration makes the training error better and the validation error worse, and the gold marker on the right stays where it is.
  • With $\lambda = 0.05$ the coefficient norm in the readout levels off instead of doubling, the wiggles stay small, and the validation error climbs by a fraction of its minimum instead of several times over.
Try this
  • Run to the end with $\lambda = 0$ and note the final validation error and the best step's. The gap between them is what shipping the last weights instead of the best ones costs.
  • Set $\lambda = 0.2$. The fit is now too stiff: the training error stays high and the validation error, though it never climbs, never gets as low as the best early-stopped value either. Too much shrinkage is a constraint the data cannot satisfy.
  • Set $\lambda = 0.05$ and compare its validation error at the end with the $\lambda = 0$ run's at the end, and with that run's best step. Decay holds the fit back; early stopping stops it. Neither beats the other here, and together they are cheaper than either alone: with decay on, the best step hardly matters.
