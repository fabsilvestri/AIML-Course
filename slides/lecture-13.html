Colour is three numbers, not one

Each pixel carries red, green and blue. So one image is

$$32 \times 32 \times 3 \;=\; 3072 \ \text{numbers}$$

We will flatten that into a single vector, which throws away the fact that neighbouring pixels are neighbours. That is a real loss and it is deliberate: today's subject is depth, not vision. Lecture 15 stops throwing it away, and the accuracy you get today is the number that makes the case for doing so.

Which metric, and why you are allowed it

Lecture 4 spent ninety minutes on why accuracy is usually a bad idea. Three conditions make it defensible, and here all three hold:

ConditionHere
The classes are balanced1,000 test images each, exactly
No error is more expensive than anotheran editor fixes any wrong tag in one click
You want one number for all classesthe index is judged as a whole

Say the conditions out loud when you choose a metric. A metric chosen without stating them is a metric you cannot defend when one of them stops holding.

The number to beat, before anything is built

Predict the commonest class for every image. With 10 equal classes there is no commonest class, so any fixed answer gives the same thing:

10.0%

And the corresponding loss, for a model whose ten probabilities are all one tenth:

$$-\log \tfrac{1}{10} \;=\; \ln 10 \;=\; 2.3026$$

Write both down. You will compare every loss curve today against that second number, and it is the one that will tell you something the accuracy does not.

The other anchor

A baseline tells you what nothing scores. It does not tell you what good is. For this dataset, published results run from the seventies for a dense network on flattened pixels to the high nineties for a large convolutional one.

Scope Those upper numbers come from architectures in Chapter 12 and beyond. They are here so your commitment is anchored at both ends, not because we are going to reach them today.

So the interval you are estimating inside runs from 10% to somewhere in the nineties, and the honest answer for a first attempt is much nearer the bottom than most rooms guess.

Part three

Build

60 minutes · the simplest thing that runs

The specification

What we are asking for A fully connected classifier: 3,072 inputs, 20 hidden layers of 100 units, a logistic activation on each, and a linear head of 10 outputs. Cross-entropy loss, Adam at 0.001, batches of 128, 20 epochs.

Read that again and note what it does not say: nothing about how the weights start out. That is not something we are hiding — it is the ordinary case. Almost no specification mentions it.

The stack

def make_net(depth=20, width=100, n_in=3072, n_out=10):
    layers, prev = [], n_in
    for _ in range(depth):
        layers += [nn.Linear(prev, width), nn.Sigmoid()]
        prev = width
    layers.append(nn.Linear(prev, n_out))
    return nn.Sequential(*layers)

net = make_net()

Nine lines. It is the same nn.Sequential from the previous lecture with the middle repeated 20 times.

Reviewer question 5, from Lecture 2 What is the default I did not ask for?

Keep the number 0.05789 somewhere. Nothing on this slide is a complaint — the default is a perfectly reasonable one, and for a three-layer network it is fine. We are simply writing down a quantity nobody had written down.

Resist explaining what is wrong with it. At this point in the lecture it is a fact with no consequence attached, and it should stay that way until the gradient profile is on the screen.

Why 20 layers

Because more layers is supposed to be more expressive. Each layer composes a new nonlinearity on top of the last, and the number of regions a network can carve out of its input space grows with depth faster than with width.

That is the theory. Today we test it.

Nobody deploys a twenty-layer dense network on flattened pixels. It is here because it is the smallest thing that breaks in the way Chapter 11 is about, and because the break is invisible at three layers.

Why the logistic activation

$$\sigma(z) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \;\in\; (0, 1)$$
  • It is the activation the field used for thirty years
  • It is smooth, bounded, and its derivative is $\sigma(1-\sigma)$ — one line to write
  • It is the one the textbook uses to introduce this chapter

It is also, as of the next lecture, a choice you will never make again. But make it once, deliberately, and measure what it does.

What comes back

class DeepClassifier(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, input_dim=3072, hidden_dim=100,
                 num_layers=20, num_classes=10):
        super().__init__()
        layers = []
        for i in range(num_layers):
            layers.append(nn.Linear(input_dim if i == 0 else hidden_dim,
                                    hidden_dim))
            layers.append(nn.Sigmoid())
        layers.append(nn.Linear(hidden_dim, num_classes))
        self.model = nn.Sequential(*layers)

    def forward(self, x):
        return self.model(x)

Part three, continued

Instrument it

the reason you own the loop

What we can do now, and could not before

Backpropagation computes 500,210 partial derivatives on every batch. Under MLPClassifier.fit() every one of them was discarded.

They are all still here, in p.grad, and we can look at any of them.

  • Forward — what does each layer output?
  • Backward — what does each weight matrix receive?

Two probes, about ten lines each. Without them the diagnosis today is “it does not train” and there is no next step.

Probe 1 · the forward pass

@torch.no_grad()
def activation_stats(net, X, n=512):
    net.eval()
    h, rows = X[:n], []
    for m in net:
        h = m(h)
        if isinstance(m, nn.Sigmoid):
            rows.append({"mean": float(h.mean()),
                         "sd":   float(h.std()),
                         "sat":  float(((h - .5).abs() > .45)
                                       .float().mean())})
    return rows

Running the modules by hand instead of calling net(X). That is legal because a Sequential is an iterable of modules, and it is the cheapest possible instrumentation.

Every word of that paragraph is wrong.

The probe measured the wrong thing

h.std() takes the spread over the whole tensor — and that is dominated by the spread of the layer’s random biases across units, which does not change with depth. It would sit near 0.07 in a network that had stopped computing anything at all.

What carries information is the spread down the batch, averaged over units — how much a unit moves when the input does.

Hidden layer1251020
h.std() — what we plotted0.1310.0770.0710.0710.071
h.std(dim=0).mean() — the signal1.3e−011.9e−025.9e−053.0e−092.4e−17

Sixteen orders of magnitude. The forward signal does die — by a factor of 0.149 per layer, which is the number Thread 7 predicts from the fan-in and the same rate the gradient vanishes at. By layer 20 the network is no longer a function of its input.

One flat line and one cliff, from the same activations. A probe that cannot fail is not instrumentation, and this one was promoted to standing instrumentation in Lecture 14 before anyone checked which axis it reduced over.

Gradient norm per weight matrix, at initialisation

Read the axis. Each gridline is a factor of ten, and the line crosses about 15 of them.

The shape of that line is the finding

It is straight on a logarithmic axis.

A straight line on a log axis is a geometric sequence. The attenuation is not one bad layer, or a fault at the input: it is the same factor, applied at every layer.

$$\frac{\lVert g_{\ell}\rVert}{\lVert g_{\ell+1}\rVert} \;\approx\; \rho \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\lVert g_{1}\rVert}{\lVert g_{20}\rVert} \;\approx\; \rho^{19}$$

Which means one number characterises the whole network, and it is a number the next lecture predicts from the shapes of the matrices.

The one number to take away

0.1593

The geometric mean of the ratio between neighbouring layers, over 19 steps.

Which is one part in1.45e+15

Is it a floating-point artefact?

A reasonable suspicion: a norm is a sum of squares, and squaring $10^{-20}$ underflows to exactly zero in single precision. If that were happening, the plot would be an artefact of the arithmetic rather than a fact about the network.

CheckResult
Layers whose float32 gradient is exactly zero0
Largest relative disagreement, float32 against float645.6e-06

So no. The two precisions agree, and the smallest gradient is comfortably inside float32's range. Check this rather than assuming it — on a deeper or narrower stack it would not be true, and the plot would silently become a lie.

Why Adam does not rescue this

Adam divides by a running estimate of the gradient’s own magnitude, so it is tempting to think a small gradient is automatically compensated.

  • It rescales, so the direction is what matters — and at $10^{-17}$ the direction is dominated by batch-to-batch noise
  • The $\varepsilon$ in the denominator, default $10^{-8}$, is larger than the gradients themselves at the bottom of the stack
An optimiser can compensate for a badly scaled gradient. It cannot manufacture information that the backward pass did not deliver.

“Just raise the learning rate”

The obvious reply, and it is worth stating why it cannot work.

The gradient at the head is 4.87e-01 and at layer 1 it is 4.57e-17. Any learning rate large enough to move layer 1 is 1e+15 times too large for the head.

One knob, two demands A single scalar cannot serve a quantity that spans 15 orders of magnitude across the network. The repair has to change the spread, not the scale.

The diagnosis, as far as we can take it

What we measured The gradient is attenuated by a constant factor of about 0.159 at every layer, so by the time it reaches the bottom of a 20-layer stack it has been multiplied by 1.4e+15. The layers nearest the data never learn anything.

What we cannot yet say: why that factor is 0.159 and not 1.

That is a question about the weight matrices themselves, and it has an exact answer.

A checklist for “it does not train”

  1. What is the baseline, and what is the loss of a model that has learned nothing?
  2. Can the loop memorise 200 examples? If not, the bug is the loop
  3. Does a shallower version of the same thing work?
  4. What is the activation statistic at each layer — mean, spread, saturated fraction?
  5. What is the gradient norm at each layer, on a log axis?
  6. Is that profile flat, or is it a straight line?

Six questions, about twenty lines of code, and they distinguish four different failures that all look like a flat loss curve.
